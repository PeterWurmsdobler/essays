A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature

An attempt to demonstrate that the recent changes in CO2 and GMST are unusual in the Earth's history.

The Earth system has been extensively researched over the past decades: ocean currents, vapour cycles, climate models, you name it; humanity has gained a decent understanding of some Earth mechanisms as well as important insights. For instance, the Earth’s global mean surface temperature (GMST) is understood to have risen and fallen considerably over the last 500 million years, as presented in A 485-million-year history of Earth’s surface temperature [1] and recreated in Figure 1. Judd et al. demonstrate that GMST has varied from about 10 °C to 35 °C over the full 485-million-year record and is correlated to a certain extent with the carbon dioxide content in the atmosphere (or atmospheric CO2), with varying causality.

Figure 1. Global mean surface temperature (GMST) over the past 485 million years, recreated from Judd et al.’s own PhanDA reconstruction. Shaded bands denote 16th–84th and 5th–95th percentile uncertainty intervals; the strip above is the same median curve read as a colour, cooler blue to warmer red, a continuous view of the hothouse and icehouse states this history moves through.

It is sometimes said, not surprisingly given Judd’s findings, that there is nothing to worry about with climate change: the planet has seen far higher atmospheric CO2 content and far higher temperatures than today, and got along just fine. Those hothouse and icehouse episodes unfolded over millions of years, however, time enough for the web of life to migrate, adapt and speciate its way through them as life can adjust to gradual change; abrupt change as in recent decades is a different situation and quite a challenge to the web of life that underpins our civilisation. The extent of these recent changes, however, is not visible on a plot of CO2 and GMST levels spanning 500 million years with a linear time scale. Therefore, we need to look at the rates of change and at how the two variables correlate in time; to prove my point, I identified published records that would let me do both:

  • PhanDA: the data assimilation behind Judd et al.’s combined 485-million-year CO2 and GMST reconstruction [1][2];
  • Ice core: the Antarctic ice core, a composite splicing several ice cores onto one CO2 gas chronology [3];
  • EPICA Dome C: a single Antarctic core’s deuterium-based local temperature [4];
  • Temp12k: a multi-method global Holocene temperature reconstruction [5];
  • Mauna Loa: the direct atmospheric CO2 record since 1958 [6];
  • HadCRUT5: the modern instrumental global temperature record [7].

These sources allowed me to create plots of both CO2 and GMST, each with its own rate of change next to it, on one shared axis, Figure 2: reversed logarithmic time axis, running from 500 million years on the left down to 10 years on the right. Value and rate are shown together, rather than as two separate figures, because a logarithmic axis can itself mislead when used for values only: on a logarithmic time axis, an excursion that took millions of years and one that took mere decades can look deceptively alike (at least the recent changes are visible); the rate panels next to the value panels make the actual difference in rate blatantly obvious.

Figure 2. Atmospheric CO2 (top row) and GMST (bottom row) across the full 485-million-year record, each variable’s value on the left and its own rate of change on the right, on one shared logarithmic, reversed time axis running from 500 million years to 10 years before present. Various data records were concatenated, handed off at 800 kyr, 12 kyr and in 1850, about 200 yr ago (thin vertical lines): for millions of years the rate is very flat, for hundreds of thousands of years slightly more active, then for the past twelve thousand years mostly flat again, but during the industrial era increasingly spiky, culminating in the sharp modern rise.

GMST is plotted in absolute terms throughout in the above plot, PhanDA’s convention; the other records, natively anomalies against 1850–1900, are converted to PhanDA’s most recent sample. This leaves a small step where PhanDA meets the ice-core record at 800 kyr, a limit of resolution rather than an error: PhanDA has no sample between 800,000 and about 1.29 million years ago, so its line stops at 1.29 million years, roughly half a million years before the ice-core record begins, leaving a genuine gap in the line rather than a connecting one.

General Observations

Millions of years ago our planet was a very different place: CO2 in the thousands of ppm, GMST ten degrees and more above today’s; since both were oscillating on cycles of millions of years, the rates on the rate panel next to each value panel appear indistinguishable from zero across that whole turbulent prehistoric era: obviously, an oscillation amplitude divided by the corresponding oscillation period (millions of years) yields a very, very small rate indeed. About a couple of million years ago the planet started to cool down and transition into a new phase¹. Within these last 800,000 years the situation changes: both rate panels show a visibly noisy band instead of a flat line. That is genuine, not a mistake: the ice cores can represent centuries-to-millennia glacial-interglacial oscillations due to a small enough sampling period.

For roughly the last twelve thousand years the record has been at its flattest: the stable Holocene climate that agriculture, cities and writing all developed in. Only since 1850 does anything change again in what appears to be a modest rise. The corresponding rate, however, is several orders of magnitude larger than the one before, and that makes all the difference².

This article presents the same conclusion in more quantitative detail across the four nested time windows listed in Table 1, each between sixty and six hundred times shorter than the one before: 485 million years, 800 thousand years, 12 thousand years, and the industrial era since 1850.

While this article focuses on CO2 and GMST values only, two follow-on articles take the same four windows further: The Rate of Change of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature works out the rate of change, the first derivative of CO2 and GMST across the same four windows, and The Correlation Between Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature cross-correlates the two variables in each window to see whether one leads the other in time.

485 Million Years

The longest view available comes from PhanDA, the data assimilation behind Judd et al.’s 485-million-year reconstruction: proxy observations from many archives are combined using a Kalman filter run over geological time with a process model (climate state variable model for spatially discretised globe) and appropriate observation models (projecting global state variables to observations); Judd et al.’s plot is recreated in Figure 3 and shows the result for most of the Phanerozoic. As in the overview figure above, GMST here is plotted in absolute degrees Celsius; the three closer windows below switch instead to the anomaly convention used for the rest of the record, degrees above or below the 1850–1900 average, since there is no physically meaningful preindustrial baseline 485 million years ago.

Figure 3. Phanerozoic climate evolution across 485 million years, mapping GMST against atmospheric CO2 trends. Shaded bands denote 16th–84th and 5th–95th percentile uncertainty intervals. The dashed vertical lines mark the five mass extinctions discussed below: Ordovician-Silurian (445 Ma), Late Devonian (372 Ma), Permian-Triassic (252 Ma), Triassic-Jurassic (201 Ma) and Cretaceous-Paleogene (66 Ma).

Two observations are worth noting. First, the correlation between the two curves is real but rather loose, and the causality runs both ways at different times; see The Correlation Between Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature for more details. Second, this record is resolved by geological stage: the sampling period is over five million years, so the data give one value for each stage, but carry no information about what happened within it (limitation of Nyquist’s sampling theorem: only oscillations with a period longer than twice the sampling period can be represented at all; anything faster than that is invisible to this archive). A flat stretch here is therefore not evidence that nothing moved quickly within it; it only means this archive’s own resolution could not have shown it either way, whether through undersampling or through the Kalman filter’s own process model smoothing it out.

Throughout the reconstructed history of GMST in Judd’s paper the estimated temperature changes per annum were very, very small (perhaps of the order of 1 °C over a million years), small enough for the web of life on Earth to follow and adapt. There were long periods in which state variables changed little, as well as periods of more rapid change (possibly of the kind found in complex system transitions, though these cannot be fully resolved given the Nyquist sampling-period limit); the latter are often correlated with mass extinctions, which is why the five great ones are marked as dashed vertical lines in this figure and in its companion in The Rate of Change of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature. They are, in short:

  • Ordovician-Silurian, around 445 million years ago, some 85 % of marine species, attributed to rapid global cooling and the fall in sea level that came with massive glaciation.
  • Late Devonian, around 372 million years ago, some 75 % of species, attributed to oceanic anoxia and the spread of marine dead zones.
  • Permian-Triassic, the Great Dying, around 252 million years ago, some 96 % of marine life and 70 % of land species, attributed to the Siberian Traps: volcanism on a scale that heated the atmosphere and acidified the oceans.
  • Triassic-Jurassic, around 201 million years ago, some 80 % of species, attributed again to volcanism, this time the eruptions that broke Pangea apart.
  • Cretaceous-Paleogene, around 66 million years ago, some 75 % of species, attributed to the Chicxulub asteroid impact, which blocked the sun and stopped photosynthesis.

800,000 Years

Zooming in by a factor of six hundred gives Figure 4, the 800,000 years covered by the Antarctic ice cores (Table 1): not an arbitrary span, but eight glacial cycles of roughly 100,000 years each, the era sometimes called the “100-kyr world” in the literature. Here the two variables are measured rather than inferred: CO2 trapped in bubbles in the ice, continued from 1958 onwards by direct atmospheric measurement, and temperature from a deuterium ratio, with the modern instrumental record laid over the top.

Figure 4. Atmospheric CO2 and GMST over the past 800,000 years. Top: the Antarctic ice-core composite, continued by Mauna Loa from 1958. Bottom: EPICA Dome C divided by a polar amplification factor of two to give a global-mean equivalent, with the instrumental record in red; both referenced to 1850–1900.

What the figure shows is the familiar sawtooth of the glacial cycles with CO2 oscillating between roughly 180 ppm in the depths of a glacial and 280 ppm in an interglacial, and GMST varying by some 6 °C in near-lockstep. This is the band the Earth system has occupied for the whole of that period; 280 ppm was the ceiling, reached at the top of every interglacial and never exceeded. The red marks at the right-hand edge sit at 427 ppm and 1.47 °C. Both lie well outside the entire 800,000-year envelope, and both were reached in an interval so short that, at this scale, it barely registers: the whole of the industrial era spans a few hundredths of a millimetre on the printed page.

One caveat is needed for the temperature panel: an ice core records the temperature above one spot in Antarctica, not a global mean; a mean is always smoother than any single location within it, since averaging cancels out local weather that a single ice core cannot. Polar temperature changes run at roughly twice the global-mean amplitude on the slow, orbitally-paced glacial cycle (published estimates range from 1.5 to 2, a difference that would move the glacial cycles’ amplitude by about a third), so the ice-core curve is divided by two to match the instrumental record’s scale; extrapolating one Antarctic point to a global value is reasonable at this slow timescale. Faster, higher-frequency variation is a harder question: it could be local weather rather than global climate, and a single ice core has no spatial averaging to filter that out the way a true mean would. So at least part of the apparent high-frequency variability here is plausibly an artefact of measuring one point rather than many, not a faster climate signal. If anything, this makes the modern instrumental spike below, already a true global mean, stand out more, not less.

12,000 Years

Zooming in once more, by a factor of sixty-seven, gives Figure 5 and the Holocene: the interglacial we are in, and the only climate any settled human society has ever known. The temperature here is no longer a single Antarctic core but Temp12k (Table 1), so it needs no amplification correction. For some seven thousand years atmospheric CO2 remains between about 260 ppm and 285 ppm, a band 25 ppm wide, and GMST stays within a few tenths of a degree of its own long-run value. Agriculture, cities, writing, and every civilisation that followed all happened inside that flat stretch.

Figure 5. Atmospheric CO2 and GMST over the past 12,000 years. Top: the Antarctic composite, carried through the Holocene by the high-resolution Law Dome cores, and Mauna Loa. Bottom: the Temp12k multi-method global reconstruction with its 5th–95th percentile band, and the instrumental record. Both panels referenced to 1850–1900.

Whether the middle of it was slightly warmer than the end is still argued over (proxy reconstructions and climate models disagree, known in the literature as the Holocene temperature conundrum), but the disagreement concerns only tenths of a degree in a record that is otherwise remarkably level. And then, at the right-hand edge, both curves turn upwards like the end of a hockey-stick (reminiscent of Tom Murphy’s article Death by Hockey Sticks [8]).

Since 1850

That right-hand edge of the previous 12,000-year plots deserves a window of its own, Figure 6, the years since 1850, zooming in by a further factor of sixty-eight. 1850 is HadCRUT5’s own native start, not a claim that nobody measured temperature earlier: individual station records reach back much further (Central England Temperature to 1659, several European stations to the 1700s), and the ice-core CO2 record used here already extends back to 1800. What is missing before 1850 is coverage broad enough, across both land and ocean, to support a genuine global mean rather than a regional one; HadCRUT5’s own uncertainty band, wide through the nineteenth century in the figure below, is the visible trace of that limit. The underlying records are the same ones the previous figures end on, only now at a close zoom level, including, in the CO2 panel, the Keeling curve [9]: a superposition of a steady increase and the roughly 6 ppm annual oscillation by which some atmospheric CO2 is drawn down each northern summer and refilled each winter as the land vegetation exhales; it is a good illustration of a living and breathing biosphere.

Figure 6. Atmospheric CO2 and GMST since 1850. Each record is drawn as measured, in the lightest weight, with three zero-phase low-pass filtered results over it (five, ten and twenty-year cutoffs, progressively darker and thicker); the grey line is the 280 ppm ceiling of the past 800,000 years. Note the x-axis is the calendar year here, not years before present.

Since we are interested in the recent steady increase, however, each record is drawn both as measured and with a low-pass filter applied to the measured data over the top (at varying cut-off frequencies, equivalent to five, ten and twenty-year periods, progressively darker and thicker), each curve drawn all the way to the last measurement³. Note that atmospheric CO2 is already above the 280 ppm ceiling of the last 800,000 years at the very start of this window, in 1850, and never comes back below it; the curve then bends upwards through the whole of the twentieth century and is still bending. The temperature follows, with the plateau between about 1945 and 1975, and then a rise that has not paused since.
