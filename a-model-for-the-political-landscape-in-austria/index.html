A Model for the Political Landscape in Austria

Political thinking cannot be laid out on a line between left and right; the surface of a sphere with two poles and its meridians serves better.

Since the French Revolution, the political camps in the most diverse states have been laid out linearly on a straight line in a one-dimensional space, between the extremes ‘right’ and ‘left’. To represent the complex political landscape of a state, however, this approach is quite inadequate. For this reason, this essay sets up a multi-dimensional model using the surface of a sphere with two poles and meridians running between them. This makes it possible to model the political ideologies in a state well and, furthermore, to make the actions of political figures understandable.

Introduction

Again and again the labels ‘left’, ‘right’, ‘centre-left’ etc. appear in the media, attached to persons, groups and parties. They are meant to bring a certain order into the political landscape that allows citizens to place the ideologies conveyed. Depending on the prevailing ideology, one or the other directional attribute carries more or less positive associations. In any case, everybody uses this labelling, in whatever context and with whatever intention. In a word, the vocabulary from ‘left’ to ‘right’ is firmly anchored in the minds of all people with even a passing interest in politics.

A critical mind, however, will not fail to notice that the linear arrangement, this threading of political ideologies on a straight line, is inadequate. Thinking in one dimension is surely a crude reduction of political reality. Above all, the unreflected weighting of one extreme or the other as ‘good’ or ‘bad’ is striking. A mapping onto a multi-dimensional model, so to speak, suggests itself.

The Model

The starting point for mapping the political landscape of a state onto a mathematical model is the surface of a sphere.¹ The position of the sphere in space is of no importance, nor is its radius, since it can be normalised to any size. Under these assumptions the surface of the sphere has no distinguished points with special properties. If one were to stand on such a surface, purely in thought, one could understand one’s own standpoint as the ‘centre’.² However, every inhabitant of the surface can claim this for himself as well. So how can the surface of the sphere be given an unambiguous coordinate system in which to order the standpoints?

If one imagines the sphere rotating about a straight line through its true centre, the result is an axis through two opposite points, generally called poles. Whether the sphere really rotates is of no further importance; all that remains is that the axis with its poles allows a polar coordinate system to be set up. Following geodesy, the following elements are defined:

  • two opposite points, the poles, which define an axis;
  • meridians, which connect the poles along the surface by the shortest route;
  • circles of latitude, which define the position relative to the poles and lie in planes normal to the axis.

The position of the circles of latitude relative to at least one of the poles can be fixed unambiguously, while the position of the meridians relative to each other is as yet undefined. This means that no meridian is distinguished from any other.³ This ambiguity is to be kept deliberately in the political model, too.

These are the topological prerequisites for the spherical surface model. Its correspondence to political systems is explained in more detail in what follows.

The Poles

In ordinary language, poles express opposites. This is the case here, too, for the North and South Poles are antipodes, even though it is very cold and inhospitable at both. The poles are named, with their political assignments, according to the associations that ‘south’ and ‘north’ evoke.⁴

The North Pole, the Totalitarian

All people to whom order and discipline are agreeable notions feel at home on this pole. The individual does not have to think much, for law and order determine what the individual does. Freedom is reduced to the possible minimum by a legal system worked out down to the last detail. The system is predictable and manageable. What cannot be, must not be. Full stop. End of story.

The South Pole, the Idealistic

The South Pole is where the idealists live. Whether it is called the Kingdom of Heaven on Earth or the classless society is of no consequence. Everybody wants to be there, but living there is strenuous, for one must behave in an ideally social manner. That can be very hard work, as it demands a great deal of self-discipline and severity towards oneself, renunciation and surely other virtues that are rather unpleasant.

The freedom of the individual results implicitly and adaptively from the points of contact with one’s neighbour.⁵ The restriction comes from far-sighted reason and is not imposed.

The Meridians

As in geography, meridians connect the two poles. On the political sphere, a meridian is determined by a ‘political direction’, which mostly arises historically. Only three are presented here, as examples.

The Christian Meridian

In Austria this meridian is set by the dominance of the Catholics. Here the arc spans from the Christian idealist to the reactionary conservative with a penchant for discipline and order. Many an Austrian probably still feels queasy at the thought of the dominance of the politically northern implementation of this meridian. It is nevertheless undifferentiated when the inhabitants of this meridian are punished with ‘negative’ associations through the automatically assigned attribute ‘right’, even though this meridian, too, has its idealistic roots.

The Socialist Meridian

Starting from Karl Marx’s manifesto, a political meridian of its own was opened, which has taken various forms in history and in the present. It seems odd, though, that this meridian, labelled ‘left’, is at present taken as positive as a matter of course. For if one adds the prefix ‘National’ and moves the way of thinking further into the political north, a historical phenomenon becomes more understandable as well. Nor are contemporary politicians of this meridian immune to moving north.

The Liberal Meridian

A liberal meridian is somewhat harder to pin down. Considering the development of a corresponding party, however, one sees that along this meridian a split has taken place between an authoritarian camp towards the north and an idealistic, socially liberal camp. The struggle between the latter and an economically oriented camp, which likewise converges towards the north, will nevertheless remain.

The Circles of Latitude

Circles of latitude connect points at the same height, i.e. they represent a connecting plane between different meridians with the same social character or idealistic orientation. It is therefore only natural that communication is possible within a circle of latitude, across the meridians.

The Equator

In the balanced surroundings midway between the extremes of the poles, the distance between the meridians is, on the one hand, at its greatest. On the other hand, the moderate climate of the equator again makes for broad possibilities of understanding, since here many a standpoint is softened somewhat by plenty of the water of pragmatism.

Northern Circles of Latitude

The northern hemisphere shares a tendency towards the authoritarian North Pole, which is capable of creating an identity across all meridians. But a cold wind blows from the north.

Southern Circles of Latitude

In contrast to the northern hemisphere, these dreamers tend towards the south. And it is precisely this tendency that is fertile ground for a willingness to communicate across the boundaries of the meridians.

Conclusion

The aim of this short treatise was to create, with a multi-dimensional model of the political landscape, a basis for a better understanding of the political camps and their relations to one another. A spherical surface model has shown that political thinking cannot be laid out on a straight line between ‘left’ and ‘right’. Rather, when one speaks of this divide, one means a south–north thinking, a thinking that extends between two poles along different meridians, or planes.
