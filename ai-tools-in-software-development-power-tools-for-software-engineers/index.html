AI Tools in Software Development --- Power Tools for Software Engineers

What power tools are for the building trade (or DIYers) AI tools are for software engineers: increase productivity:

My previous posts, Productivity in Software Development using AI models — A Pilot Project and Productivity Increase using AI models — Creating a Home Energy Model demonstrated how Microsoft Copilot [1] in agent mode was employed for vibe-coding a JavaScript MuseScore plugin and building a Home Energy Model, respectively. The objective of this essay is to distil some lessons learnt from both private and professional use of AI-tools; they turn out to be to software development what a collection of power tools is to the building trade (and DIYers alike); used appropriately they make work much more enjoyable and productive.

My AI-tools Journey

The use of earlier ChatGPT versions was considered a bit of an amusement, at home and at work, through prompts such as ‘could you produce a recipe for a banana cake in the manner of speech of the current president of the USA’, or similar but not very serious use cases. In addition, the tendency for hallucinations kept these models in the toy-box for me until one day my 16-year old son asked me whether I could help him with an issue in the Docker compose [2] yaml configuration for a Debian [3]/Plex [4] based media server he had been trying to build. How did he know all that, Debian, Docker compose, yaml, Plex, given his computer science knowledge at that time?

Well, being focused on his objective, i.e. building a media server, my son used ChatGPT to coble together a Debian server and configure Plex [4], which got him nearly 99% there; only some fine-tuning was needed by reading a bit of documentation. My son’s experience made me aware that ChatGPT has since evolved to be more than a toy. Since my employer made Microsoft Copilot [1] available some time later, I thought I would try it out, privately for the afore-mentioned projects as well as professionally at work.

Professional Exposure

My current employer Five.ai is an early adopter of modern AI-tools and its parent company, Robert Bosch GmbH, is investing heavily in AI with a clear ‘AI first’ mindset. In that vein AI-tools were rolled out in various guises, on github.com, on internal websites and within VSCode as Microsoft Copilot [1] plugin. It is the latter I have been using, from mundane little tasks to rather delicate ones and in different problem areas. To a large degree, if I do understand a problem well and am able to articulate the requirements as an input for Copilot, the cases are:

  • Clear solution in mind: I do know exactly what the solution looks like but at the same time I am aware that the implementation will be tedious, involves legwork and boiler plate code; of course, one could argue, if some boiler plate code is need, perhaps there is an issue in code automation or in the programming language itself. Copilot’s output is usually rather impressive on these tasks.
  • Fuzzy solution in mind: I have an inkling on how the problem could be solved, but it would take me a while to figure out all the details; traditionally I would Google or go on stack-overflow asking for aspects of the solution, and of course I would read documentation for libraries and tools. Given a decent prompt with some guidelines, however, Copilot will produce a decent solution much faster.
  • No solution in mind: I have no idea how and what, but as long as the problem can be articulated, it is highly likely that someone, somewhere has found a solution to a the same or at least a similar problem. It is impossible to describe the problem in a single Google search string, but as prompt to Copilot, its model will produce a solution, beyond the possibilities I am aware of (obviously, it had ingested a bit more).

Without going into the details, I have been using Copilot in agent mode for problems such as:

  • Scour through a new or large code base to explain in a few words, sentences or paragraphs what the key concepts are (rather than read all the source code and trying to infer that myself); it is just much faster.
  • Discover duplication in the code base, or in parts of it; ask to analyse code and put one implementation in a common place, then change all references and make sure all tests are adjusted and pass.
  • For an existing program with a command line interface and known arguments, add another argument describing its function, as well as tests for the newly added argument.
  • In a poorly tested component, come up with test cases and propose tests for common but also edge and corner cases.
  • In a broken development environment, try to figure out why certain errors occur and fix it at the source.

Over the past months, not writing a single line of code has nearly become some kind of sport: simply ask Copilot for everything, even simple changes. This way of working feels more like orchestrating a group of agents writing software on my behalf, more productive but more taxing too at times.

Lessons Learnt

Copilot, or rather its AI models have been trained on a large code base; they are a mathematical and statistical representation of a large body of knowledge, far beyond what a single human could ever acquire; I may knot know the solution to a problem, but they embody everything known in the trade thus far. Therefore, working with these models offers an enormous learning potential as they push you beyond your current state of knowledge. Quite often, when looking at the proposed solution, I thought to myself: Oh, I was no aware of that feature or approach.

Prompted in the right way, and given the right context, AI models are indeed very powerful tools, like power tools from a DIY shop (or in my shed). As with any builder’s tool, however, it is important to know when to use which tool from a collection of tools, including manual and power tools. For instance, I would cut a small piece of wood with an appropriate hand saw and not a chain-saw. In a similar spirit, I do write some very specific code by hand. Then, it is at times frightening how good AI-based code completion is: given the context it infers what you were about to type anyway, demonstrating how predictable we are after all. If I do decide to use CoPilot and its agents, I try to follow these rules:

  • articulate very well what you need or want, i.e. the problem statement and most importantly the context and its constraints; clear statements are likely to produce a clear solution; clear thinking => clear code.
  • at times Copilots digs itself into a hole or goes around in circles (perhaps due to context window length limitations). So, one needs to detect when that happens, pull the plug and start a new session.
  • Copilot, like all agents are very flattering and confident in their response. It is important to distinguish between good suggestion and rubbish by applying common sense (and experience).

Bottom line, AI tools are the power tools of the software trade, hence increasing productivity. However, it is very important to remain focused on outcome, and to keep a solution concise and simple. The cooperation is complementary: Copilot has the stamina to go through large amounts of data (and code) in no time and recognise patterns (sic!) while a human brings intuition (which AI currently lacks).

Future AI-tools

Maybe the limitations of Large Language Models (LLMs) are just a matter of quantity, complexity, architecture and training sets, in particular comparing current models to the brain:

  • the human brain has about 86 billion neurons and 100 trillion to 1,000 trillion synapses (akin to artificial neural network weights) and, in my case, my brain was trained for quite a few decades on a varied diet; at the same time the brain remains malleable and evolves with an instant feedback process during learning (neuroplasticity);
  • ChagtGPT 4 and Claude 4 use about 1.8 trillion and 50–100 billion weights, respectively and were trained on publicly available data, a vast amount of information beyond the capability of a human; yet the architecture is static, at least for now.

That said, LLMs are still a few orders of magnitude away from the complexity of a human brain and lack an architecture for dynamic adaptation. That said, it might just be a matter of evolution in network architecture and quantity to allow perceived qualities of the human mind to emerge; perhaps even intuition is only a form of hallucination or vice versa. And perhaps we are over-estimating our own abilities?
