AI Tools in Software Development --- Some Considerations on their Impact

Some considerations on the consequences of using AI tools in software development in a wider societal context.

My previous posts relating to AI tools in software development, AI Tools in Software Development — Power Tools for Software Engineers and AI Tools in Software Development — The Next Era for its Growth Rate, were more or less up-beat techno-optimistic pieces on the productivity gain and positively perceived aspects. The objective of this essay is to present some considerations on the consequences of using those tools in software development, but in a wider societal context.

AI-models in software development, or any other industry for that matter such as arts of legal services to name but a few, constitute powerful tools that increase productivity but also threaten the status quo. Our civilisation’s history contains many similar situations, e.g. the mechanisation of weaving looms which was first met with ‘sabotage’, i.e. throwing of wooden boots (sabots) into the automatic looms, the Jacquard machine, as a sign of protest. Should we throw computer science age equivalents of boots at AI-models? Perhaps not, the unavoidable cannot be stopped; rather we need to work with these new means of production, but at the same time consider the impact on a wider social and societal scope. The following points are not exhaustive but the result of my experience using those tools.

Development Cycle

Software construction is only one part of the software development cycle including stages such as requirements capture and specification, system and subsystem design, construction (sic!), integration, verification and validation, all at various levels of the software architecture. Within the construction phase there is development proper (where AI tools are helping me at the moment), but there is also code review, e.g. as part of merge/pull requests (MR/PR), and continuous integration and deployment (CI/CD). If the construction step can be accelerated though AI tools in this development chain, all other links need to be accelerated, too. Otherwise, the development speed will be throttled and determined by everything but the software construction.

CI/CD are already mostly automated; suppose, MR/PR review and approval can be automated, too. As a result, AI tools would not only be helping to construct software, but also in reviewing its own product: AI is marking its homework (as it happens I have tried using copilot review on Github Copilot software by Microsoft Copilot, no comments). If in addition requirements capture is aided by AI tools, too, the entire chain will be able to turn plain English into software code. Furthermore, since AI-tools are quite verbose in the generated code in my experience, a lot of verbose code would be produced, resulting in significant inflation of code bases, nearly uncontrollable proliferation of software. Beware of exponential growth of an autonomous unleashed software construction machinery!

Junior Coders and Learning

In an earlier post, AI Tools in Software Development — Power Tools for Software Engineers, I mentioned that it is currently necessary to monitor AI tools during construction as they tend to be a bit verbose and at times dig themselves into a hole, or completely miss the point, or the solution only appears correct (and is presented with quite some confidence). It is important to recognise this situation; that needs experience. So, if software construction only needs experienced developers using AI-tools, how will newcomers be able to gain that experience? What about young people? Would anybody still hire an inexperienced software developer?

What I have learned so far, as described in the previous post, one has to be very outcome driven; only code that runs and meets a requirement or a set of requirements counts. Therefore, the learning curve and experience is different. It is less about learning first principles of engineering, at least to start with, but more about having a clear understanding of the requirements, then iterate on code using AI tools until the requirements are met. Documentation is read as needed to aid in achieving a goal; learning happens by assimilation. This process is perhaps more akin to natural language learning for children in contrast to learning a language as an adult, or at least in my case, where I first learn the grammar.

The transition to working with a new tool reminds me of the pocket calculator dispute in my youth (the early 1980s). Parents would say: if children are using those machines, they will not learn their times tables properly and children will not be equipped with the ability to reason about numbers. Well, it did not happen: we used the calculator to get quicker to an answer, and gradually — by assimilation — we still acquired a notion and understanding for numbers or orders of magnitudes. One does not preclude the other, it can enable it, but there is no guarantee either.

License and Attribution

An important issue that I haven’t seen solved, not even addressed is the question of licensing and attribution. AI-tools, or to be precise, their underlying large language models were trained on large bodies of data scraped off publicly available sources; each source may have had a licence specifying how the piece of work can be used and how derived work has to be treated. When somebody is using an AI tool to produce a piece of software given a certain specification, the output is generated by some kind of fusion of pertinent solutions, probably from different sources; nevertheless, the result has to be considered as derived work.

In Free and open-source software (FOSS), derived work is obvious and defined by the associated licence specified in the source code, usually in the headers of the source files, be it GPL, LGPL, MIT licence, you name it. So if somebody explicitly uses and modifies some code to produce a derived piece of software, the stipulation might be to only give credit. In some cases, when source code flows one way, money flows the other way. So what about AI generate code? AI is implicitly using other people’s or organisation’s code, or only certain elements, each of which under a different licence. Information flows downstream, from open source through the AI model to an end user, money does not flow upstream, or not all the way, but only to those who trained the model, admittedly with some significant investment and expenditure.

What would be a solution to the problem, or is it even a problem? I think so, like it is in the creative industry, e.g. music world. Suppose one artist comes up with a special ‘lick’ or ‘riff’, say the very recognisable way Nile Rodgers produced a funky sound. When then other artists started to make money by copying that very sound, e.g. ‘Rapper’s Delight’ by the Sugar Hill Gang, understandably a legal dispute ensued which was later settled out of court. To me it sounds fair that if somebody financially exploits the creation of somebody else, the creator should also benefit from that exploitation, with the terms set out in licence agreements. If the derived work does not exploit the used work for financial gain but returns the creation back into the common software pool, that should be fine, too.

So how could some creative traceability be established when using AI-tools (trained on the available software pool) to generate new software? It is certainly a bit tricker; ironically, perhaps AI could be used to recognise patterns, do some pattern matching of the derived work and the sources being used, with some probability assigned to all associations made? Then it should be possible to trace back software to its sources, and if somebody makes some serious money with a commercial piece of software, money can flow upstream to benefit the inventor, unless the source points to some publicly available work.

Alternatively, perhaps some water-marks can be left in source code; they might make it through to the derived work as a mix of water-marks, but still with recognisable source watermarks allowing tracing. Or perhaps even some kind of block-chain mechanism with embedded tokens (like water-marks)? The result would be some kind of genealogy of code, linking code at some point in time to its tributaries upstream, a nested mechanism. Then monetary flows can also be established.

Authoritative Source

When I was young, I was curious to learn about how things work or happen, in history, music or technology; I still am. To do that, I loved studying encyclopedias and my dream was to own a Brockhaus, or Encyclopedia Britannica, peer-reviewed and authoritative sources I could trust. Then came the world-wide-web followed by search engines such as Google which then could be consulted at the drop of a hat if I wanted to know something; now it was up to me to assess whether a source is trustworthy or not, whether it was peer reviewed or not, and who was behind the source (Cui bono principle). Later Wikipedia emerged with at least some review of articles, and now we have AI-tools to give us a response to any question we might have; every response is given with 100% certainty, but no source, and no reasoning behind, at least for now.

What is the importance of that in the context of software development? Well, software code is not so dissimilar to stories being told, facts being presented. Due to the self-re-enforcing character of machine learned models producing output which in the next iteration is fed back into the learning cycle, a ‘consensus’ on incorrect approaches could emerge. If people ask for a solution to a problem, then use the generated code, publish that, and so on, after a while this will become the ‘established’ way of solving this problem or problem class. In the exponential growth of software code across the world, the human input and the part concientally reviewed will diminish exponentially, too, until what is correct or incorrect is a side-product of a perpetual enforcement loop. Perhaps something to watch out for.

Sharing of Productivity Gain

The most obvious impact of AI-tools is their resulting gain in labour productivity; during humanity’s history there were many occasions where a ground-breaking invention or innovation made it possible to produce the same economic output with less labour than before (or more output with the same amount of labour); the automatic looms, the Jacquard machine, is one example, mechanisation in agriculture another. The same is true to a certain degree for labour productivity gain in the software trade when using AI-tools: the same labour input could produce much more output, or for the same output only a fraction of labour input is needed. How have these situations panned out in the past, where are the differences in software and what could happen in the future?

Economics text books certainly lay out established theories and models on how an increase of labour productivity is resolved, at least in a capitalist economy. There, an economic agent (investor or proprietor) invests in improved means of production, which usually entails an increase in labour efficiency: reduction of labour (cost) while maintaining the same output (increase margins and profit); the investment yields a return which flows back to the investor (capital gains). The ‘freed up’ labour will be available on the market and diverted, with some retraining, to other industries or ventures, quite often where the capital gains from before will be re-deployed. So as long as the return on investment is invested again, the economy grows and everybody is happy, prosperity for all.

There are two comments I would like to make here: first, perpetual growth is in contradiction with life on a finite planet with finite resources. Something will have to give at some point unless humanity succeeds in destroying the life supports system beforehand; then all bets are off. If not, one would hope that at some point most material needs of humans are met, and any increase in labour productivity does not need to be compensated with more production, i.e. use the freed-up labour to produce more stuff (consumerism). Instead, in the spirit of John Maynard Keynes from his Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren [1] (1931): ‘in 100 years hence people would only need to work 15 hours per week’, perhaps most of the productivity gain, at least for material products, can be shared, benefiting all people, to more LIFE as opposed to (subsistence) WORK. Over the past century this has already happened, and it can continue.

The second thought on labour productivity gain is related to the non-material nature of software (albeit a material basis is needed, i.e. resources and energy). Similar to the creative industry, is there a limit to how much that industry can grow? Well, people have a limited amount of time per day to listen to music or watch a film. Yet content creation continues to grow as the material limits are less important (or at least for now, until data centres will consume more power than the rest). In the same vein, software can grow, i.e. using the same amount of labour as input, simply produce much more. And since more software will be around, more engineering is needed to maintain that, with more software, until software becomes self perpetuating with nearly no utility left; then growth will stop naturally.
