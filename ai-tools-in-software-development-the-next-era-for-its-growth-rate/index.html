AI Tools in Software Development --- The Next Era for its Growth Rate

A qualitative model of the increase in productivity and growth rate in software development over the past few decades.

My previous post AI Tools in Software Development — Power Tools for Software Engineers and some AI-related preceding posts tried to demonstrate the power of AI-tools in software development. In a wider historical context one could say that the software industry has entered a new phase. The objective of this essay is to present a qualitative model of the increase in productivity and growth rate in software development over the past few decades in three distinct phases or eras.

Figure 1. Qualitatively expressed model of increasing growth rates in software development shown as increasing slopes on a graph with logarithmic scale for the total number of unique lines of code as a function of time.

Software Development Acceleration

Suppose the total number of unique lines of code in all code bases in the whole world could be captured in a time series — perhaps some accounting has been done somewhere — then I would have thought that the result is a monotonically increasing function showing the total number of unique lines of code per year. The number of unique lines of code added per year is likely not constant (linear growth), but proportional to the number of unique lines of code of the preceding year (exponential growth). In the latter case, the added lines of code per year divided by the number of lines of code in the preceding year determine the growth rate per year. For a constant grow rate, the resulting graph would show an exponential function on a linear scale; on a logarithmic scale the graph would be a line of constant slope, the growth rate. I would like to claim that three phases of nearly constant yet increasing grow rates can be observed in software development over the past decades:

  • mainly proprietary closed-source software development,
  • FOSS, free and open source software development,
  • AI-tool aided software development.

Closed Source Era

To me software development pre-2000 signifies the dark ages of the industry. As far as I was involved in or exposed to software development, people would keep software on a PC with no source control; development was not test driven, no sharing of source code occurred and most importantly, no peer review happened; some of the ‘closed’ source code from ‘professional’ suppliers I had visibility of was appalling. No wonder the authors were reluctant in showing poor quality engineering. Software engineers were hidden behind the business facade, for better or worse.

In general, commercial software was built using commercial tools and commercial components, often leaving a lot to hope for due to little or no competition. If a special component was needed for a piece of functionality, say a charting tool for creating nice views of exponential functions, it had to be bought, followed quite often by long integration woes. Alternatively, and as a consequence, people tended to build many mundane pieces of software from scratch and become quite protective and precious of their software source code, even if that software was only a component and not part of the company’s value proposition.

In a way, at that time software was nearly considered like a material product with a bill of materials including external software components. As a whole the growth rate was very limited in this world.

Open Source Era

A major paradigm shift was introduced by Richard Stallman through the Free Software Movement as ‘a social movement with the goal of obtaining and guaranteeing certain freedoms for software users’; making the source code available is a necessary condition to and consequence of realising this freedom. Later the ‘free-as in-freedom’ character and open source aspects were combined into the expression ‘Free and open-source software’ (FOSS) which is more specific and not wrongly tied to the open source nature of software. This marked the beginning of a software sharing culture (with peer reviews) which produced the foundations of a significant amount of software being used commercially: gcc, glibc, and many other elements of the now established software development ecosystem.

The new paradigm was initially fought against by incumbents with tooth and nail, but it prevailed as businesses gradually grasped the potential of leveraging FOSS, learned that it is the license that matters, and focused on their unique selling points, the part of the software that was unique to their business. Consequently, the best approach in this new world is to cooperate on the mundane parts: the operating system, the tools, and other pieces a software stack is built from; build on others and add value and differentiate only at the top or through increments. It is this delta that has commercial value, either as a necessary element for hardware, or as a service and means to an end, not as a product per-se.

Sharing software really took off in late 90s and early 2000s and found its way naturally into a lot of hardware produces and services: internet routers, TV sets, Android phones, and many other devices. If a company can leverage a large body of software to build a product on, then feed back some of the generic software in the global pools of FOSS, the growth rate is naturally significantly higher as in the preceding world. As a side-effect, software developer names became visible through the license headers hailing in ‘The revenge of the nerds’ as once used in a keynote speech at the Linux expo in Paris 1999; now developers gained more importance by being exposed on a merit basis.

AI Agentic Era

Large Language Models such as ChatGPT or Claude Sonnet were trained on vast amounts of data, scraped off the internet I believe, and partly curated (begging the question of what bias would be introduced). It can be assumed that naturally a significant amount of FOSS would have been ingested, too, together with computer science papers and other software development sources. The result is a mathematical representation of the global collective knowledge on computer science and software development. It is therefore not surprising that such a ‘super-coder’ is able to churn out new code given a well-presented problem specification using plain English.

There is a similarity to the open source software: take a large body of code, create a derivative, add a tiny bit of innovation, automatic or human, and feed it back into the pool which the next generation of AI will be trained on. The result is enormous growth in the code base, like with FOSS in the 2000s, but with a significantly higher iteration frequency. The implicit increase in the positive feedback loop gain is obvious which materialises as a substantial increase in the growth rate. That said, if a constant growth rate leads to an exponential expansion of total lines of unique code, then an increasing growth rate means growing exponential growth.

Conclusion

Based on the notion that growth rate is the increase of the total number of unique lines of code as a function the current total number of unique lines of code available to humans or machines, it seems to be follow that the growth rate is determined by the technical and economic constraints of a given period; closed source development offers limited growth through lack of sharing; FOSS development allows higher growth rates through sharing; AI-tool based development accelerates the growth rate through automatic generation and sharing of software leading to re-enforced exponential growth.

In this fast feedback loop of AI generated content the share of genuine human contribution decreases exponentially, too. One could say that the human contribution gets gradually diluted with the danger that certain ways of re-enforce themselves though numbers. What is the position of a software developer in this accelerating world? Possibly a conductor of agents who needs to be able to read the orchestral score in order to keep up with the speed and acceleration of development.
