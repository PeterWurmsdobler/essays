Changing the Face of a Country for the Sake of Energy Efficiency

England has a large stock of brick-built houses with poor energy efficiency; insulation could improve that at the cost of losing its face.

Whenever I come back to my adopted home country, England, by plane, I feel elated looking down onto the beautiful countryside, the lovely landscape, the fields and meadows in all shades of green, often surrounded by hedges [1]. In between I notice all these quaint little villages made up of brick-built houses; England seems to be God’s garden on earth indeed. Once landed, and later on the train journey to Cambridge, I still enjoy looking out of the window while going past these neat houses in those villages. Mostly with brick façades, they give English villages their very consistent and typical appearance, quite inviting and instilling a sensation of cosiness.

Once at home, however, the experience may well be a bit different: one discovers that English houses tend to be a bit cold and could do with better heating systems, some kind of ventilation system that maintains humidity at a constant level, and much more insulation. This essay investigates the energy-saving potential of cladding brick houses with external wall insulation, as well as of building new houses using modern building techniques, both of which would result in a very different look for an entire country. The question then is: is it worth changing the face of a country for the sake of energy efficiency and as a contribution to mitigating climate change?

Living Space Energy Consumption

A significant portion of energy is spent on housing; according to Residential energy expenditures and the relevance of changes in household circumstances [2], ‘In the UK, household’s residential energy consumption accounts for about 20–25% of the overall household direct and indirect CO2 emissions.’

The household energy expenditure comprises the construction, operation and decommissioning of dwellings, where the operational energy expenditure is mostly due to space heating (and cooling, though not so much in the UK), domestic hot water and running appliances of all sorts. While energy can be saved in all of these, to an extent that depends considerably on the occupiers’ habits, this essay focuses on space heating, which is tied to the construction: once the building is erected, it is more or less fixed by the internal temperature set-point, the outside weather and the thermal properties of the building.

Taken over the whole life of a building, the energy expenditure related to space heating is then the sum of the energy needed for construction, the average heating power times the life span, and the decommissioning energy. The average heating power is in turn a function of the external to internal temperature difference and overall thermal transmittance. Consequently, the balance of construction and decommissioning energy against life span and thermal transmittance is of utmost importance. A house that consumes a lot of energy in construction (concrete, bricks and mortar) needs to have a long life span and a low thermal transmittance to keep the energy expenditure in balance and to offset its construction.

There is one laudable aspect of the UK approach to housing: people do tend to extend their dwellings rather than demolishing them and building new ones from scratch in their stead. For instance, there are perhaps 3 million 1930s houses, all built to much the same blueprint, and many families add extensions to them. Even though they were built to a lower standard, extending and upgrading them may be quite energy efficient after all. It would be an interesting optimisation exercise to work out what the energy expenditure, thermal performance and life span of a new house would have to be in order to be more energy efficient over its full life span than the old but inefficient building it replaces. Simon Chung [3] from Sow Space [4], an architecture and planning practice in Oxford, UK, once said:

To quote Carl Elefante ‘the greenest building is the one that is already built’. We absolutely need to be using buildings to at least their designed lifespan, but we also need to be designing them to be easily deconstructed and / or retrofitted. In the UK, 80% of the buildings that will be standing in 2050 have already been built — we can’t repeat this enough (until we start getting close to 2050…). Hence the importance of retrofit, and the importance of not adding to our future retrofit problem.

Following Simon Chung’s advice to retrofit existing houses, there are a few parameters with which to improve their energy efficiency. The outside temperature is not negotiable; the inside temperature is, but let us assume that a constant 21 °C is desirable. From there, it is all about the overall thermal transmittance and its determinants: floor, windows, external walls and, most importantly, the roof or loft space (U-value of approximately 2.5 W/(m2 K) for uninsulated roof space; a lath-and-plaster ceiling like ours, without loft insulation, is about 5.2 W/(m2 K)). The latter is easy to address: if there is little or no loft insulation, anybody can go up into the loft and, over a weekend, put down 300 mm of rock wool to reach a comfortable 0.1 W/(m2 K). No need to dwell any further on that; fitting loft insulation ought to be made mandatory.

External Walls

Once the loft as the most significant thermal conductor has been addressed, next in line are the external walls. In the UK, new houses are built in a traditional manner: brick façade outside, 100 mm insulation, and aerated concrete blocks inside. The thermal transmittance (U-value) requirements for these kinds of external walls have been tightened over the years and, at the time of writing (2020), stood at a mandatory 0.2 W/(m2 K). In older buildings the external walls may have an empty cavity, or none at all and be completely solid, with very poor thermal performance, as the comparison in Figure 1 shows.

If, in addition, a high-performance 100 mm insulation is applied on the external walls, the U-value decreases significantly for the types of wall that have the poorest insulation, as shown in Figure 2.

Whereas cavity walls do show some improvement, the most significant change, more than an order of magnitude, can be observed for solid brick walls. Using data from The Greenage [5], the gains in energy efficiency can be seen as a function of wall type in Table 1.

In order to assess the total efficiency gain across the country, it is important to know the distribution of houses, e.g. as shown in What 175 years of data tell us about house price affordability in the UK [6]. It would be an interesting exercise to calculate a sum of the efficiency gains in the table, weighted by the number of houses completed in each period according to the housing completion graph. The result would be the average gain across the housing stock. While I might do that at some point, once I have found the data sources, it is probably safe to assume that the most significant gain would be in adding external wall insulation to all houses without a cavity wall, which may well be half of the existing housing stock. The consequences of adding a warm blanket to all these houses would be:

  • Less heating would be required; obviously, the heating energy expenditure would be significantly reduced.
  • No replacement gas or other boiler would be needed; a heat pump, perhaps powered by solar panels, may be sufficient
    (as reported in 2020, ‘UK should ban gas boiler sales by 2033, say climate advisers’ [7]).
  • The thermal energy store (the masonry) would be inside and the insulation outside, as it should be.

However, there is another consequence: the face of the country would change; no more brick look, but rendered façades instead. Perhaps that is a price worth paying for the sake of the climate. Brick buildings were introduced at some point in history; could they not be phased out again, making way for a new face of England?

Modern Alternatives

Suppose that all façades of most brick houses have now been fitted with external wall insulation and have been rendered; since the face of the country has already changed, why not go all the way? The UK could adopt more modern building techniques as used in Continental Europe, e.g. my native Austria.

Building technology in Austria is often quite advanced, in particular in terms of energy efficiency, and the work is generally carried out well, too. Walls are usually 50 cm thick, made of high-performance bricks whose thermal performance alone roughly matches the UK building requirements. For instance, a Wienerberger Porotherm 50.20.H.i Plan [8] brick is specified at 0.22 W/(m2 K), or Ziegelwerk Eder’s EDERBLOCK 50 VZ [9] achieves 0.2 W/(m2 K). In addition, external insulation is usually applied together with an insulating render, which brings the thermal transmittance down to 0.12 W/(m2 K), in this case using only a 38 cm Porotherm, which on its own exhibits 0.28 W/(m2 K), as shown in Figure 3. Compared with a common 38 cm brick wall, the difference is quite significant, and the energy expenditure for heating would be reduced by more than a factor of two.

Figure 3. Comparison of rendered modern brick with external insulation with a common 38 cm brick wall.

Last, but not least, these modern bricks are glued; there are no mortar joints, which usually constitute a thermal bridge. After the first course is laid on the foundation, one layer after the other is glued onto the previous one. It is like building a house out of large Lego bricks; a walk in the park for qualified English bricklayers who are capable of making beautiful brick walls. This way, one storey can be built per day, as a YouTube clip illustrates [10]. The result is an energy-efficient house built in a short time. The only drawback is that the house looks different: its bricks are no longer visible, but rendered. If the UK were to adopt this building technique, the face of the country would change again, and, in my view, for the better.

Conclusion

It is self-evident that more insulation will help reduce energy expenditure and, as a consequence, the carbon footprint of housing. The announcement at the time (2020), reported as ‘Bidding starts for £10bn whole house net zero retrofit deal’ [11], was most welcome. It would also be desirable for the UK to adopt more modern building techniques to achieve better-performing buildings much more quickly. Both together would change the look of a whole country within a decade or two.

The reader might ask: is it worth it? Well, one could install a PV system to power a heat pump to keep an old brick building warm; it is a net-zero game, as all energy from the sun and the surroundings is pumped into the house, eventually to leave through the walls. This might be a solution for some, in particular for listed buildings. For the rest, we might get used to a new look; a similar change must have happened centuries ago with the transition to brick buildings. Some people might have objected to that change, too.

One last note: with more insulation, a heat-recovery ventilation system becomes advisable to control indoor humidity; alternatively, one can simply follow a strict airing regime as described in Managing Relative Humidity in an English Home.
