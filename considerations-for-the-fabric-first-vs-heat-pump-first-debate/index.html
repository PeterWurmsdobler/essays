Considerations for the Fabric-First vs Heat-Pump-First Debate

How to balance spending on insulation against the size of a heat pump: a simple cost optimisation for our 1930s house.

Domestic heat pumps are an important element in the electrification of our economies and, as such, an important contribution to the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions in the fight against climate change. Yet there seems to be a divide between those who think that government policy should first address the shortcomings of the poorly insulated housing stock, i.e. insulate the building fabric, e.g. Insulate Britain [1], and those who think that adopting heat pumps, enabled through the Boiler Upgrade Scheme (BUS) [2], matters most. To understand the debate better, I have been reading a few books on heat pumps. Among them, ‘So You’re Thinking About a Heat Pump: The UK Homeowner’s Guide to Heat Pumps’ [3] was quite helpful, as it addresses the fabric-first vs heat-pump-first debate in a well-balanced manner.

The objective of this essay is to inform that debate. To me, it is not so much a question of choosing one or the other, as if they were mutually exclusive, but a question of balance in investment: which combination of measures will have the most impact? It boils down to an optimisation problem. To this end, the essay presents a quantitative analysis of our own house, using estimated heat pump installation and home insulation costs to determine an optimal outcome.

Analysis Scenario

The background to this analysis is the experience recorded in Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses, an account of a series of improvements made to our 1930s semi-detached house over the past few years. The house started out in the following state:

  • Solid brick walls and walls in some areas only 10 cm thick,
  • Mostly basic double glazing, with single glazing in the entrance area,
  • Minimal loft insulation (a thin fleece, about one inch thick, between the joists) and no insulation in the eaves (skeiling).

In this state, my U-value calculation put the worst-case annual heat loss of our house in Cambridge, UK, at about 25,000 kWh/year, which is quite a lot by modern standards. The heating power needed at winter conditions (21 °C inside, 2 °C outside, the average low temperature in January) was estimated to be 9 kW. There was a lot of scope for improving the thermal performance.

With all improvements implemented except external wall insulation and triple glazing, our house now needs about 3.6 kW at these winter conditions (188 ×19 ≈3,570 W, from the heat transfer coefficient derived in How the Spark Gap Drives Radiator Upgrades for Heat Pump Installations). In 2025 we used 11,508 kWh of gas, of which about 4,000 kWh went to domestic hot water, leaving about 7,100 kWh of heat for space heating at a boiler efficiency of 95%. Relating this annual heat demand to the heating power gives an empirical factor of about 1,990 kWh/year per kW of heating power needed at winter conditions³.

Heat Pump Options

This heat demand could be met by an electrically driven heat pump. Its Seasonal Coefficient of Performance (SCOP), the ratio of heat output to electricity input over the year, then determines how much electrical energy is needed to produce the same amount of heat; the SCOP can be expected to be around 4.0 for modern air-source heat pumps under UK conditions. Optimisation Objective

The optimum balance between heat pump and fabric investment lies at the minimum of a cost function, here the combined expenditure on heat pump and fabric improvement. To that end, the improvements are applied in order of cost-effectiveness, the most cost-effective first, and their cost accumulates from the original state of the house; as the cumulative cost increases, the heat loss decreases. For every point along this path, a heat pump is sized to meet the remaining heating power⁵, and its power-dependent cost is added to the total.

Two scenarios are examined:

1.
No Grant: Heat pump installation without government support,
2.
With £7,500 Grant: Heat pump with the UK government’s BUS grant.

Both scenarios are analysed in two cases: first, with capital expenditure only, and second, with both capital and operational expenditure over a 25-year lifetime.

Capital Expenditure Only

When considering only the upfront capital costs of installation, the analysis yields the same optimal strategy in both scenarios.

Scenario 1: No Government Grant

Without any government support, the optimal approach requires a total capital investment of £8,839, which consists of:

  • £350 for insulation (loft insulation + rock wool in bay window, reducing heating power from 9.0 kW to 4.8 kW),
  • £8,489 for a 4.8 kW heat pump installation.

Figure 1. Scenario 1: No Government Grant – Total Capital Cost vs Heat Loss Reduction.

Implementing the two most cost-effective improvements and installing a moderately sized heat pump is therefore the optimum on capital costs alone.

Scenario 2: With £7,500 Government Grant

The BUS provides a £7,500 grant towards a heat pump installation. With this support, the total capital cost falls to £1,339:

  • £350 for insulation as before, 4.8 kW heat loss at winter conditions,
  • £989 for a 4.8 kW heat pump (after £7,500 grant deduction).

Figure 2. Scenario 2: With £7,500 Government Grant – Total Capital Cost vs Heat Loss Reduction.

On capital costs alone, the optimal strategy is therefore identical in both scenarios: invest £350 in the two most cost-effective improvements (loft and bay window insulation) to reduce the heating power to 4.8 kW. The grant reduces the upfront cost by £7,500, making heat pumps more affordable without changing the underlying optimisation.

Capital and Operational Expenditure

The picture changes when operational costs, specifically electricity consumption, are included over the 25-year lifetime of the system. For this analysis, I assume:

  • the 1,990 kWh/(year kW) factor applied to the heating power to obtain the expected annual heat demand,
  • an electricity rate of £0.15/kWh, achievable on a dedicated heat pump or time-of-use tariff (the OVO energy rate in Convincing Numbers to Install a Heat Pump for Domestic Heating).

Scenario 1: No Government Grant

Including 25 years of electricity costs at £0.15/kWh yields a total lifecycle cost of £17,407 with:

  • Capital costs: £9,684 (insulation + heat pump),
  • Runtime costs: £7,722 (44.4% of total).

Figure 3. Scenario 1: No Government Grant – Total Lifecycle Cost (Capital and 25-Year Running Costs) vs Heat Loss Reduction.

The runtime costs are now of the same order as the capital investment, so operational expenses weigh significantly in the long-term economics. The optimal heating power shifts down from 4.8 kW to 4.1 kW, requiring £1,650 in insulation: loft, bay window, the second layer on the ground floor and the entrance glazing and insulation; this is close to what was actually done in the house. Annual electricity consumption at this optimal point is 2,059 kWh, costing £309 per year at £0.15/kWh. The optimum holds across the price-cap rates of Q1 2024 to Q1 2026 (Table ??): only the running costs rise, to £530 a year at the mean rate, and external wall insulation would pay off only from about 59 p/kWh.

Scenario 2: With £7,500 Government Grant

With the £7,500 grant and 25 years of operation, the total lifecycle cost is £9,907, including:

  • Capital costs: £2,184 (insulation + heat pump after grant),
  • Runtime costs: £7,722 (78.0% of total).

Figure 4. Scenario 2: With £7,500 Government Grant – Total Lifecycle Cost (Capital and 25-Year Running Costs) vs Heat Loss Reduction.

The grant reduces the capital costs but leaves the runtime costs unchanged; these now make up 78% of the total lifecycle cost, which shows that the grant addresses the upfront barrier rather than the long-term economics. The optimum itself is the same as without the grant.

Conclusion

The fabric-first vs heat-pump-first debate has a clearer answer when heat pump efficiency is properly accounted for. On capital costs alone, minimal insulation (£350) combined with a moderately sized heat pump is optimal, reducing the heating power to 4.8 kW. When operational costs over a 25-year lifetime are included, the optimal strategy remains similar: £1,650 in insulation (the four most cost-effective measures) and a heating power of 4.1 kW.

The main finding is that modern heat pumps with SCOP ≈ 4 are sufficiently efficient that electricity costs, while substantial (£309/year or £7,722 over 25 years at £0.15/kWh), do not justify extensive insulation measures like external wall insulation (£12,000) or triple glazing (£10,000). The optimal solution lies in implementing the most cost-effective improvements (loft, bay window, second ground-floor layer and entrance), which provide good thermal performance at modest cost.

With the government grant, runtime costs dominate the total lifecycle cost (78%), yet the grant’s £7,500 capital cost reduction has a larger impact on total cost than would be achieved through extensive insulation investment. The grant addresses the upfront cost, while the efficiency of the heat pump keeps operational costs manageable.

For a 25-year ownership horizon, the analysis therefore suggests that, for this house, a balanced ‘Sensible Insulation, then Heat Pump’ approach is optimal: a moderate insulation investment (£350–£1,650) combined with a properly sized heat pump provides the best economic outcome, whether considering capital costs alone or including 25 years of operation.
