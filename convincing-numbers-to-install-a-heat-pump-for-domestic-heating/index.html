Convincing Numbers to Install a Heat Pump for Domestic Heating

Simple calculations on whether replacing a gas boiler with a heat pump makes economic sense in the UK, and how the ratio of electricity to gas prices has moved since.

Being ecologically minded, I have been trying to reduce our family’s footprint wherever possible, mostly through more insulation, as described in Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses. The next item on the list would be a heat pump to replace our gas boiler for domestic hot water and space heating. In the past I was put off by a perhaps overly simplistic line of reasoning that only compared the ratio of electricity to gas price with the Coefficient of Performance (COP), until a post by Jan Rosenow [1] and a subsequent discussion convinced me otherwise. Here I would like to share my Python calculations, based on a spreadsheet created by Jan, so that others can weigh up a heat pump for themselves.

Initial Simplistic View

Our recorded gas consumption for 2025 was about 11,500 kWh, of which about 4,000 kWh went to domestic hot water and about 7,500 kWh to space heating; at a boiler efficiency of 95% this is about 10,900 kWh of heat, and a heat pump would have to supply both. Assuming a COP of 3 as a worst case, a heat pump would need 1/COP = 1/3 of that heat in electricity, i.e. about 3,600 kWh. However, electricity cost about 4 times as much as gas with my supplier at their standard rates in 2024. The heat pump would therefore use a third of the energy, but at 4 times the price; allowing for the boiler efficiency, the running costs would be about a quarter higher (£893 instead of £718 a year), taking into account only the unit prices at the standard rate and a conservative COP of 3.

As for the capital expenditure, the starting point is an installer’s initial quotation in 2024 of £12k, less a £7.5k grant from the UK government (it was £5k when I last looked into heat pumps); this still leaves an investment of £4.5k. Since the running costs of a heat pump would be higher under the above simplistic assumptions, the system would appear never to pay for itself.

If gas prices were higher (with the environmental costs priced in through higher carbon taxes), or if electricity prices were more dynamic, so that I could buy wind or solar energy when in surplus (the thermal inertia of the house and the water tank allowing for a demand response), the price ratio would move in favour of heat pumps. Once the COP exceeds the break-even COP defined in the postscript, heating the house with a heat pump becomes economical.

Jan’s Improved Model

The picture changes, however, once two additional factors are taken into account:

  1. the non-negligible standing charge for gas, a daily fee, independent of consumption, for remaining connected to the gas grid, and
  2. alternative electricity suppliers that offer tariffs for heat pumps and source their energy from wind or solar farms.

Having turned Jan’s spreadsheet into a small Python program, I found it instructive to see how the cost of running a heat pump falls with increasing COP for different suppliers:

Figure 1. Comparison of gas boiler and heat pump costs when disconnecting gas.

The gas standing charge [2] is taken as £115.559 per year and the standard gas price as 0.0624 £/kWh, both from the 2024 price cap; applied to our 2025 consumption of 11,508 kWh of gas, they give an annual gas bill of £834. The electricity prices (in 2024) are 0.245 £/kWh for Standard [2], 0.2009 £/kWh for Octopus Cosy [3], 0.1799 £/kWh for Octopus Agile (average) [3] and 0.15 £/kWh for OVO energy [4]. The break-even COPs for these four tariffs are then easily calculated: 3.21, 2.63, 2.36 and 1.97, respectively; they include the saving of the gas standing charge. These COPs should be achievable by a well-designed installation in the moderate UK climate. With an average COP of 3, the OVO energy tariff and about 10,900 kWh of heat a year, the annual saving at 2024 prices is £287, so the capital expenditure of £4.5k mentioned above would take about 16 years to recover.

In summary, taking into account heat pump tariffs as well as the opportunity to disconnect the gas supply altogether (and so save the standing charge), there is an economic case for replacing a gas boiler with a heat pump.

Postscript: The Spark Gap from 2024 to 2026

The ratio of the unit price of electricity to that of gas is known as the spark gap. Since our gas boiler converts gas to heat at 95% efficiency, a heat pump costs as much per unit of heat as the boiler when its COP equals the spark gap times the boiler efficiency; I call this the break-even COP. Unlike the break-even points above, it refers to unit energy costs alone, without the saving of the gas standing charge. An efficiency of 95% corresponds to a very efficient condensing boiler such as our Viessmann Vitodens 222-F. Modern condensing boilers are rated at 90–94% in laboratory tests, but in practice often achieve only 80–83% unless the return water temperature stays below about 54 °C, the point below which the flue gases condense. Against such a boiler the break-even COP at the central spark gap below falls from 3.86 to about 3.25–3.37, and against a poorly performing boiler at 75% to about 3.05; it is easy to make a case for a heat pump by comparing it with a poor boiler. The Ofgem price caps, and with them the spark gap, change from quarter to quarter, as Table 1 shows for the nine quarters from Q1 2024 to Q1 2026.

Table 1. Ofgem energy price caps, Q1 2024 to Q1 2026: GB average unit rates and gas standing charge for a typical dual-fuel household paying by Direct Debit, including VAT [5]. The break-even COP is the spark gap times the boiler efficiency of 0.95.

Because any comparison between heat pump and gas boiler is sensitive to this ratio, the calculations in my other essays on heat pumps do not rely on a single quarter. They use the mean unit prices as a central case, a spark gap of 4.06 and a break-even COP of 3.86, and give the range spanned by the quarters with the lowest (Q1 2024, 3.86) and the highest (Q1 2026, 4.67) spark gap, i.e. a break-even COP of 3.66–4.44. Results are given as the central value followed by the range in brackets¹. This essay keeps its original 2024 tariffs, whose standard rates give a spark gap of 3.93, close to the lower end of that range.
