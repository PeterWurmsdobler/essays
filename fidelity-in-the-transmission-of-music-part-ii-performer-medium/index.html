Fidelity in the Transmission of Music, Part II: Performer --- Medium

Several links in the chain from a performance to the medium determine the fidelity of the recording to what could be heard live.

Whenever I went to the Golden Hall in the Vienna Musikverein I noticed quite a few steel wires being strung from one side to the other, both over the stage and the audience, with an array of microphones hanging off these wires. I do not know much about the recording process, but I can only imagine that recording, mixing and releasing is quite an art, compressing the live performance spectrum SP into the medium carrier spectrum SM, with aspects such as but not limited to:

  • choosing a venue with appropriate room acoustics [1, ch. 28],
  • positioning of high bandwidth microphones in suitable locations [2, chs 6 and 7],
  • microphone signal conditioning and transmission with little loss of quality,
  • multi-track recording with digital or analogue technology with high signal-to-noise ratio and dynamic range way above the medium requirement,
  • mixing and balancing multiple tracks into a stereo signal,
  • down-sampling and reduction to target medium.

The linear chain that converts the performance spectrum into the medium spectrum is in reality fraught with some feedback loops, as explained in Conceptual Feedback Loops in the Transmission of Recorded Music.

Recording, Mixing & Release

What happens in this signal processing chain is that the soundscape of the performance in a concert hall is captured in many recorded tracks at very high definition in frequency and signal magnitude, but eventually all has to be distilled to (and released onto) the target medium format with as consequence a reduction in bandwidth and quantisation. This may happen in various stages as shown in Figure 1 and explored below.

Microphone & Transmission

Suppose the stereo frequency spectrum SP of the sound pressure at the location of the listener in a concert is quasi-infinite as shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Conceptual sound pressure signal with quasi-infinite bandwidth.

This ideal signal will, however, be picked up by an array of microphones which all have a certain bandwidth. As one can imagine, there will be a roll-off at low and high frequencies, with some frequency ranges even elevated as shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3. Modelled amplitude (dB) and phase (degrees) response curves for a cardioid condenser microphone across the human audible spectrum (20 Hz–20 kHz), after the published response of the AKG Perception 420 [3].

A microphone is an electro-mechanical device that translates air pressure into an electrical voltage, depending on its principle [4, secs 10-9 to 10-11]. An important aspect is the dependency on the amplitude, as at high sound pressure mechanical or electrical parts will start to saturate and create harmonic distortions (which we all know from the clipping effect when somebody speaks into a microphone at too close distance) as shown in Figure 4.

Figure 4. Modelled microphone THD+N (%) vs. input amplitude (dB SPL), showing the low-level noise floor valley, steady linear range, and sudden saturation knee leading to hard clipping plateau, with the acoustic overload point at 10% THD as defined by TDK InvenSense [5].

The resulting analogue signals for all microphones will consequently carry a bandwidth-limited representation of the sound pressure present in the concert hall as voltages, but with a distortion in time and frequency domain, as shown in Figure 5.

Figure 5. Analogue voltage signal representing sound pressure with finite bandwidth.

Note that it will be difficult to recover any information outside that bandwidth. In addition, the microphone signal will be affected by sound noise but also electrical (or thermal) noise picked up on the path to the recording desk which, using balanced transmission, should be minimal.

Pre-amplifier and Level Adjustment

The signal level at the microphone output needs to be adjusted to the level needed for the subsequent sampling stage. There is no power involved and, these days, low-noise operational amplifiers or amplifier chips with sufficient signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) are cheaply available for frequency ranges up to the MHz and beyond. The bandwidth for audio applications, up to say 100 kHz, should not constitute a problem any more; therefore I do not consider this stage in much more detail but assume the transfer function to be constant.

A Google search for low noise audio operational amplifier for microphones yields a long list including but not limited to components by Texas Instruments [6] or Analog Devices [7] such as OPA1692 [8] and LT1115 [9], respectively. These kinds of operational amplifiers have a gain–bandwidth product of several MHz or more, so that with feedback, at the gains needed here, their frequency response is flat well beyond the audio band; the THD+N of the OPA1692 is as low as 0.00004%, i.e. about 128 dB below the signal.

Analogue to Digital Conversion

A crucial stage in the signal processing chain is the transition from the analogue to the digital time domain which happens in the analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) at the recording frequency and bit-width which is certainly much above the release specification, as shown in Figure 6.

Figure 6. Sampling of analogue signal at recording frequency and resolution.

There is no need to go into further detail on the sampling process and theory behind that [10, secs 4.2–4.5] [11, ch. 4]; suffice to say that the bandwidth of the signal is limited to the Nyquist limit of half the sampling frequency, e.g. 24 kHz for 48 kHz, or 48 kHz for 96 kHz, but usually even less than that because of the anti-aliasing low-pass filter cut-off frequency. No frequency, no information beyond the Nyquist frequency limit can be represented in the digital domain. For completeness, an anti-aliasing low-pass filter in front of the ADC will make sure that no artefacts of higher frequencies mirror down into the audible band.

In addition, sampling at a certain bit-width will add quantisation noise due to the rounding to a fixed number of values [10, secs 4.9–4.10] [11, sec. 8.3]. The dynamic range and signal-to-noise ratio depend on the bit-resolution, in the order of 96 dB for 16 bits and, in practice, 120 dB for 24 bits¹. Ideally, if very low-noise amplifiers are used in the chain up to the ADC, the noise floor of the analogue signal to be digitised ought to lie below the quantisation noise, but probably will not always be. Any signal below that noise floor or outside the dynamic range of the ADC will not be represented in the digital signal and may not be easily recovered at a later stage any more (unless with coherent integration).

That said, the rounding error only behaves like noise as long as the signal is large and busy compared to a quantisation step. For a quiet signal, say the reverberation fading away at the end of a piece, the error follows the signal and turns into distortion, i.e. harmonics which are audible and unpleasant. The remedy is dither [10, secs 4.11–4.13]: a small amount of random noise, in the order of one quantisation step, added before quantising. It decorrelates the rounding error from the signal and turns the distortion into a constant, benign noise floor; perhaps surprisingly, it also allows signals well below the smallest step to be represented on average, so that they remain audible in the noise rather than disappear. In practice, the noise of the analogue electronics in front of the ADC often serves as dither by itself.

Note that the reference voltage and the clock source are of utmost importance. The clock defines the instants at which the signal is sampled; if the crystal oscillator is not as stable as expected, these instants vary in time, an effect commonly called jitter [10, sec. 4.7]. Having worked for many years on radar systems, I prefer to call it what it is: phase noise, the result of variations in the frequency of the oscillator; a variation in frequency can always be expressed as a variation in the phase of a constant, ideal mean frequency. Its effect on the sampled signal is nasty. An error in time becomes an error in amplitude proportional to how fast the signal changes at that instant, i.e. to the derivative of the signal and hence to its amplitude and frequency. The resulting noise is therefore not simply added to the signal but depends on it, i.e. it is signal-dependent noise: every tone is spread into a skirt of noise on either side, and a strong tone generates noise in proportion to its amplitude. All effort put into a high SNR pre-amplifier, high sampling frequency and bit resolution will be obliterated and dwarfed by the effects of an unstable clock. The same applies to the DAC on the playback side, where the clock defines the instants at which the samples are turned into voltages.

As a bottom line, precise clocks and ADCs with sufficient bit resolution and sampling frequencies well above the audible spectrum are inexpensive and ubiquitous, see Texas Instruments [12], Analog Devices [7], Cirrus Logic [13] or many others, offering an SNR of up to 127 dB and sampling rates of 768 kHz². Unless some principles of sampling theory are violated, it can be expected that a decent recording desk can produce an image of the original spectrum SP at a very high level of fidelity, in frequency, dynamic range and SNR.

Mixing

Once all the signals live in the digital domain, no signal below the dynamic range defined by the sampling resolution or frequency beyond the Nyquist limit can be represented or will ever be present. However, all signals can be mixed and transformed at a much higher resolution, even as floating point numbers with quasi-infinite dynamic range, or at least as much as it matters for the human ear [10, secs 3.12–3.14]. The sky is the limit, allowing one to filter out unwanted frequencies, noise or sounds, compensate for a microphone frequency response or even combine several signals to one, and funnelling many tracks to a stereo pair at high resolution (in frequency and amplitude).

Release

Unfortunately, at some point all this wide band, high dynamic range information has to be decimated to what the release medium is capable of carrying [10, sec. 3.24] [11, sec. 9.2.2]. Reducing the word length from, say, 24 to 16 bits is a requantisation and needs dither for the same reasons as the ADC [10, sec. 4.12]; done properly, quiet passages fade into a gentle noise floor rather than into distortion. While there may be some high resolution audio formats on the market, the most common one is still the compact disc format, in essence a stereo signal with 16 bits per channel at 44.1 kHz.

Figure 7. Decimation of digital signal to release frequency and resolution.

I can only reiterate: once the high resolution mixing output has been decimated to the release frequency, there is no information above the associated Nyquist limit frequency, 22.05 kHz for digital audio CD; note that this frequency is still above the limit of human ears which is about 20 kHz for young listeners and most likely around 15 kHz for older people [1, ch. 4].

The SNR and theoretical dynamic range of a digital audio CD are about 96 dB for its 16-bit resolution. With the dynamic range of the human ear of about 120 dB and the noise floor in a quiet room at about 40 dB, exploiting the whole CD range of 96 dB on top would be pushing the sound pressure to 136 dB, above the pain threshold [14, ch. 2]. Bottom line, 16 bits at 44.1 kHz seem to match the limits of human hearing quite well [10, sec. 13.8] [1, ch. 4] and be sufficient for many recordings, provided care has been taken in all the steps to the release.

Fidelity in Recording, Mixing & Release

As far as fidelity is concerned in the recording, mixing & release process, high fidelity would mean that the digital stereo signal on the medium SM is a very close representation of the sound experienced at the live performance SP . I could imagine that this process can yield high and low fidelity representations, depending on how much effort is put into all; most links in the chain have a flat response, with the weakest link perhaps being the room acoustics, the microphone and its positioning. But I would hope that it is safe to assume that most modern releases are the result of a diligent recording & mixing process and exhibit a very high level of fidelity.
