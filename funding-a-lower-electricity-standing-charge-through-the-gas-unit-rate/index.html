Funding a Lower Electricity Standing Charge through the Gas Unit Rate

An alternative to scrapping the gas standing charge: gas 1p per kWh dearer, electricity standing charge 46% lower, same total revenue.

In Will Scrapping the Gas Standing Charge Move Households Off Gas? I argued that the government’s reform, which moves the gas standing charge into the unit rate, makes gas cheaper for most households and so encourages them to stay with gas. That left me wondering what would encourage them to move. Well, here is a proposal: keep the gas standing charge, raise the gas unit rate by 1p per kilowatt hour and return the money through the electricity standing charge.

In this essay I would like to work out who gains and who pays, and what the proposal does to the case for a heat pump. My claim is that this proposal strengthens the case for a heat pump without changing total revenue, at the price of a small increase in the gas bill of the median gas household. I worked it out with the same data as before, now with the electricity consumption of the same properties added, and with the full-year simulation of a heat pump in the mean home.

The Proposal

Currently a household with both connections pays a standing charge for gas and one for electricity. My proposal leaves the first as it is, lowers the second and raises the gas unit rate.

Rationale

The government’s reform moves the fixed cost of gas into the gas unit rate. My proposal keeps the gas standing charge and moves part of the fixed cost of electricity into the gas unit rate instead. So why give the money back through the electricity standing charge? Well, for a household deciding on a heat pump it makes a difference. Leaving gas saves the gas standing charge of £108 a year as well as the cost of the gas units, so keeping the standing charge keeps that saving, and a higher unit rate adds to it.

The electricity standing charge of £190 a year, i.e. 52 p/day, on the other hand, is paid whatever the household heats with, so cutting it favours neither fuel; it returns to the households the money that the higher gas unit rate raises. Ideally, I would cut the electricity standing charge for connections below 100 A, which still allows a heat pump to run, and keep it in full for higher ratings, i.e. make the standing charge a function of the amperage¹.

The Arithmetic

The DESNZ sample of four million properties holds both the electricity and the gas consumption of each property [2]². For prices I use the Ofgem price cap of October to December 2026 for direct debit [3]; all household prices include VAT, 5% on gas and none on electricity, for which a temporary zero rate applies until 30 June 2027 [4]. The data hold no power rating, so I assume in the calculation that every household qualifies for the cut, although in practice some will not.

An increase of 1p per kilowatt hour on the 223.7 TWh of gas would generate a revenue of £2.1 billion a year, VAT excluded, whereas the electricity standing charge, £190 a year for each of 24.5 million properties, generates £4.65 billion in revenue. So 1p pays for a cut of the charge by 46%, i.e. £87 on average, from £72 to £103 depending on the region. Dropping the charge altogether would need 2.2p on gas, i.e. 27% of the unit rate, and 31% of gas households would then pay over £100 a year more; I do not pursue that, as it asks too much of those households.

The Households

The proposal changes the bills of households with gas and of those without it, so I looked at both.

Electricity use is much smaller, with a median of 2,600 kWh and a mean of 3,359 kWh, and has the same long tail to the right. Net Change for Households

So who pays and who gains? The net change of a property is about 1p times its gas use minus £87, so it depends on gas use alone. Figure 4 draws it as Figure 2 does for gas: properties by gas consumption (top), the net change of one property (middle) and the two multiplied (bottom). The properties without gas use no gas and only save; they form the thick bar at 0 kWh, cut off because it is far taller than the rest.

Figure 4. Net change of the annual bill under the proposal, by gas consumption. Top: properties by annual gas consumption; the 5.1 million properties without gas are the thick bar at 0 kWh, cut off at the top. Middle: net change of one property at a given gas consumption, the extra gas cost less the average saving of £87. Bottom: the two multiplied, the change in all bills together for each 100 kWh, with the properties without gas saving £435 million (bar cut); green is a saving, orange an extra cost.

The line crosses zero at about 8,700 kWh: the 41% of the gas households that use less gas, and all the properties without gas, pay less, which makes 53% of all 24.5 million properties; the others pay more. The break-even moves with the region, because the electricity charge does, from 7,200 kWh in London to 10,300 kWh in the north east and in Yorkshire. The median gas household pays £12 more per year, i.e. £1 a month. For 63% of gas households the change is smaller than £50 in either direction; 12% pay more than £100 and 2.5% more than £200, while 9% save more than £50.

About £0.7 billion a year moves from the households that pay more to those that pay less. Of the £2.2 billion that gas households pay extra for gas, £1.7 billion comes back to them through the electricity charge and £0.4 billion goes to the properties without gas; the rest is VAT.

Which Homes Gain

Consumption follows the size and type of the home, so the change does too. Figure 5 shows the average change by type and by size. The properties without gas gain £85 on average, flats £10 and homes of 50 m2 or less £18; the other types pay between £16 and £28 more, detached houses £65, and homes above 200 m2 £155.

Figure 5. Mean change of the annual bill under the proposal, by property type, including the properties without gas, and by floor area of the homes with gas; negative means a saving.

Moving Off Gas

A heat pump replaces the gas bill with a bill for electricity. The proposal makes the gas bill, and with it the saving, larger and leaves the price of electricity alone. Since the gas unit price is higher under the proposal, the spark gap, i.e. the ratio of the electricity to the gas unit price, falls from 3.29 to 2.92, and a heat pump breaks even at a lower Coefficient of Performance (COP).

Replacing the Boiler

I take the mean home, the house of Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Space Heating and Domestic Hot Water, simulated over 2025. It uses 11,371 kWh of gas, close to the mean, and a heat pump would use 2,852 kWh of electricity. At today’s prices the gas bill is £1,016 a year and the electricity for the heat pump £749, so the heat pump gain, the saving against gas, is £267 a year. Table 1 shows what each policy does to the annual bill of this home against today, if it stays on gas and if it moves to a heat pump. The reform leaves the heat pump gain where it was. With the proposal, the household that stays pays £27 more and the one that moves £354 less, so the gain grows by £114.

Table 1. Change of the annual bill of the mean home against today, in pounds, at the Ofgem cap of October to December 2026, if it stays on gas and if it moves to a heat pump. The last column, the heat pump gain, is the difference between the other two: how much less the home pays with a heat pump than by staying on gas. The reform scraps the gas standing charge; the proposal includes the cut of £87 of the electricity standing charge.

The mean home is just one case. Figure 6 shows the annual cost of heating at every level of heat demand, with gas under today’s charges, the reform and the proposal, and with a heat pump, whose electricity is a quarter of the gas use, as in the mean home (a seasonal COP of 3.79). A heat pump can be installed under all three policies, and its line is the same today and with the reform. The distance between a gas line and the heat pump line is the heat pump gain.

Figure 6. Annual cost of heating by the heat needed per year, with a gas boiler under today’s charges, with the reform (gas standing charge scrapped) and with the proposal, and with a heat pump. The proposal lines are net of the £87 cut of the electricity standing charge, which the household receives whatever it heats with. The dots mark the mean home of the table; the vertical lines are the median and the mean.

The reform lowers the gas line at low demand and raises it at high demand, so the gain falls for demand below the mean and rises above it: the compensation comes through the gas bill, and a household that leaves gas gives it up. With the proposal the gas line rises by 1p per kilowatt hour and both lines drop by £87, because the compensation comes through the electricity bill, which the household keeps. As a result the gap is wider than today at any demand above zero; at 20,000 kWh of heat it is £613 against £402.

Figure 7 adds the capital, as in Viewing a Heat Pump Installation through the Levelised Cost of Heating: the cost of heating per day over 25 years, with boilers at £2,000 to £4,000, a heat pump at £3,500 to £7,500 after the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant, and finance at 5%. The prices are those of October 2026, with a spark gap of 3.29, lower than the 4.06 of that essay, so the heat pump already leads today.

Figure 7. Levelised cost of heating of the mean home in pounds per day over 25 years, capital included, for a gas boiler and a heat pump under today’s charges, with the reform (gas standing charge scrapped) and with the proposal; the bars span the range of installation costs and the shaded band marks the range of the gas boiler.

Today the two ranges overlap, £3.73 to £4.40 for gas against £3.00 to £3.77 for the heat pump, and the reform does not change that. With the proposal they separate: the cheapest gas boiler, £4.04 a day, costs more than the dearest heat pump, £3.77, and the heat pump is ahead by £0.95 to £1.04 a day instead of £0.64 to £0.73.

Cooking with Gas

The proposal keeps the gas standing charge, so a household that cooks with gas alone keeps its reason to disconnect. For instance, a household using 1,000 kWh pays £188 a year for gas, whereas an induction hob would need about 520 kWh of electricity, i.e. £136, assuming efficiencies of 40% for the gas hob and 77% for the induction hob [5]. Leaving saves £52 today and £62 with the proposal; with the reform the gas bill falls to £89 and the induction hob costs £47 more, encouraging the homes to stay with gas.

Conclusion

In short, the proposal moves money from gas to electricity without changing total revenue. It raises the gain from a heat pump and separates the cost ranges of gas and heat pump, where the reform changes nothing. The price is that 59% of the gas households pay a little more, the median one £12 a year; against that, 53% of all properties pay less, among them every property without gas.

Seen by gas consumption, the proposal and the reform look alike: in both, the households that use most gas pay and those that use least gain. What differs is where the money goes. The reform moves it within the gas world; the proposal moves it from the gas world into the electricity world, where a household that leaves gas keeps it. To me that is why it enables electrification; gas consumption pays for the electrification of heating.

That being said, this is a static calculation, not observed behaviour. Each 1% of connections that keep the standing charge in full generates £21 million more in revenue, and when the temporary zero rate of VAT on electricity ends, the figures shift by a few pounds.
