How the Spark Gap Drives Radiator Upgrades for Heat Pump Installations

The ratio of electricity to gas prices, rather than the technology, sets the flow temperature a heat pump needs and hence whether the radiators must be upgraded.

Impediments to UK Heat Pump Adoption and Possible Solutions addresses the complexity (and cost) of heat pump installations as one impediment to their wider adoption; this essay is about another, the spark gap, and its effect on the required radiator capacity.

Various sources such as ‘So You’re Thinking About a Heat Pump: The UK Homeowner’s Guide to Heat Pumps’ [1] or ‘The Ultimate Guide to Heat Pumps: Britain’s best installers and experts tell you exactly what to watch for and what to ask’ [2] recommend radiator upgrades, which unfortunately increase installation costs. I had hoped to avoid such an upgrade by using a heat pump in tandem with our log burner, but was not sure whether that would work. To make a more informed decision, I carried out a simple analysis using a steady-state thermal model.

The outcome does not come as a surprise: the spark gap, together with the gas boiler efficiency, directly sets the minimum Coefficient of Performance (COP) a heat pump must achieve to be cheaper to run than a gas boiler. Reaching that COP requires sufficiently low flow temperatures at a given outdoor temperature, and lower flow temperatures require more radiator capacity to deliver the same power. Radiator upgrades are therefore driven by the spark gap rather than being an inherent requirement of heat pump technology; whether they are needed depends on the heating load and on which costs are accounted for, as investigated in this essay.

This analysis uses the central value and range of the spark gap set out in the postscript to Convincing Numbers to Install a Heat Pump for Domestic Heating, i.e. a spark gap of 4.06 (3.86–4.67) and a break-even COP of 3.86 (3.66–4.44).

In short, for average winter heating loads in our home, the current radiators turn out to be sufficient on unit energy costs alone, across the whole range of spark gaps. At design temperature conditions (−2 °C for Cambridge), however, the spark gap would require a radiator upgrade to about 1.6× (1.4–2.9×) the current capacity, which is hard to justify for the dozen or so days a year at such conditions. Conversely, the current radiators would suffice even at design conditions if the spark gap fell to roughly 3.5, lower than in any of the nine quarters but not unusual in other European countries.

Static Analysis and Heat Loss

The starting point of this analysis is the state of our property after the efforts detailed in Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses. To work out a representative heat loss of the building, I looked at a cold winter month in Cambridge, UK: January 2026, with an average outdoor temperature of To = 5 ∘C. During that month the indoor temperature averaged Ti = 19 ∘C (about 20 °C downstairs and 18 °C upstairs; since 2022 we have kept the house a little cooler), giving a temperature difference of ΔTio = Ti − To = 14 K.

Energy consumption in January 2026 was as follows: electricity 263.75 kWh (8.5 kWh/day on average or 0.35 kW continuous power), and gas 1,924.91 kWh (62.1 kWh/day on average or 2.59 kW continuous power); the latter includes both space heating and domestic hot water (DHW). I assume that the energy for DHW stays much the same throughout the year and alone accounts for the gas used in summer, which in summer 2025 averaged about 12.5 kWh per day. In addition, we use a log burner as a supplemental heat source, providing about 3 kW for 3 hours a day, i.e. 9 kWh/day of heat (0.375 kW average power). Over the heating season we burn about 1 m3 of locally sourced hardwood costing £150, i.e. about £1/day.

After subtracting the estimated DHW portion (12.5 kWh/day) from total gas consumption (62.1 kWh/day), the remaining 49.6 kWh/day of gas provides 47.12 kWh/day of actual heat to the radiators at the 95% efficiency of our Viessmann Vitodens 222-F condensing boiler, i.e. 1.96 kW average power. Electricity consumed by appliances, eventually dissipated as heat, adds about 0.3 kW. Altogether, the estimated total average heating power is 2.635 kW (radiators 1.96 kW + log burner 0.375 kW + appliances 0.3 kW). This heating power compensates for the heat loss of the house to maintain a constant temperature, Q = hΔTio, where h is the Heat Transfer Coefficient. Using the actual heating power, h = Q/ΔTio = 2.635 kW/14 K = 188 W/K.

For comparison, the first-principles U-value calculations behind Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses give h = 196 W/K when applied to our house, including the extension; this corresponds to a theoretical heat loss of Ql = 196 × 14 = 2.74 kW. The actual heating power (2.635 kW) is about 96% of the theoretical heat loss. Both estimates carry some uncertainty, however, and the factors involved pull in opposite directions. The U-value calculation does not model ventilation and air infiltration, which would increase the heat loss. Unaccounted solar gain and heat from occupants, the fact that not all rooms are kept at 19 °C continuously (the heating is off from 10pm to 6am, and the entrance hall is more like 15 °C), and the thick curtains we use for the main entrance, French doors and windows all reduce the heating actually needed. The contribution of the log burner, on the other hand, adds to the heat supplied and is itself only an estimate, based on about three hours of use a day. The close agreement is therefore reassuring rather than exact. For the remainder of this analysis, I use the empirical value of 188 W/K (on the safe side).

Stationary Model of a House

The model used here is a simple lumped-mass representation of the house with indoor temperature Ti and outdoor temperature To; heat is supplied as Qh at flow temperature Tf and return temperature Tr and transferred to the internal thermal mass through the radiators as Qr; supplemental heat Qb comes from the log burner and Qa from appliances. The house loses heat as Ql through the building fabric with Heat Transfer Coefficient h.

Empirical Radiator Validation

The simplified stationary model treats all radiators as a single unit, assuming uniform flow and return temperatures through a well-balanced system. The characteristic constant K represents the combined heat transfer capacity of all radiators, of all surfaces and types, plus, in principle, the contribution of the pipework distributed throughout the house. My radiator survey, which sums the manufacturer specifications of our radiators but not the pipework, gives K = 71.2 W/K1.2, see Radiator Survey [3].

Using the radiator equation Qr = K ΔTm1.2 and Qr = 1.96 kW gives 1,960 W = 71.2 W/K1.2 × ΔTm1.2, i.e. a required ΔTm of 15.8 K, the difference between the mean radiator temperature and the indoor temperature of 19 °C; the mean radiator temperature in turn depends on the flow rate and flow temperature. Assuming about 10 l/min, this gives a mean radiator temperature of about 35 °C (flow ~36 °C, return ~34 °C). These numbers are consistent with the temperature felt when touching the radiators during the day: they are warm, around body temperature, but never more than 40–45 °C.

Operating Constraints

With the radiator constant thus validated (K = 71.2 W/K1.2), the relationship between flow rate and flow temperature for a given heating power can be explored. With water as the working fluid (ρ = 1 kg/l, cp = 4.18 kJ/(kg K)), the January 2026 average conditions (To = 5 ∘C, Ti = 19 ∘C) and a radiator exponent n = 1.2, many combinations of flow temperature Tf and flow rate V f deliver the same heating power, shown as curves of constant power in Figure 2. In other words, a heating controller can modulate either the flow temperature Tf or the flow rate V f to achieve the same effect (if not sabotaged by Thermostatic Radiator Valves, TRVs).

Figure 2. Constant heating power curves for the current radiators (K = 71.2 W/K1.2); the 1.96 kW average load is highlighted in red.

The red curve shows how 1.96 kW can be delivered with the current radiators: low-flow operation (~1 l/min) requires 49 °C flow temperature with a large temperature drop to the return (ΔTfr ≈ 28 K), whilst high-flow operation (~20 l/min) requires only 36 °C flow temperature with a small temperature drop to the return (ΔTfr ≈ 1.4 K). At both extremes the mean radiator temperature is roughly the same (~35 °C, determined by K and the heating load), but the higher flow rate achieves the target power with a much lower flow temperature, which is what matters for the heat pump COP.

The Impact of the Radiator Constant

The contour plot also shows that, for a given radiator constant K and flow temperature Tf, the delivered heating power converges towards a limit with increasing flow rate V f: there is a maximum power that existing radiators can deliver at a given flow temperature, reached when the difference between flow and return temperature goes to zero as the flow rate goes to infinity. Pumps struggle to approach that point, since hydraulic resistance rises with flow and pumping power is limited; in practice, the flow rate is limited to about 20 l/min. The heat delivered then becomes a function of the flow temperature only, and so does the COP.

Performance over Power

The break-even COP of 3.86 (3.66–4.44) is used throughout the analysis. Below that threshold, each unit of heat costs more to produce electrically than with gas. It is useful to show both the required flow temperature Tf and the achievable COP (at a given outdoor temperature To) as a function of the heat delivered with our current radiators (K = 71.2 W/K1.2). Note that the heat pump condenser needs to run at about 5 °C above the radiator flow temperature, since heat has to be transferred from the refrigerant circuit to the radiator circuit; this is included in the COP calculation (see COP estimation [4]). The three scenarios below illustrate the situation.

Figure 3. Flow temperature (left axis, blue) and COP (right axis, green; solid for To = 5 ∘C, dashed for To = −2 ∘C) plotted against heating power at K = 71.2 W/K1.2. The red dashed line marks the break-even COP of 3.86 in the central case, the shaded band its range of 3.66–4.44.

In all three scenarios the heat pump is assumed to cover DHW as well, allowing the gas connection to be fully decommissioned and the standing charge of 31.93 p/day (£0.32/day; £0.30–0.35) to be saved.

Scenario 1: Heat Pump with Log Burner

If the gas boiler is replaced with a heat pump and the log burner is still used for supplemental heat, the heat balance remains unchanged, with the radiators delivering Qr = 1.96 kW, the log burner providing Qb = 0.375 kW, appliances contributing Qa = 0.3 kW, and a total of Ql = 2.635 kW. High-flow operation optimised for the heat pump at 20 l/min requires a flow temperature of 36 °C with return temperature ~34 °C (ΔTfr ≈ 1.4 K), delivering 1.96 kW heating power. The estimated COP is 4.85 (at To = 5 ∘C, Tf = 36 ∘C), requiring an electrical input of ~0.40 kW, i.e. a daily electricity consumption of 9.7 kWh at a daily cost of £2.49 (£2.68–2.77). Adding the log burner wood cost of £1.00/day gives a total daily cost of £3.49 (£3.68–3.77).

For comparison, with Qr = 1.96 kW, the current gas-plus-log-burner baseline is: gas space heating £3.14/day + gas standing charge £0.32/day + log burner £1.00/day = £4.46/day (£4.29–4.97). The heat pump is £0.96/day (£0.60–1.20) cheaper, approximately 22% (14–24%) less expensive. The COP of 4.85 exceeds the break-even COP of 3.86 (3.66–4.44) by a wide margin, so the heat pump has the advantage on unit energy costs alone, even before the gas standing charge saving.

Scenario 2: Heat Pump without Log Burner

Without the log burner, the heat pump must meet the full heating requirement. With Qb = 0, the heat balance gives Qr = Ql − Qa = 2.635 − 0.3 = 2.335 kW required from the heat pump. Heat pump operation at maximum flow (20 l/min) requires a flow temperature of 38 °C with a return temperature of ~37 °C (ΔTfr ≈ 1.7 K), delivering 2.335 kW heating power. The estimated COP is 4.56 (at To = 5 ∘C, Tf = 38 ∘C), requiring an electrical input of ~0.51 kW, i.e. a daily electricity consumption of 12.3 kWh at a daily cost of £3.16 (£3.40–3.52).

The heat pump now covers what was previously provided by both the gas boiler and the log burner, so the baseline is the same as in Scenario 1: £4.46/day (£4.29–4.97). The heat pump is £1.29/day (£0.88–1.45) cheaper, about 29% (21–29%) less expensive. The COP of 4.56 is above the break-even COP of 3.86 (3.66–4.44), so the heat pump is cheaper on unit energy costs alone across the whole range: any spark gap up to 4.56/0.95 ≈ 4.80 would do. Taking into account the standing charge saving and the wood no longer bought (£1.00/day), the effective break-even COP falls to 3.23 (3.23–3.62).

Scenario 3: Heat Pump at Design Temperature

For planning and sizing heat pump capacity, it is standard practice to consider operation at the design outdoor temperature, typically −2 °C for Cambridge, UK. At this design condition the heat loss through the building fabric increases: with h = 188 W/K and ΔTio = 19 ∘C − (−2 ∘C) = 21 K, the total heat loss becomes Ql = 188 × 21 = 3.948 kW. Subtracting appliance heat gains of Qa = 0.3 kW, the radiators must deliver Qr = 3.65 kW (assuming no log burner at design conditions for conservative sizing).

At maximum flow (20 l/min), delivering 3.65 kW requires a flow temperature of ~47 °C with a return temperature of ~44 °C (ΔTfr ≈ 2.6 K). However, the COP at design temperature is reduced by the colder outdoor air: at To = −2 ∘C and Tf = 47 ∘C, the estimated COP is 3.32, requiring an electrical input of ~1.10 kW, i.e. a daily electricity consumption of 26.4 kWh at a daily cost of £6.80 (£7.31–7.56).

For comparison, the gas boiler at design conditions would need 3.65 kW / 0.95 = 3.84 kW of gas input, costing 3.84 × 24 × 0.0634 ≈ £5.85/day for space heating; adding the gas standing charge of £0.32/day gives a total of £6.17/day (£5.82–7.14). The heat pump is about £0.63/day (£0.42–1.49) more expensive, a 10% (6–26%) cost premium. The COP of 3.32 sits below the break-even COP of 3.86 (3.66–4.44) across the whole range; conversely, the spark gap would need to be below 3.32/0.95 ≈ 3.49 for the heat pump to be economically viable on unit costs alone.

Adjusting the Radiator Constant

Figure 4 shows flow temperature and COP as a function of radiator constant K for two heating loads at To = 5 ∘C: 1.96 kW with log burner (S1) and 2.34 kW without (S2); Figure 5 shows the same for the design temperature condition (S3). The required K is found where the COP curve crosses the break-even threshold of 3.86 (3.66–4.44).

Figure 4. Flow temperature (left axis, blue) and COP (right axis, green) plotted against radiator constant K for S1 (solid, 1.96 kW) and S2 (dashed, 2.34 kW) at To = 5 ∘C. Circles mark the break-even COP of 3.86 in the central case; the shaded band shows its range of 3.66–4.44.

With the current K = 71.2, Scenario 1 (1.96 kW with log burner) comfortably exceeds the required COP of 3.86, achieving COP = 4.85. Because that COP threshold is set by the spark gap and the boiler efficiency alone, all break-even points for scenarios at To = 5 ∘C converge to the same flow temperature of 46.3 °C (39.4–49.1 °C) regardless of heating load; reaching that point requires progressively more radiator capacity as the heating load increases. For the 1.96 kW scenario with log burner, K ≈ 38 W/K1.2 (34–55), or 0.54× (0.48–0.77×) the current value, would suffice. For the 2.34 kW scenario without log burner, K ≈ 46 W/K1.2 (41–66), or 0.64× (0.57–0.93×) the current value, is required on unit energy costs alone, so the current radiators are sufficient in both cases, with a wide margin in the central case that narrows to about 7% at the highest spark gap.

Scenario 3 at design temperature is a different matter. For the 3.65 kW design temperature condition at To = −2 ∘C, the break-even COP of 3.86 requires a flow temperature of 38.1 °C (31.4–40.9 °C); with colder outdoor air, the flow temperature must be lower to keep the temperature lift, and thus the COP, the same. Delivering 3.65 kW at 38.1 °C means a mean radiator temperature of 36.8 °C, about 18 K above indoor temperature. The required K is approximately 115 W/K1.2 (97–204), which is 1.6× (1.4–2.9×) the current value; such an upgrade is hard to justify economically for conditions that occur on only a dozen or so days a year.

Figure 5. Flow temperature (left axis, blue) and COP (right axis, green; solid for To = 5 ∘C, dashed for To = −2 ∘C) plotted against radiator constant K for S3 (3.65 kW at To = −2 ∘C). The break-even marker sits at K ≈ 115 (1.6× current) for the central case, the shaded band spanning 1.4–2.9×.

Conclusion

Including the gas standing charge saving (and, in Scenario 2, the wood no longer bought), the heat pump costs 22–29% less to run than the gas-plus-log-burner baseline at typical heating conditions (Scenarios 1 and 2) in the central case, and 14–29% less over the range of spark gaps. At design temperature (Scenario 3: −2 °C), it costs 10% (6–26%) more per day (£6.80 vs £6.17 in the central case); such conditions occur in Cambridge on about a dozen days a year, so the seasonal balance remains in favour of the heat pump.

Whether a radiator upgrade is required in each scenario is a direct consequence of the spark gap: it sets the break-even COP, which sets the maximum permissible flow temperature, which in turn determines the minimum radiator capacity. Across the whole range of the past nine quarters, at typical winter conditions the current radiators are sufficient and the heat pump is cheaper on unit energy costs alone, with or without the log burner; at design conditions it is dearer, because the spark gap is too large for the COP achievable in cold weather, not because of any inherent limitation of heat pump technology. The upgrade needed to break even there, 1.6× (1.4–2.9×) the current capacity, comes at its lower end close to what replacing radiators with higher-output models can achieve (the replacement considered in Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Space Heating and Domestic Hot Water reaches 1.3×); the upper end would be out of the question.

Should energy pricing change, for example through a move away from merit-order marginal pricing, a shift of energy transition costs from electricity to gas, or increased renewable generation, the spark gap would fall and the required upgrades would become less onerous. A spark gap below roughly 3.5 would bring the break-even K for design conditions within the current radiator capacity.
