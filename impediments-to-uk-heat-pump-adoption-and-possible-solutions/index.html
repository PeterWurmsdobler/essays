Impediments to UK Heat Pump Adoption and Possible Solutions

Capital cost, space and the spark gap stand in the way of heat pump adoption in British homes; compact, appliance-like systems could remove the first two.

Considerations for the Fabric-First vs Heat-Pump-First Debate concludes that a balanced ‘Sensible Insulation, then Heat Pump’ approach is optimal for a house like ours. Yet there are significant impediments to the adoption of air-source heat pumps in British homes: capital expenditure, space requirements and the ‘spark gap’. The last of these, the ratio of the unit price of electricity to that of gas, sets the Coefficient of Performance (COP) a heat pump must reach to be cheaper to run than a gas boiler, a COP not always reached on the coldest days (see the postscript to Convincing Numbers to Install a Heat Pump for Domestic Heating).

The first two, the focus of this essay, concern the high upfront cost of a heat pump installation, owing to oversized heat pumps, a complex system design with many discrete components and the related plumbing work, and the space these components require. My conclusion is that a heat pump installation will have to be on a par in complexity and cost with a gas boiler installation to become acceptable to the majority of homeowners, not only to early adopters prepared to put up with higher costs and hassle.

Historic Context in the UK

The standard central heating system in the UK consists of the following components: a ‘system’ boiler, at times installed into the builder’s opening of the chimney breast as a ‘back boiler’, a vented hot water tank in an ‘airing cupboard’ (often found on the first floor), complex pipework for this vented system, i.e. a heating system header tank and a cold water storage tank in the loft (gravity-fed low-pressure system feeding both cold and hot water taps), single-panel radiators (fed by pipes of varying diameters: 8 mm, 10 mm or 15 mm) and other paraphernalia distributed over the house. The power rating of such a boiler is about 10–15 kW, because the hot water tank acts as a thermal buffer. Also, people used to take mostly baths, and running a bath does not need high pressure. Those who wanted showers later installed a power shower to boost the water pressure from the vented hot water tank; some still use an electric shower connected to the cold water mains.

Then came the transition to the high-efficiency combi(nation) boiler, which runs the central heating (with an integrated circulation pump) and produces domestic hot water (DHW) on demand: mains water is heated directly through a heat exchanger by burning gas. This boiler could simply be installed instead of the system boiler, and it made the header tanks as well as the hot water tank superfluous. Its power rating was and is determined by the larger of the space heating and the DHW demand, and it is usually the latter that decides: flow rate × ΔT × specific heat capacity of water. A 10 l/min flow (a bucket a minute) and heating water from 5 °C to 40 °C (ΔT = 35 K) yields 0.167 kg/s × 35 K × 4.18 kJ/(kg K) = 24.4 kW, a substantial power owing to water’s high specific heat capacity.

Nowadays a range of installations can be found in UK homes: by my rough estimate, about 10% use the traditional system, 30% use a modern condensing system boiler with a hot water cylinder, and 60% use a condensing combi boiler. When a combi boiler was installed, the radiators may have been updated at the same time, or the pipes to the radiators upgraded to 15 mm. Quite often the space once taken by the hot water tank has since been repurposed. If one now wanted to move to a heat pump and keep the habit of taking showers (with their implicit power demand), one would need either a heat pump with an industrial rating of 25 kW or more, or to bring back the hot water tank. One thing is certain: UK homes are small compared with continental ones and in general have neither a basement (at least in Cambridge) nor a plant room. So where does one put all the equipment?

Costs of a Real Installation

An internet search for heat pumps will generally show the external unit with its big fan. However, the capital expenditure for a heat pump installation extends well beyond the outdoor unit featured in marketing materials, or costed in Considerations for the Fabric-First vs Heat-Pump-First Debate. While the outdoor unit itself costs £3,000–£8,000 depending on capacity, a complete installation requires numerous additional components, extensive plumbing work and substantial labour. The total cost in the UK typically ranges from £15,000 to £25,000 before the £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme (BUS) grant. Core Heat Pump Components

The external unit, the one with the big fan, contains the compressor, the expansion valve, the evaporator and the condenser. To go with it is usually some kind of control unit, which may be integrated into the external unit or installed separately indoors. The costs of these core components are:

  • Outdoor unit (air-source heat pump): £3,000–£8,000
  • Indoor unit/controls: £500–£1,500

Hot Water System

While a common combination boiler produces hot water on demand (at say 25 kW), a heat pump installation (at say 5 kW) therefore requires a hot water cylinder to store the heated water. The costs of these components are:

  • Hot water cylinder (insulated thermal store, 100–300 l): £800–£2,000
  • Primary expansion vessel (heating circuit): £150–£300
  • Secondary expansion vessel (DHW circuit, if separate): £100–£200

Hydraulic Components

Additional components are needed to circulate the water through the radiators and/or underfloor heating, and to switch between heating and DHW modes. The costs of these components are:

  • Circulation pump(s) for radiator circuit: £200–£400
  • Diverter valves (3-way or 4-way): £150–£400
  • Buffer tank (optional, for system stability): £400–£800
  • Pressure relief valves: £50–£100
  • Fill and drain valves: £30–£80

Pipework and Insulation

At times micro-bore pipework is used, without insulation, but more often the existing pipework is upgraded to 15 mm diameter. The costs of these components are:

  • Pipe upgrades to 15 mm diameter (possibly): £500–£1,500
  • Refrigerant lines (split systems): £300–£600
  • Pipe insulation (lagging): £200–£500
  • Condensate drain: £100–£200

Controls and Sensors

For all components to operate in harmony, a control system is required, with sensors to monitor temperatures and flow rates. The costs of these components are:

  • Smart thermostat/controller: £200–£500
  • Flow and return sensors: £100–£300
  • Outdoor temperature sensor: £50–£100
  • DHW cylinder sensors: £50–£150

Electrical Work

A standard consumer unit would need to be upgraded to accommodate the higher electrical load of a heat pump, and dedicated circuits may be required. The costs of these components are:

  • Dedicated circuit upgrades (typically 32 A): £300–£800
  • Wiring and consumer unit modifications: £200–£500

Labour and Installation

Due to the complexity of the installation, multiple tradespeople are often required, including plumbers, electricians and heating engineers. The costs of these services are:

  • Design, heat loss calculation and grant application: £300–£800
  • Installation labour (4–5 days, multiple tradespeople): £3,000–£8,000
  • Building control notifications: £100–£300
  • Commissioning and testing: £300–£600

All added up, the total typical cost is £15,000–£25,000 (before the BUS grant).¹ This complexity, and the cost it entails, is a significant impediment to adoption. Most households (roughly 60% by the estimate above) are accustomed to combi boilers occupying minimal space. Installers often propose elaborate systems with high labour costs that remain expensive even after the grant, and the householder has to give up internal space to accommodate them.

Commoditisation of Heat Pumps

Modern combi boilers replaced complex multi-component heating systems with single integrated appliances, reducing both installation time and costs. Heat pumps could follow a similar path through standardisation and component integration. A standardised heat pump system would require five basic connections:

  • Mains water input (for DHW),
  • Mains electricity input with power supply to external unit,
  • Flow/return connections to heating system (radiators and/or underfloor),
  • Flow/return connections to external unit (mono-bloc configuration),
  • Hot water output (DHW).

Examples of Integrated Systems

Several manufacturers have developed systems approaching this integration, albeit split between an external unit (the heat pump proper) and an internal unit (with the hot water tank):

  • Viessmann Vitocal 151-A: Indoor 60 cm × 60 cm × 190 cm unit, similar in footprint to our Vitodens 222-F gas boiler,
  • Bosch Compress 6800i AW MB: New-generation heat pump with 60 cm × 60 cm indoor footprint, not available in the UK at the time of writing,
  • Vaillant aroTHERM Plus: 7 kW heat pump with 190 l uniTOWER cylinder, 60 cm × 70 cm footprint.

Going one step further, NUSKU is developing a system that integrates the heat pump proper, a hot water tank and everything else into a single outdoor unit with only mains water in, hot water out, radiator flow and return, and mains power [1].

Potential Benefits of Integration

Integrated system design could reduce installation complexity through:

  • Factory assembly and testing of hydraulic components at scale,
  • Standard appliance footprint dimensions (approximately 60 cm × 60 cm),
  • Height ranging from 100 cm to 200 cm depending on hot water tank capacity,
  • Pre-plumbed configurations reducing copper work and on-site labour,
  • Modular designs for different DHW capacities.

This approach could potentially reduce:

  • Installation time from 4–5 days to 1–2 days,
  • Labour costs through fewer trades required,
  • Component costs through volume manufacturing,
  • System complexity with fewer connection points.

Compared with current installation costs of £15,000–£25,000, integrated appliances and simplified installation procedures might bring the total cost down to £7,000–£11,000 including installation, although this assumes manufacturing economies of scale comparable to those of white goods.

Radiator Compatibility and Control Systems

Radiator replacement is a significant cost in heat pump installations. Heat pumps operate at lower flow temperatures (35–45 °C) than gas boilers (60–80 °C), and installers commonly recommend upgrading to larger, double-panel radiators to emit the same amount of heat. More sophisticated control systems, however, offer an alternative that may reduce or eliminate this requirement.

Control System Challenges

Traditional Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs) present operational challenges with heat pumps. TRVs were designed for high-temperature gas boiler systems with bypass circuits. When a TRV closes to limit room temperature, it restricts flow, which conflicts with the heat pump’s requirement for constant high-volume, low-temperature circulation. The central heat pump control and the TRVs can then work against each other, causing oscillations and poor performance.

For properties with consistent occupancy and uniform heating requirements, a properly designed system with correctly sized and balanced radiators can be modulated centrally through a common flow rate and flow temperature, with all zones responding uniformly. For properties with variable occupancy patterns or different heating needs per zone (living room at 21 °C, bedroom at 17 °C), however, distributed control, room by room, has more to offer. Modern control systems address these challenges through:

  • MIMO control (Multiple-Input Multiple-Output) treating the home as an interconnected thermal environment,
  • Predictive algorithms incorporating weather forecasts and occupancy patterns,
  • Zone management allowing different temperature profiles without flow restriction,
  • Weather compensation adjusting flow temperature based on outdoor conditions.

The Adia Hub Approach

The Adia Hub system from Adia Thermal [2] is one example of managing existing radiators through control rather than hardware replacement. The system comprises:

  • Smart thermostatic valves on each radiator,
  • Room temperature sensors throughout the home,
  • Central controller implementing optimisation algorithms,
  • Heat pump integration via Modbus/TCP communication.

This configuration enables the system to maximise the flow by fully opening the valves when heat is needed, operate at lower temperatures with extended run times, balance heat delivery dynamically across rooms, and optimise energy consumption based on variable electricity pricing. Benefits reported by the company include:

  • Installation time reduced from 5 days to 2 days,
  • Elimination of most radiator replacements (saving £2,000–£5,000),
  • Minimal additional components beyond DHW cylinder and expansion vessel,
  • Compatibility with multiple heat pump brands (Ideal, Haier, Samsung, Vaillant, Midea, Bosch Compress 2000).

Extending this to additional brands, or integrating similar control logic directly into manufacturers’ systems, could broaden adoption. Traditional suppliers are experienced in mass-manufacturing heat pumps and components but less so in modern software development, which leaves room for collaboration or licensing.

Integrated System Configuration

An integrated heat pump system suitable for UK residential properties would combine hardware simplicity with intelligent control:

Outdoor Unit

The core unit would provide the following features:

  • Mono-bloc air-source heat pump with all refrigerant components contained,
  • Weatherproof construction with acoustic output below 45 dB,
  • Capacity matched to building requirements (typically 3–10 kW).

Indoor Unit

With a footprint of at most 60 cm × 60 cm and a height of up to 2 m, the indoor unit would house:

  • Integrated insulated hot water cylinder (100–300 l),
  • Plate heat exchanger for instantaneous DHW from mains,
  • Circulation pump for heating circuit,
  • Expansion vessels for heating and DHW circuits,
  • Diverter valves for heating/DHW priority management,
  • Pre-assembled and tested hydraulic components,
  • Integrated controls and communication interfaces.

The indoor unit could be made considerably smaller by replacing the integrated hot water tank with a heat store using phase change material, such as the Sunamp Thermino, with two benefits:

  • The store needs about a quarter of the footprint of the water tank, which shrinks the indoor unit to the size of a kitchen unit,
  • No hot water is stored in the indoor unit: water is heated on demand through a high-power heat exchanger (no legionella risk).

Standard Connections

A standardised integrated system would require only four connections to the house:

  • Single electrical connection to the indoor unit, also powering the outdoor unit,
  • Mains cold water input and hot water output (DHW),
  • Connection to outdoor unit (2 insulated pipes),
  • Heating flow and return (2 pipes).

This configuration would occupy about the same space as a floor-standing combi boiler or a washing machine, making it compatible with typical UK homes without basements or plant rooms.

Control Options

Once the hardware has been commoditised by companies experienced in making white goods, what distinguishes one product from another would lie mostly in the control system, which third parties could offer as software only, through cloud services, for example Havenwise [3] or Adia Thermal [2]:

Integrated Smart Controller

An integrated smart controller would provide the following features:

  • Weather compensation,
  • Time-of-use tariff optimisation,
  • DHW scheduling with legionella protection,
  • Self-learning algorithms for occupancy patterns,
  • Remote monitoring and control capability.

Smart Zone Management

Moving away from traditional TRVs and balanced flows, a smart zone management system would allow for individual room temperature control without flow restriction:

  • Heat pump integration via standard protocols (Modbus/TCP),
  • Wireless smart radiator valves in all rooms,
  • Hub controller for MIMO optimisation,
  • Multi-zone temperature profiles,
  • Room temperature sensors,
  • Dynamic flow balancing.

Installation and Economics

Installation could then take two days: removal of the existing boiler, positioning of the indoor and outdoor units and pipe connections on day one; electrical work, commissioning and control installation on day two. The estimated costs of this approach are:

  • Equipment: £5,000–£8,000 (assuming mass production at white goods scale),
  • Installation: £2,000–£3,000 (simplified 2-day procedure),
  • Total: £7,000–£11,000, after £7,500 BUS grant: £0–£3,500 net cost.

These figures assume manufacturing economies of scale and simplified installation procedures. Achieving them would require coordination among manufacturers for integration and scaling up production, standards bodies for defining communication protocols, policy makers for appropriate incentives, and installers to adopt simpler procedures. If that can be achieved, the capital cost and space impediments to the adoption of heat pumps in the UK would be largely removed, and the market could be expected to grow.
