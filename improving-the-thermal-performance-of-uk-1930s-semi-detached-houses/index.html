Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses

Sharing the experience of improving the thermal performance of a UK 1930s semi-detached house and suggestions for what could be done beyond that.

When my family and I moved into a 1930s semi-detached property, we made a few improvements to the house. One aspect was addressing the thermal performance, as the building appeared to be poorly insulated and felt cold. In this essay I would like to share what we have learnt and done, as well as what would be possible beyond that. Given that there are quite a few of these houses in the UK, I hope that our experience will be useful to others, with some measures even transferable to other property types. In this way, all of us may contribute towards combating climate change by reducing the energy needed for domestic space heating, mostly through insulation, or perhaps a heat pump.

1930s Semi Assessment

According to the 1930s House Manual [1], ‘The 1930s witnessed nothing less than the biggest building boom ever seen in the UK (before and since), with around 300,000 new houses being built every year by the middle of the decade, …’. These 1930s houses come in various shapes and styles, most in the easily recognisable mock-Tudor style, some in the style of the Continental Modern Movement, and a few with features of the latter in the former; one can find some detached houses, but more often whole roads lined with semi-detached pairs on either side. I reckon there may be up to 3 million dwellings of that kind in the UK, all with a very similar layout, built from the late 1920s and going well into the 1940s.

The 1930s Semi Blueprint

The term blueprint truly deserves its name in British housing. There seem to have been only a couple of designs for 1930s semis that were rolled out across the whole country, with just a few variants such as whether the entrance halls are in the middle, next to the party wall, or towards the lateral walls. In addition, one can find variations of features such as the shapes of bay windows with either flat or pitched roofs. Overall, the layout is very practical for a small family, with entrance hall, front room, dining room, kitchen, two large bedrooms, a small spare bedroom and a bathroom as shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Floor plan of a typical 1930s semi-detached house; the external walls are solid using imperial bricks, about 228 mm × 110 mm × 65 mm in size, hence these walls are about 228 mm + 20 mm (internal plaster) thick; the window bay on the first floor uses bricks standing on their sides, i.e. its walls are 20 mm (internal plaster) + 65 mm + 20 mm (pebble dash) thick; the entrance door and frame are wooden and the side of the entrance porch uses only a single brick, i.e. 20 mm (internal plaster) + 110 mm + 20 mm (external render) thick; all internal walls are rendered using lime plaster, about 20 mm.

Thermal Performance of Surfaces

Any property will lose energy by transferring heat through its external walls and surfaces as well as through uncontrolled air exchange, e.g. draughts through doors and windows. The overall thermal performance of the house is dominated by its ‘weakest links’, i.e. the surfaces that combine a high conductivity with a large area. The first stage in working out a baseline thermal performance is therefore some thermal modelling and analysis of the entire house, based on an understanding of the types of surfaces, their area and conductivity [2], [3]. For this purpose, in addition to the floor plan, I am using the two vertical cross sections shown in Figure 3.

Improvements Made

Our aim was to preserve the look of the building as much as possible; for example, the brick walls and pebble dash remain as they are for now (the pebble dash was later replaced, as described in Literally Rendering a 1930s House Thermally More Efficient).

Loft Insulation

The building regulations nowadays stipulate 250 mm rock wool for loft insulation [4], [5]. However, most people like some kind of storage area in the middle part of the loft where it is possible to stand up. Also, considerable heat loss occurs through the slanted part of the ceiling, going into the eaves, where there is little vertical space. Therefore, I went for the solution shown in Figure 4.

Figure 4. Section through the loft: 50 mm Knauf rock wool slabs between the rafters, 100 mm between the joists, a boarded platform on 150 mm joists with 150 mm in between, and a further 200 mm above the original joists on either side of the platform.

After having removed everything from the loft (including the thin layer of old and dusty insulation between the joists), I cleaned and hoovered between the joists to create a clean space. Then the following steps were carried out:

1.
Use 50 mm Knauf rock wool slabs, cut into appropriate pieces and slid between rafters in order to add insulation on the sloped ceiling part of the upstairs bedrooms. This leaves some space above the rock wool to breathe.
2.
Install 100 mm rock wool between the 4 inch (100 mm) high joists.
3.
Build a platform in the middle of the loft using 40 mm × 150 mm timber on top of and perpendicular to the existing joists, because I had some timber left at the time; an alternative is to use a set of plastic stilts.
4.
Fill the gaps between the new 150 mm joists with 150 mm rock wool.
5.
Install loft boards on the 150 mm joists to create a new platform.
6.
On either side of the platform, use 200 mm rock wool on top of the 100 mm layer between the original joists, perpendicular to the joists.

Entrance Porch Glazing

We removed all single-glazed panes from the entrance porch and fitted double-glazed replacement units ordered from Go-Glass in Cambridge. This brought the U-value down from 4.8 to 2.8 W/(m2 K).

On either side of the front door in the entrance porch there is a very thin brick wall. Bay Window Insulation

While I was carrying out some work at the property before moving in, a neighbour drew my attention to the fact that the upstairs bay is very cold. I then realised that indeed bricks are used on their sides, making the wall as thick as a brick is high (65 mm). Therefore I immediately installed 100 mm rock wool insulation on the inside, before installing the radiator there. The resulting performance is down from the original 3.351 W/(m2 K) to 0.307 W/(m2 K), a factor of about 10, as computed in Table 15.

Table 15. U-value calculation for window bay with internal insulation.

Double Wooden Floor

In theory, it is possible to lift the floorboards and install some insulation between the joists (possibly blocks of 100 mm Celotex/Kingspan). I did lift some boards in order to clear out the rubble that had been left there and was creating moisture bridges, but refrained from installing insulation. Instead we simply put down new floorboards on top of the old floor, with a thin layer of insulation felt in between; the new boards were made from reclaimed Victorian timber sawn and planed into floorboards by a company in Yorkshire, Ecopine. This improved the insulation by a factor of 2, as computed in Table 16.

A-Rated Double Glazing

Even though the property already had double glazing on most windows and the side entrance, we had all windows and doors (except the front door) replaced with new A-rated uPVC equivalents at about 1.4 W/(m2 K).

Possible Further Improvements

With the ceilings insulated, the external walls now contribute 70% of the heat loss; the exposed solid brick walls at the rear and side alone account for more than half of the total, the side wall for nearly a third. What can be done?

Entrance Porch Side Insulation

The small side wall of the entrance porch can be given the same treatment as the bottom walls, with the same performance. This knocks another 100 W off the total heating power and avoids a cold corner, as accounted for in Table 18.

Table 18. Total heat loss for building after further improvement (in green).

Replacing the Pebble Dash

A further, if modest, gain in thermal performance could be achieved by replacing the pebble dash with insulating render. Removing the current pebble dash gives an allowance of approximately 20 mm; it should be possible to make the render a bit thicker, perhaps 25 mm, and over the window bay, say 30 mm.

A commercially available render is called ThermoPor and its thermal conductivity, around 0.050 W/(m K), is not far from that of rock wool (0.034 W/(m K)) [2]. This type of render does not change the appearance of the property too much, in particular if the pebble dash was already painted. The solid wall and window bay, both pebble-dashed, would obtain the performance computed in Tables 19 and 20.

Now the heat loss is down to 2,317 W for a very cold day, perhaps down to 1 kW for clement winter days. Consequently, the heat generated by appliances and people would almost be sufficient, inadvertently producing something close to a passive house. However, it would not look the same any more. Is it worth the effort?

Postscript

Following a discussion with Simon Chung [6] from Sow Space [7], an architecture and planning practice in Oxford, UK, I learnt that there is professional software for thermal building analysis, including but not limited to:

  • IES (Integrated Environmental Solutions) [8], a software and consultancy company that specialises in building performance analysis,
  • PHPP (Passivhaus Planning Package) [9] by the Passive House Institute [10], an independent research institute for energy efficiency in buildings,
  • Ecotect Analysis by Autodesk (since discontinued), an environmental analysis tool for simulating building performance from the early stages of conceptual design,
  • ArchiCAD [11], architectural BIM CAD software covering both aesthetic and engineering aspects throughout the design process, with its own built-in EcoDesigner,
  • WUFI [12], which performs dynamic simulations of coupled heat and moisture transfer and can be used for thermal bridge calculations,
  • Sefaira [13], cloud-based software for building performance analysis during design.
