In Tyrannos --- A Plea for a Free Software Market

Why so many computer users submit to the dictate of one software company, and how open interfaces would set them free.

Any half-way critical computer user will already have noticed that his dependence, as far as software is concerned, on a single company spanning the globe is more of a nuisance than a pleasure. This pamphlet examines that circumstance more closely by analysing the present situation, how such a phenomenon comes about, and where its causes lie. Finally, it points out alternatives.

A delight in uniformity and a penchant for perfectionism are traits often found in some people and, strangely enough, in fascist and totalitarian systems as well. They contradict, however, the heterogeneity of nature and the manifold needs of people. Nevertheless, it happens again and again in society that some structures attempt to create a uniform system, only to fail in the face of reality. Even a training officer in an army will have great difficulty producing a homogeneous mass of soldiers, owing to the differences between the recruits, since uniformity as such is unnatural.

It seems somewhat strange, then, when in the computer market, in an essentially democratic society, a kind of uniformity is sought voluntarily, namely through one software solution as a blessing for the whole world. Alarm bells ought to ring for anybody of a democratic disposition. Is too much power not concentrated in one person, violating an essential democratic principle? Why do so many computer users submit to the dictate of one company? How can it happen that the needs of computer users are made so uniform by one all-embracing piece of software?

Given the diversity of human needs, one can easily imagine that the tasks in the field of computer applications will be very diverse as well, and would call for special solutions. An all-in-one solution, however, can only cover all needs superficially and would have to be extremely complex for its intended purpose, so that in the end it is unsuitable for individual tasks. For these it then offers, to borrow from Karl Kraus, everything hitherto surpassed. If one sticks with it nonetheless, the result is hypertrophic and soon drags a lot of ballast along with it. In a natural, evolutionary environment, such a system would have to die out like a dinosaur.

The question a user faces is why such a product can prevail, and even hold its own in the market for a long time. Well, all computer users use one and the same product because everybody else does. And, with a queasy stomach, the computer user lightly submits to the dictate of one company through his resigned attitude: ‘What am I to do on my own if I want to stay compatible?’ The result of this faint-hearted thinking is positive market feedback with negative effects, since it increases the pressure for compatibility once more, and so the circle closes. Thus everything ordered from above is gone along with, driven by the myth of compatibility.

But what does one think one gains from this dearly bought compatibility? When a document or any other digital information is created on a particular system with particular software, one wishes it to be transferable to another. That is, information should be transportable across system boundaries. There would in principle be several ways of doing so, the trivial one, having only one system in use, being unrealistic given the way things develop.

The most common variant is that the maker of an all-embracing piece of software defines its own format and changes it from version to version and from system to system. To make the desired compatibility possible, there are then conversion programs. A document created in a certain version on a certain operating system must then be converted into a parallel system with a different version number of the same program, although the maker is the same. One gladly accepts this, since one wants to be compatible, and so follows the myth without reflection, for there can no longer be any talk of compatibility.

Another possibility is to use a format accepted by all software makers and based on international consensus, either internally or, above all, externally as an interface between different systems. Information can then be exported or imported through a powerful language whenever internal information needs to be taken outside. Every company could then develop its own software, or have it written, tailored to its individual needs. Compatibility thus takes place at an interface, not through a piece of software. And the interface has emerged from a democratic process. The argument that one has to use the dinosaur because everybody else has engaged it, too, then falls away.

The myth of compatibility, however, is surely not the only mainspring of a monopolising process, for underneath lies the unspoken wish for uniformity, i.e. for simplicity of mind. It is always easier to move in a homogeneous environment than to cope with a heterogeneous one. This means that convenience, a penchant for totalitarian systems and stupidity are surely factors in such economic processes with feedback, with which, in any case, good money can be made.

So what alternatives are there? Based on the approach of powerful interfaces, all it takes is the courage of a company to back alternative operating systems or programs, too. Saying that one could do nothing about it was, and always is, a convenient, faint-hearted excuse. For as long as there is a way to export digital data to a suitable interface and to import them from there, one need not fear incompatibility. For an internal flow of data, compatibility with the outside world is of no importance.
