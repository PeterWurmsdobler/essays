Integrated Media Player and Stereo Power Amplifier in 1980s Style

An essential link between digital music sources and high-quality speakers to obtain high-fidelity sound.

This is the story of Eumeles, a media streamer and stereo power amplifier in a single compact case, with a display in the style of 1980s hi-fi equipment, which I designed and built myself. The name derives from the Greek for ‘well-sounding’; perhaps a bit pretentious, but at least aspirational. It went through four stages, from a small off-the-shelf media PC to a device that connects streamed music to a pair of good speakers and nothing else. At the beginning it was perhaps ahead of the market, towards the end it was a bit behind, but it is still a nice device to listen to music with, and it is compact enough to fit into a small living room without taking up too much space.

Background

The reference for listening to music must and can only be attending a live performance, be it classical music, jazz, rock or pop. Failing that, in particular in times when we are supposed to stay at home due to a pandemic, some technology is needed to recreate the concert experience to an acceptable level of fidelity, aka high fidelity, from an available recording. Nowadays, the electronics in mobile phones and suitable headphones are probably good enough to render any decent recording with acceptable fidelity, even if it is most likely compressed and streamed. The listening experience, however, remains a solitary one, allowing people to live in their own bubble disconnected from the rest of the world.

In contrast, in the 1980s, at the beginning of digital recordings and the availability of CD players, quite some equipment was needed to render music at a reasonable level of fidelity: special turntables or over-sampling CD players, pre-amplifiers, separate power amplifiers, large speakers, special arrangements for the room acoustics, etc., you name it. I loved to go to so-called Hi-End trade shows where the most sophisticated audio equipment was presented and one could experience the difference good audio gear does make. There is, however, a danger of entering snake-oil territory, as quite a few features being touted are only present in the imagination of all people involved and have no technical or scientific substance.

That being said, is there still a demand for a listening experience in a quiet room? Not wearing headphones, but just sitting in a comfortable chair in front of a stereo system, appreciating a symphony, a piano concerto, a string quartet or any piece of music you are fond of? I believe there is; but what about the audio equipment needed, you might ask? Given the advance in electronics development and the availability of music in high definition as a streaming service, much less gear is needed these days. Apart from a quiet room with suitable acoustics and some quality time, a pair of decent speakers is still needed; the latter convert electrical signals and power into audible sound waves and are critical to any high fidelity conversion. The missing link is a simple device that connects the extensive music sources out there to those speakers, for those who love listening to music through proper speakers but embrace streaming and are happy to get rid of a tangible ‘medium’ such as CDs (the clue is in the word ‘medium’, which is only a means to convey music in bits).

Requirements

It all started with the birth of my son. Operating a conventional CD player to play a nursery CD while carrying a baby in my arms proved to be difficult, so I thought it would be nice to have all our music stored on a system that allowed easy playback with a remote control; the same system could also show pictures on the TV set. Before looking for anything to buy, I wrote down what such a system should do. My first list of requirements, written in 2010, was short: listen to internet radio (FIP, Ö1 and WGBH Boston) and to all our CDs stored somewhere, present classical music in a musical manner (e.g. by epoch, composer, work, performer and part) rather than by artist, album and song, show lyrics and pictures, keep the digital content free of any vendor’s software, and all of it in as few devices as possible, compact and non-intrusive.

The market offered audio streamers, multimedia players, jukeboxes and home theatre PCs (HTPCs), but none of them met all the requirements: they either needed further boxes for pictures and films, or locked the content into their own way of storing it, and none allowed the user interface to be changed, e.g. to browse classical music by composer and work. A combination of several such boxes would only have added remote controls to a sideboard with room for few devices; even a standard amplifier would have been too large.

First Stage

The first stage was a compromise: a small HTPC, an ASRock ION 330 (Figure 2, left), running the open-source XBMC media software under GNU/Linux and connected to our HDTV, which also played the sound. I added some simple code to show the lyrics stored next to a music file, and for a few months we enjoyed music with lyrics on screen, DVDs and our pictures. However, the box was noisy (26 dB, still audible on a calm evening), it took too long to boot, and the TV had to be on to listen to music. In the end, the CDs stayed stacked next to the small Teac hi-fi, because it was simply easier and faster to pop one in and press play.

Figure 2. Left: an ASRock ION 330 like the one of the first stage (here the model with a Blu-ray drive). Right: the works-like prototype in its Antec Veris case, rough and ready [Image-1].

Works-Like Prototype

Since I was unhappy with the (in)convenience and performance of the first system, I moved on to the second stage, a works-like prototype for an integrated system (Figure 2, right): a media player on a Zotac ION board in an Antec Veris case with a two-line LCD and a remote control, a server with a hard disk and a TV card, and a simple fixed-gain stereo amplifier made from an off-the-shelf amplifier module and toroidal transformers, as volume and tone control happen in the player. It taught me a few lessons: the player needs a fanless thermal design (heat pipes), the player should have a four-line vacuum fluorescent display (VFD) rather than a bright two-line LCD, and the player should switch the amplifier on and off.

The Mark I Split System

The third stage, the Mark I, put these lessons into a design for manufacture: a split system of two cases of the same size, 244 mm × 76 mm × 264 mm, the media player stacked on top of the stereo power amplifier (Figure 3). I designed the cases myself and had them made by Rowley Engineering in Cambridge; I first built everything in raw aluminium, then took it apart again, had the cases black anodised and put it all back together. One feature I insisted on, reminiscent of the 1980s, was the display: performer, composer, piece, movement and elapsed time on a four-line VFD with a font big enough that I can read the text from 10 feet (3 metres) away while sitting in a comfortable chair and listening. There is no point in a high-resolution display, as one would not sit half a metre away from it while listening.

Figure 3. The Mark I, front and side views, with the media player on top of the stereo power amplifier.

Figure 4 shows both cases opened. The media player held a Zotac IONITX board (an Intel Atom 230 with an NVIDIA MCP7A-ION chipset), cooled passively by heat pipes, together with a small SSD, a mains power supply and the VFD; it ran XBMC under GNU/Linux. The amplifier held two toroidal transformers and two ILP HY2001 amplifier modules, each rated at 30 W (40 W RMS at most). As in the prototype, it had a fixed gain, and the player switched it on and off over USB.

Figure 4. The Mark I opened. Left: the media player from above, with the heat pipes and the VFD. Right: the stereo power amplifier from above, with its two toroidal transformers and amplifier modules.

With the Mark I complete, I found a manufacturer of audio equipment in Italy and travelled there at my own expense to present the concept and to look for a partner; alas, the company was not comfortable launching into such a venture.

The Mark II

Once I had built the Mark I and was more or less happy with it, I had the opportunity to talk to an audio expert from Bowers & Wilkins, who made an important comment: to cater for high-end customers, streaming high-resolution audio through a power amplifier to good speakers, I would need better power amplifiers, and ideally everything integrated in one case. That brought me to the class D amplifier modules made by Hypex, which are used in many professional digital audio systems. I also realised that I needed a balanced audio interface between the DAC and the amplifiers. This led to a redesign, the Mark II: a media streamer and stereo power amplifier in a single case, as compact as possible while keeping its high-fidelity properties.

Figure 5. The Mark II, Eumeles: views from the front corner and from above.

I bought all the components and a case, and was ready to build it. Then, however, I had the opportunity to join a start-up, and later a second one; all components stayed in a box in the shed, and the project lay dormant for nearly ten years. Only during lockdown did I unpack the box, borrow my son’s Raspberry Pi, order a HiFiBerry DAC2 Pro XLR and use the time at home to finish the new, integrated media streamer and stereo power amplifier called Eumeles [1] (Figures 1, 5 and 6).

Figure 6. The Mark II opened, from above, with the VFD behind the front panel on the left and the connectors on the rear panel on the right.

Eumeles runs Mopidy [2], with an extension [3] to drive the VFD, and is operated from a mobile app or a web browser (Figure 7). The VFD need not show more than what is playing, as browsing for music happens on a smartphone or tablet; once started, put the phone aside and enjoy the music. Most importantly, the listening experience can be shared with friends and family.

Figure 7. Mopidy/Musicbox browser interface to Eumeles Integrated Media Player & Amplifier.
