Literally Rendering a 1930s House Thermally More Efficient

Replacing the pebble dash on the front of our house with insulating render: what it achieved, and why the gain was modest.

It takes energy to keep our houses warm on a cold day, most houses at least. This heating energy is determined by the difference between inside and outside temperatures, the thermal conductivity of their floors, walls and roofs, and any incidental internal heat sources such as appliances and people (100 W/person). While Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses covers a wider range of measures to address the issue, this essay focuses on one measure that was listed there under ‘Possible Further Improvements’: replacing the pebble dash with insulating render to literally render a 1930s house thermally more efficient.

Figure 1. Insulating render covering what used to be pebble dash on a typical 1930s house.

Status Quo

A non-negligible portion of the UK housing stock was built in the 1930s, in various shapes and styles, and with parts of the external walls rendered in pebble dash or roughcast. This popular type of coating came into use in the 1920s, partly to cover up poor brickwork, and continued to be used up to the 1970s; its one major advantage is that it needs little maintenance. Unfortunately, its thermal performance is poor¹.

Solid Walls with Pebble Dash

Houses built before the 1930s, and some built in the 1930s like ours, have solid brick walls and do not benefit from cavity walls. One of these solid brick walls, the front elevation, is pebble-dashed. Together with the internal plaster, this wall is about 270 mm thick and shows the rather poor thermal performance computed in Table 1.

Bay Window with Pebble Dash

Another common feature of 1930s or similar houses is the bay window. The wall of the window bay on the first floor is commonly rather thin: it uses a wooden framework and solid bricks toppled onto their side, and the outside is pebble-dashed. Together with the internal plaster, this wall is only about 100 mm thick and, as built, has a very poor U-value of 3.351 W/(m2 K). I had therefore already fitted 100 mm of rock wool on the inside of the bay (see Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses), so before the render replacement it showed the thermal performance computed in Table 2.

Render Replacement

The solution we chose was a compromise between keeping the general appearance of a 1930s house and improving the thermal performance of some of its outside walls. This was achieved by:

  • completely removing the existing pebble dash and base coat in order to expose the underlying brickwork (without damaging the bricks),
  • applying breathable insulating render² of 25–35 mm thickness and a breathable, coloured finishing coat³, carried out by a local company⁴.

The U-value drops to less than half, about 47% of the original. On a cold winter day with −10 °C outside and a cosy 21 °C inside, the heat loss would be only about 30 W/m2 instead of 60 W/m2, a reduction of 30 W/m2.

Since the rock wool already provides most of the thermal resistance, the U-value drops only by about 15%. Again, on a cold winter day with −10 °C outside and 21 °C inside, the heat loss would be about 8.1 W/m2 instead of 9.5 W/m2, a reduction of only 1.5 W/m2.

Performance Improvement Specifics

The total wall surface area of the original house is about 70 m2. The surface areas of the pebble-dashed solid wall and first-floor window bay are about 6 m2 and 3 m2, respectively. Given the corresponding new U-values above, the reduction would be about 194 W or 4.7 kWh/day for the solid wall and only about 4 W or 0.1 kWh/day for the already insulated window bay, both given a temperature difference of 31 K, with −10 °C outside and 21 °C inside.

In summary, for the original house, replacing the pebble dash with insulating render on about 9 m2 of wall (13% of the total wall area) is expected to yield a reduction in heat loss of about 200 W, almost entirely due to the solid wall section. This corresponds to a reduction of about 3% with regard to the baseline of Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses, a heating power of about 6 kW for the worst-case scenario mentioned above. Over a day, these 200 W amount to about 4.8 kWh, which would save about £0.50/day at 2022 gas prices, and this for only 13% of the external wall. If the entire house were covered with insulating render, I reckon the heat lost through the solid walls would be reduced by about 50%.

Conclusion

There are various possible responses to climate change and rising energy costs, in particular if that energy comes in the form of natural gas. A simple, low-tech and cost-effective way to save energy is to improve the thermal performance of houses through more insulation: energy not lost does not have to be put in to maintain the internal temperature. The measure shown in this essay, in addition to the general insulation of the house, is the replacement of pebble dash with insulating render; it maintains the typical appearance of a 1930s house and improves the thermal performance, albeit modestly for our house, as the window bay had already been insulated internally.
