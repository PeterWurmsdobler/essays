Managing Relative Humidity in an English Home

How periodic airing at the right moments creates a water vapour pump shifting humidity from the inside to the outside, resulting in a dry home.

Having lived in England for the past two decades, I have quite often heard people say that the humidity in their house is terrible in the cold seasons, with condensation and mould all over the place. Interestingly enough, in all the properties we have lived in so far we have not experienced that. I wonder whether this might be due to our strict ventilation regime: airing long enough for a complete air exchange with all windows open several times a day, at least in the early morning and late evening, and also after every cooking session. In what follows, I would like to explain the positive effects of airing thoroughly and, conversely, the negative consequences of not airing enough, using simple thermodynamic concepts such as relative and absolute humidity.

Keeping the Windows Shut

If you happen to live in the same street, please forgive me for sharing my observation that most windows of nearly all houses appear to remain closed all day or at least are not being opened for an airing period. Apart from some exchange through trickle vents and other leaks, the air and the water vapour it contains stay in those houses. As a result, in the cold seasons a daily cycle starts when the heating switches on in the morning:

  1. Heating up with decreasing relative humidity – The indoor air is almost at the dew point in the early morning hours, laden with water vapour at a relative humidity close to 100%. As the heating system gradually heats up the air (and the water vapour therein) to, say, 20 °C, the absolute amount of water in that air remains almost constant, since the house is nearly sealed. Because warmer air can hold more water vapour, the relative humidity decreases. Once the target temperature has been reached, the air will feel comparatively dry, say 70%.
  2. Maintaining the temperature with increasing relative humidity – During the day, the heating system maintains the temperature at said 20 °C. Due to respiration and perspiration of all occupants, cooking and showers, and drying clothes indoors (in airing cupboards), the air inside the house will absorb more and more water vapour, increasing both the absolute and relative humidity. In the example shown in Figure 2, an additional 2 g or so of water per kg of air is assumed. Towards the end of the day, the relative humidity would have climbed to about 85% in this example.
  3. Cooling down with increasing relative humidity – During the night the heating is usually set to a lower temperature, say 15 °C. As the air cools down, its capacity to store water vapour decreases, causing the relative humidity to increase.
  4. Cooling down with condensation – Once 100% relative humidity is reached, condensation will set in on the windows, or in all places that are cooler, like outside walls or behind cupboards. This continues until the heating is switched on again.

The whole cycle is depicted in the temperature–humidity diagram in Figure 2: 1) heating up at constant absolute humidity, 2) absorbing humidity at constant temperature, 3) cooling down with increasing relative humidity, 4) cooling down with condensation. The lines in shades of blue show the absolute humidity [g water per kg air] for constant relative humidity [%] as a function of temperature [°C]; the lines in shades of green show absolute humidity for constant enthalpy [kJ/kg] as a function of temperature. Enthalpy is a measure of energy contained in a substance, here the humid air.

As a consequence of this cycle, the inhabitants and their habits add water vapour to the indoor air every day, and every night some of this water vapour will condense somewhere. The result is a rather unhealthy indoor climate in which mould can grow in all areas where condensation forms. The mould produces spores which may affect the overall health of the inhabitants and exacerbate conditions such as asthma and other respiratory diseases.

Airing at Least Twice a Day

When I was a boy, it was my duty to open all windows in the house in the morning for 10 minutes and close them again, even in the cold Austrian winter with −20 °C outside. I hated that chore; only now, applying the same routine in our own household, do I understand its purpose and benefits. For instance, one morning at 7am the temperature outside in Cambridge, UK, was about 5 °C with a relative humidity of 80%, which results in about 4.3 g of water in 1 kg of air. This is the beginning of our sequence (not quite a cycle):

  1. Heating up cold air – All the stale, humid air leaves the house and is replaced by cold air from outside; even though its relative humidity is quite high, its absolute humidity is low. Once the windows are closed and the heating kicks in, the cold air is heated up to 20 °C. Given the low absolute vapour content to start with, the relative humidity will drop to about 30%, i.e. very dry.
  2. Maintaining the temperature with increasing relative humidity – During the day, the heating system again maintains a constant temperature of 20 °C. As in the case above, the air inside the house will absorb more and more water vapour, increasing both the absolute and relative humidity. Again, about 2 g of water per kg of air is assumed to be added. Towards the end of the day, the relative humidity would only have climbed to about 44% in this example.
  3. Cooling down with increasing relative humidity – During the night the heating is again set to a lower 15 °C. As before, the relative humidity will increase, but not as much, perhaps only to about 60%. In the morning, however, all that humid air is expelled and the sequence starts afresh.

The whole sequence is depicted in Figure 3: 1) heating up at constant (low) absolute humidity, 2) absorbing humidity at constant temperature, 3) cooling down with increasing relative humidity. Humid air is expelled at the end of the sequence.

The Water Vapour Pump

As a consequence of this routine, the water vapour produced by the inhabitants and their habits is expelled several times a day. The habit acts as a water vapour pump: over a longer period, the excess indoor humidity is moved from the inside to the outside. The result is a healthier indoor climate.

Conclusion

If you want to maintain a dry indoor climate, allow 10 minutes of airing for a complete air exchange with all windows open, at least in the early morning and late evening (when the outside temperature is lowest and the water content of the air at its minimum), and also after every cooking session, bath or shower. Even though it may sound counter-intuitive, the energy you lose is small compared to the quality of life you gain. In addition, drier indoor air has a slightly smaller thermal capacity and is therefore easier to heat. In due course, mould should cease to be an issue.

It would of course be better to have a mechanical ventilation system with controlled air circulation and heat recovery (MVHR). Retrofitting such a system in an old house, however, would be difficult and costly. It is easier just to keep airing.
