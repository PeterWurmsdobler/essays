Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Design Temperature

The same model at the design temperature, with interruptions for hot water and defrost, and whether larger radiators would pay for themselves.

The essay Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Heating highlights the importance of the heating schedule for keeping the flow temperature low, the COP high and the cost low. It uses a winter day at a constant outside temperature of 5 °C, representative of a Cambridge January. This essay looks at the operation of the heating system at the design temperature for the same location, −2 °C, and compares the cost of heating with gas and with a heat pump, both with the current radiators and after an upgrade.

Heat Pump for Heating Only

For this analysis, a feed-forward planner optimises a 24-hour heating schedule to meet a target temperature profile with comfort periods at an average of 19 °C (20–21 °C in living areas, 18 °C in bedrooms, cooler in the hallway). The optimiser balances two objectives: reaching comfort quickly when needed, and keeping flow temperatures low to maximise efficiency. It computes hourly power levels that:

  • Pre-heat before comfort periods: ramp up power 2 hours before the morning (06:00) and evening (17:00) comfort periods to reach 19 °C smoothly, with peak power around 5.5–6 kW;
  • Maintain comfort efficiently: once 19 °C is reached, reduce power to ~3 kW to hold the temperature;
  • Setback periods (night/day): deliver steady baseline heating at ~1.8 kW towards a 17 °C setback target; owing to the thermal mass, the indoor temperature only drops to about 18–18.3 °C.

This approach is only one example; many other strategies are conceivable, in particular with feedback and dynamic weather compensation: model predictive control, rule-based schedulers or machine-learning planners could achieve similar results. It’s all software, ultimately, and the physics of thermal inertia favours a combination of model-based feedback and feed-forward control strategies.

Assuming an outside temperature of −2 °C, the total space heat required for one day is 59.0 kWh. Supplying that heat with the gas boiler at 95% efficiency would cost £4.25/day, including the gas standing charge¹.

Heat Pump with Current Radiators

With the current radiators, K = 71.2 W/K1.2, the required flow temperatures and the resulting COP can be calculated (see COP estimation [1]). The cost of space heating with a heat pump is £3.99/day, below the gas baseline of £4.25/day only thanks to the gas standing charge: the achievable SCOP of 3.67 is slightly below the break-even COP of 3.73, so on unit energy costs alone gas (£3.94/day) is marginally cheaper. A spark gap of 3.86 (3.67/0.95), just below the Q1 2025 value of 3.92, would allow the heat pump to break even without a radiator upgrade.

Figure 2. Flow temperature (left) and COP (right) at design temperature.

Heat Pump with Upgraded Radiators

In a second survey of our house, I worked out how, and to what extent, the radiators could be upgraded; details are in Radiator Upgrades [2]. With the upgraded radiators, K = 93.5 W/K1.2 (from manufacturer specifications), the cost of space heating with a heat pump falls to £3.69/day, 13% below the gas baseline of £4.25/day. The SCOP of 3.97 exceeds the break-even COP of 3.73, so the heat pump is cheaper even on unit energy costs; it would break even with the radiator upgrade up to a spark gap of 4.18 (3.97/0.95). This upgrade, about 1.31× the current radiator capacity, is smaller than the 1.6× found necessary in How the Spark Gap Drives Radiator Upgrades for Heat Pump Installations, mainly because the identified h puts the design load at about 2.5 kW rather than 3.65 kW.

Figure 3. Flow temperature (left) and COP (right) at design temperature with radiator upgrade.

Heating with Interruptions

The simulations so far assume that the heat pump is used for space heating alone. Most heat pumps, however, also provide domestic hot water (DHW), for which about 2 hours a day are set aside here, depending on the power rating and the amount of hot water needed. At a design temperature below freezing, there are also defrost cycles. Let’s assume about 10 minutes every hour, so about 17% of the time.

To take these interruptions into account, the planner has to work out a heating schedule that maintains the temperature profile defined above, but also ensures that there is hot water in the morning (for showers) and in the evening (for washing up and showers). This is accommodated through four short DHW slots: a 40-minute pre-heat at 04:00–04:40 charges the cylinder before the morning routine, with a 20-minute top-up at 07:00–07:20 to maintain hot water availability throughout the morning. The same pattern is repeated for the afternoon: a 40-minute pre-heat at 15:00–15:40 and a 20-minute top-up at 18:00–18:20 cover the evening. A well-insulated hot water cylinder loses only a few degrees over several hours, so staggering the pre-heat and top-up in this way provides reliable hot water while keeping each space-heating interruption short.

At an outside temperature of −2 °C, the space heat delivered in the available hours (excluding DHW slots and defrost downtime) is 58.7 kWh/day; supplying it with the gas boiler would cost £4.23/day, including the gas standing charge. Only space-heating costs are compared in this section.

Heat Pump with Current Radiators

With the current radiators, the cost of space heating with a heat pump is £4.45/day, 5% more than the gas baseline of £4.23/day. The space-heating SCOP of 3.27 sets the break-even spark gap: a spark gap of 3.45 (3.27/0.95) would allow the heat pump to break even without a radiator upgrade.

Figure 5. Flow temperature (left) and COP (right) at design temperature, with gaps.

Heat Pump with Upgraded Radiators

With the upgraded radiators, the cost of space heating with a heat pump is £4.07/day, 4% below the gas baseline of £4.23/day, again only thanks to the gas standing charge. With a space-heating SCOP of 3.58, a spark gap of 3.77 (3.58/0.95) would allow the heat pump to break even on unit energy costs with the radiator upgrade.

Conclusion

At design temperature (−2 °C), with the interruptions for DHW and defrost taken into account, the heat pump costs more to run than a gas boiler on unit energy costs, whether or not the radiators are upgraded. Without an upgrade, the space-heating SCOP is 3.27; with upgraded radiators it improves to 3.58. The corresponding break-even spark gaps, 3.45 and 3.77, both fall below the spark gap of 3.92, so under these conditions the heat pump cannot break even on unit energy costs alone. Including the gas standing charge, the heat pump costs 5% more than gas with the current radiators and 4% less with upgraded radiators. A spark gap of 3.45 is not an unusual figure compared with other European countries. As renewables are built out in the UK, gas, which set the electricity price about 90% of the time in 2025–26, will do so less often, and the spark gap could consequently fall towards 3 or less in due course².

The difference in space-heating cost between current and upgraded radiators at design conditions, with DHW and defrost interruptions, is £0.38/day. According to my radiator-upgrade survey, the upgrade would cost about £2,000 in materials, a rough estimate, as I would do the work, including rerouting some pipes, myself, which translates into about 5,200 design-temperature days to recover the investment. At roughly 12 days per year close to design conditions (daily mean at or below 0 °C at the Cambridge Botanic Garden station in 2025), recovery takes about 440 years, quite a long time. In contrast, on a more typical cold winter day (To = 5 ∘C), smooth heat pump operation with the current radiators already costs £1.76/day against a gas baseline of £2.53/day, 30% cheaper. Taking all conditions together, the seasonal economics favour the heat pump without any radiator upgrade.

A second lesson from the simulation with interruptions is that, although the average power needed to heat the house at an outside temperature of −2 °C is only about 2.5 kW, a rating of about 6 kW, more than twice that, is needed to allow for flexibility and for the interruptions due to DHW generation and defrost cycles. That’s worth noting.

While this essay only makes an allowance for DHW, its cost with a heat pump is the subject of Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Hot Water.

Analysis conducted on a 1930s semi-detached house. Code and methodology available at github.com/PeterWurmsdobler/heat-pump-cost [3].

  2. Lower spark gap: Admittedly, there is some wishful thinking here, as in theory, once gas falls off the marginal pricing merit order (gas not needed any more), only renewables would determine the price. However, since the intermittency of wind and solar means that renewables cannot fill every slot, batteries and overbuild are needed, i.e. more capital expenditure. As the build-out increases, the overbuild factor goes up and the utilisation factor goes down, so the price must go up. In other words, water, wind and solar are cheap as long as they can sell every kWh, i.e. while they only cover a fraction of the demand; the price will change to something proportional to the overbuild factor once renewables cover 100% of the demand, which I would hope will still be below 3 times that of gas. ↩
