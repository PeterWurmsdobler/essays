Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Heating

A simple dynamic model of our house, identified from recorded temperatures, shows that how a heat pump is operated matters as much as the hardware.

The essay How the Spark Gap Drives Radiator Upgrades for Heat Pump Installations looks at the implications of the spark gap for capital and operating expenses when the radiators have to be upgraded to keep the flow temperature low and the Coefficient of Performance (COP) high; there, a simple static thermal analysis considers average heat loss and temperatures for a heat pump in stationary operation. The picture changes once the dynamics of operation are taken into account, both temperature profiles and electricity tariffs. As sources such as ‘So You’re Thinking About a Heat Pump: The UK Homeowner’s Guide to Heat Pumps’ [1] point out, heat pumps require different operating strategies from the gas boilers that most households are used to. The objective of this essay is to show quantitatively how the control and operating strategy affects the economics of heating a home with a heat pump.

Dynamic Modelling

A stationary model assumes all process variables to be constant at a given point of operation: for a constant outdoor temperature a constant heating power is required to maintain a constant indoor temperature by compensating a constant heat loss. While such a static analysis gives some insight into the minimum heating requirements at various operating points, a dynamic analysis looks at time-varying process variables such as heating power or flow temperature, and that requires a dynamic model of the system first.

Dynamic Thermal Model

A dynamic model reflects the time-varying behaviour of a system. Here, I would like to use the simplest conceivable model, a linear time-invariant (LTI) first-order model with two parameters: the thermal capacity C of the entire house, and the specific heat loss, also called the Heat Transfer Coefficient (HTC), h. The process variables are the internal temperature Ti and the outside temperature To; heat is supplied as Qh at flow temperature Tf and return temperature Tr, then transferred through the radiators to the internal thermal mass as Qr; in addition, internal gains from appliances and occupants are accounted for as a heating power Qa. The house loses heat as Ql through the building fabric, as determined by h. The log burner is deliberately left out of this model: we use it on some winter days, when it contributes up to about 9 kWh a day, which is within the noise of the model.

How the Spark Gap Drives Radiator Upgrades for Heat Pump Installations provides some of the parameters for the dynamic analysis: K = 71.2 W/K1.2 (the sum of the manufacturer specifications of all radiators, see Radiator Survey [2]) with n = 1.2, ρ = 1 kg/l and cp = 4.18 kJ/(kg K). The flow rate V f is assumed to be constant at 20 l/min, so that the heating power is modulated through the flow temperature only; assuming no losses in the short pipework, the heat balance of the radiator circuit is Qh = Qr. For the heat transfer coefficient, the two prior estimates nearly coincide: h = 196 W/K from first principles and h = 188 W/K from recorded power consumption, the latter being used as the prior. The internal gains are assumed to be about Qa = 0.5 kW. The thermal capacity C of the house is completely unknown.

Model Parameter Identification

An entire domain of control systems analysis and design is dedicated to model parameter identification, usually from recorded data or from the system’s response to a stimulus. To that end, we consider a simplified model that leaves out the internals of the heating system but keeps the internal gains Qa and the radiator power Qr as the stimulus:

In practical terms, this means setting up some experiments and recording data. The indoor temperature is recorded by a Raspberry PicoPi Temperature Logger [3]. Outside temperatures are obtained from the Cambridge Raw Daily Weather Data [4]. We consider three periods:

  • ‘2026-04-04 01:00:00’ to ‘2026-04-04 06:00:00’, outside To = 7 ∘C, heating off, i.e. Qr = 0, and Qa ≈ 0.5 kW
  • ‘2026-04-05 22:45:00’ to ‘2026-04-06 04:20:00’, outside To = 3 ∘C, heating off, i.e. Qr = 0, and Qa ≈ 0.5 kW
  • ‘2026-04-06 06:30:00’ to ‘2026-04-06 08:15:00’, outside To from 3 °C to 7 °C, heating on full power, i.e. Qr > 0 kW, and Qa ≈ 0.5 kW

System identification tools then provide estimates of the missing parameters, C, h and Qa; the results are shown in Figure 2, with details in Thermal Parameter Identification for the Dynamic House Model [5]. The parameters are found to be C = 21.0 MJ/K, h = 142.6 W/K, and the associated time constant τ = C/h = 146,937 s (40.8 h), as well as Qa = 500 W and Qr = 4 kW during the morning heating period. A time constant of 40.8 h means that the house, if left unheated, would cover 63% of the way towards a constant outside temperature in roughly 1.7 days.

The identified h is lower than both prior estimates. The 188 W/K of How the Spark Gap Drives Radiator Upgrades for Heat Pump Installations was deliberately on the safe side; this essay uses the identified value, which the 2025 annual gas record supports (see Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Space Heating and Domestic Hot Water). The prior on h carries little weight: the first-principles prior of 196 W/K gives practically the same result, h = 143.5 W/K. The temperature data do not determine Qa, however: assuming 300 W instead gives h = 131.4 W/K and τ = 44.8 h with a similar fit. The log burner was not used during these April periods.

Figure 2. Measured and estimated inside temperature based on the model in three conditions.

Dynamic Heating Simulation

With a parameterised dynamic model, the dynamic heating requirements can be worked out, and eventually the cost of heating with various sources. For this essay, let’s assume a winter day with a constant outdoor temperature of To = 5 ∘C, representative of a Cambridge January; all costs are based on the Ofgem price cap for the first quarter of 2025¹. As a ballpark figure, maintaining an average of 19 °C indoors in steady state means compensating a loss of Ql = 142.6 × 14 = 2.0 kW, or ~48 kWh/day; with the internal gains of Qa = 0.5 kW covering part of that loss, the heating system itself would need to supply about 1.5 kW, or ~36 kWh/day. But houses are usually not maintained at a constant temperature throughout the day; this is where the dynamic simulation comes in.

Gas Boiler Space Heating

Let’s assume a traditional daily heating profile as pictured below. The heating schedule is simple: heating off from 22:00 until 06:00, on from 06:00 to 09:00 with set-point Ts = 19 ∘C, then off again from 09:00 to 17:00 allowing the house to cool naturally, and finally on again from 17:00 to 22:00 with Ts = 19 ∘C. The simple relay-based thermostatic controller does the following:

  • if Ti < Ts, heat at full power until the target temperature Ts is reached;
  • once Ts is reached, feed-forward heating power Qh = h(Ts−To)−Qa;
  • when heating is off, let the temperature decay naturally.

Figure 3. January day heated with gas boiler, heat = 33.2 kWh/day, £2.53/day.

The gas boiler delivers its maximum capacity during warm-up periods, approximately 8.2 kW when the flow temperature reaches 74 °C². The simulation shows that with a daily heat delivery of 33.2 kWh, the boiler consumes 35.0 kWh of gas (at 95% efficiency). The total daily cost is £2.53 (£2.22 gas energy + £0.32 gas standing charge, rounded).

Equivalent Heat Pump Variant

Let’s assume we install an equivalent heat pump capable of delivering the same power and run it in the same way, which leads to the same flow and return temperatures at the same flow rate; the COP and the resulting electricity consumption follow from these. The flow temperature profile mirrors the power demand: when the house needs rapid heating in the morning and evening, the heat pump must push flow temperatures up to 74 °C, which reduces efficiency. The COP according to my COP estimation [6] varies throughout the day:

  • During high-power warm-up periods (Tf ≈ 74 ∘C): COP ≈ 2.6
  • During feed-forward periods after warm-up (Tf ≈ 32 ∘C): COP ≈ 5.3
  • Overall average: SCOP = 2.96 (heat delivered over electricity consumed for the day)

Dividing the heating power by the COP at each point in time gives a total electricity consumption of 11.2 kWh/day. This costs £2.79/day for energy³. Compared with the gas boiler at £2.53/day (energy plus gas standing charge), the heat pump is 10% more expensive. The low SCOP of 2.96 falls well below the break-even COP of 3.73, making this strategy uneconomical. Such a system would also require a heat pump specified for the maximum power, not the average, which increases capital expenditure. Conclusion: do not run a heat pump like a gas boiler; Q.E.D.

Figure 4. January day with equivalent heat pump, heat = 33.2 kWh/day, electricity = 11.2 kWh/day, £2.79/day.

Smooth Operator Heat Pump

Let’s devise a controller that operates more sensibly: it runs continuously at varying power levels, maintains baseline heating to prevent excessive temperature drops, and keeps flow temperatures low to maximise the COP. It uses predictive logic with a 3-hour lookahead to pre-heat smoothly before comfort periods, targets a maximum flow temperature of 45 °C, and works with the following temperature targets:

  • Night (22:00–06:00): Setback target of 17 °C with reduced baseline heating
  • Morning (06:00–09:00): Achieve 19 °C comfort with ramped power
  • Day (09:00–17:00): Setback target of 17 °C with reduced baseline heating
  • Evening (17:00–22:00): Achieve 19 °C comfort with ramped power

Running the heat pump continuously at low baseline power (about 0.9 kW) outside comfort periods prevents temperature drops that require high-power recovery later. Owing to the long time constant of the house, the indoor temperature never falls to the 17 °C setback target and stays between 18.6 °C and 19.2 °C throughout. Power varies smoothly from 0.9 kW during setback periods to 2.1 kW during comfort periods, avoiding the 0–8.2 kW on/off cycling of the gas boiler. The total heat delivered is 36.7 kWh/day, about 10% more than with the gas boiler, but distributed more evenly, which is not only pleasant for cats.

Figure 5. Smooth heat pump operation, heat = 36.7 kWh/day, electricity = 7.1 kWh/day, £1.76/day.

The flow temperature profile reveals the efficiency advantage of continuous operation: flow temperatures range from 27–36 °C instead of spiking to 74 °C. During baseline heating periods, flow temperatures stay around 27–28 °C, achieving a COP of about 6.0–6.2 (such high values represent best-case performance at very low temperature lift). During comfort periods, temperatures reach 33–36 °C with COP around 4.8–5.2. The overall SCOP of 5.18 compares with 2.96 under simple thermostat control. With an electricity consumption of 7.1 kWh/day, the daily cost is £1.76, cheaper than both the thermostat-controlled heat pump at £2.79 and the gas boiler at £2.53.

Figure 6. Continuous smooth operation maintains low flow temperatures and high COP throughout the day.

Heat pump economics therefore depend critically on the control strategy: continuous operation with optimised flow temperatures and predictive pre-heating turns the heat pump from 10% more expensive than gas into 30% cheaper, while keeping the temperature more even throughout the day. The economics could be improved further by moderately upgrading radiators to allow an even lower flow temperature, hence increasing the COP and decreasing electricity consumption. In addition, a heat pump rated for the average rather than the peak power is sufficient, which reduces capital expenditure.

Playing the Dynamic Tariffs

Suppose electricity does not incur the same cost throughout the day, but rather a dynamic tariff such as Octopus Cosy [7]⁴, which offers three distinct rate periods designed to encourage load shifting away from peak demand hours:

  • Cosy rate (04:00–07:00, 13:00–16:00, 22:00–00:00): 14.53 p/kWh, 42% below the flat rate of 24.86 p/kWh
  • Day rate (all other times): 33.28 p/kWh, 34% above the flat rate
  • Peak rate (16:00–19:00): 51.68 p/kWh, 108% above the flat rate

Figure 7. Octopus Cosy tariff structure showing three rate periods throughout the day.

The tariff structure creates an economic incentive to shift heating load from the evening peak (16:00–19:00) to the cheaper periods. With a dynamic model of the house thermal response, a cost-optimising controller can exploit this price variation by using the building’s thermal mass as a ‘battery’, pre-heating hard during cheap periods and coasting through expensive ones. To minimise cost, the strategy accepts some temperature variation within a range of 18.5–19.5 °C:

  • During cheap periods (04:00–07:00, 13:00–16:00, 22:00–00:00): Heat at 2.2–3.1 kW with flow temperatures up to 40–43 °C (capped at 55 °C during cheap periods and 45 °C otherwise). Pre-heat towards 19.5 °C to maximise thermal energy stored in the building fabric while electricity is cheap.
  • During peak period (16:00–19:00): Minimal or no heating (0–0.5 kW), relying almost entirely on stored thermal energy as the temperature drops from about 19.4 °C to 18.8 °C; with a time constant of 40.8 h, the house cools slowly enough to remain comfortable.
  • During standard periods: Light heating (1.7–1.9 kW), with heating paused for an hour or two after some cheap periods end, coasting on thermal mass with flow temperatures around 33–35 °C to balance efficiency and comfort.

In the simulation, the temperature stays between 18.5 °C and 19.4 °C throughout the day. The total heat delivered is 37.2 kWh/day (similar to the 36.7 kWh from flat-rate smooth operation).

Figure 8. Tariff-optimised heat pump operation, heat = 37.2 kWh/day, electricity = 7.8 kWh/day, £1.76/day.

The flow temperature and COP profiles reveal the trade-off between efficiency and cost optimisation. During cheap-rate pre-heating, the flow temperatures of 40–43 °C give a COP of around 4.1–4.7. During the peak period, the little heating that occurs runs at a very low flow temperature (about 24 °C) and a COP of about 7. During standard-rate periods, the flow temperatures of 33–35 °C achieve a COP of about 5.0–5.2. The overall SCOP of 4.74 is lower than that of the flat-tariff smooth operation (5.18) due to the intensive pre-heating strategy, so the heat pump uses 7.8 kWh instead of 7.1 kWh of electricity. Buying most of it at the cheap rate only just compensates for that: the same 7.8 kWh would cost £1.95 at the flat rate, so Cosy merely matches the £1.76 of smooth operation.

Figure 9. Flow temperatures up to 43 °C during cheap periods to store energy; minimal heating during expensive peak period.

The Octopus Cosy tariff used to offer a cheap rate as low as 8 p/kWh, at which the economics would have been considerably more favourable. Other time-of-use tariffs, such as Octopus Agile, use half-hourly prices linked to the wholesale market that can fall very low during periods of high renewable generation, or spike sharply at times of grid stress. A cost-optimising controller with access to day-ahead prices could exploit those deeper discounts, but the outcome is inherently variable and harder to predict.

Comparison

The daily operating costs of the simulated strategies at To = 5 ∘C are summarised below; they include the heating energy and, for gas, the gas standing charge, but not the electricity standing charge:

  • Gas boiler (simple on/off control, heating 06:00–09:00 and 17:00–22:00 only): £2.53/day
    Heat: 33.2 kWh/day, Gas: 35.0 kWh/day, Cost: £2.22 gas energy + £0.32 gas standing charge
  • Heat pump with simple thermostat control (mimicking gas boiler operation): £2.79/day (+10% vs gas)
    Heat: 33.2 kWh/day, Electricity: 11.2 kWh/day, SCOP: 2.96, Cost: £2.79 energy
    Flow temperatures spike to 74 °C during warm-up periods; the SCOP falls well below the break-even COP of 3.73.
  • Heat pump with smooth continuous operation (flat tariff): £1.76/day (−30% vs gas)
    Heat: 36.7 kWh/day, Electricity: 7.1 kWh/day, SCOP: 5.18, Cost: £1.76 energy
    Continuous baseline heating, flow temperatures 27–36 °C, more even temperatures and higher COP.
  • Heat pump with tariff-optimised operation (Octopus Cosy): £1.76/day (−30% vs gas)
    Heat: 37.2 kWh/day, Electricity: 7.8 kWh/day, SCOP: 4.74, Cost: £1.76 energy
    Strong pre-heating during cheap periods (14.53 p), minimal or no heating during expensive peak (51.68 p).

Operated like a gas boiler, the heat pump is the worst performer, 10% more expensive than gas; operated smoothly with low flow temperatures and predictive control, it is 30% cheaper, with more even indoor temperatures. The tariff-optimised strategy on Octopus Cosy achieves practically the same result: at the April 2026 Cosy rates, the saving from the price differential is used up by the additional electricity of the intensive pre-heating.

Conclusion

In summary, a heating system with a heat pump cannot be operated economically like one with a gas boiler; it needs an awareness of energy prices (e.g. day-ahead prices on dynamic tariffs such as Agile), weather patterns and forecasts, and a basic understanding of heat pump principles, in particular COP. Operating and optimising the system becomes quite complex and depends on the optimisation criterion, e.g. minimising energy, cost or CO2, or some combination thereof. Control strategy matters as much as hardware. There are several options:

  • DIY: set up Home Assistant [8], automate your home with sensors and actuators, and spend your spare time on optimisation of the home energy system;
  • use the energy management system offered by the heat pump manufacturer, which might even integrate other appliances;
  • outsource the optimisation to a specialist cloud software provider that manages all of that for you, learning from patterns across a wider user base, e.g. Havenwise [9] or Adia Thermal [10].

Limitations

These simulations assume that the heat pump is used for space heating only, whereas most heat pumps also provide DHW. In colder conditions, defrost cycles also have to be taken into account: periodically, the heat pump switches into reverse mode to melt ice build-up on the outdoor heat exchanger coil. Both are treated as interruptions to space heating in Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Design Temperature; the energy needed for DHW itself is analysed in Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Hot Water.
