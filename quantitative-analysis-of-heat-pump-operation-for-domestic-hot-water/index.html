Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Hot Water

What a heat pump would have cost to heat our domestic hot water in 2025, compared with a gas boiler.

The essay Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Design Temperature looks at heat pump operation at a design temperature of −2 °C and already sets aside time during the day for the same heat pump to prepare domestic hot water (DHW). This essay focuses on DHW alone and assesses the economics of producing it with a heat pump that serves both space heating and DHW.

DHW Baseline

Heat pumps, like gas boilers, have to be rated both for space heating at a design temperature and for the expected daily use of DHW. Two independent approaches support the DHW estimate used here. First, the energy for DHW is assumed to stay much the same throughout the year and to account for the energy consumption in summer; during summer 2025, for instance, our gas usage was about 12.5 kWh per day on average, enough energy to heat up 227 l of water from about 10 °C to 55 °C at a 95% boiler efficiency. Second, an average shower takes about 8 minutes, which at the flow rate of 6 l/min in our house consumes about 50 l of hot water. Three people in the household would then use 150 l; some additional hot water in the kitchen and for other uses brings the total to about 200 l/day, a plausible figure that is used throughout this analysis.

If 200 l of hot water had to be produced on demand at the shower flow rate of 6 l/min, a powerful heat pump would be required given the thermal capacity of water (4.18 kJ/(kg K)); the heating power would be of the order of 20–30 kW, similar to that of a gas combination boiler or of our current 25 kW boiler. Otherwise, a hot water cylinder is needed as a buffer, heated gradually when time and cost permit, in particular when space heating is not required. Heating 200 l/day of water from 10 °C to 55 °C requires 10.5 kWh of thermal energy; a heat pump rated at 6 kW needs about 1.75 hours for that, during which it is not available for space heating.

Daily Cost of DHW

Costs are based on the 2025 Ofgem price caps¹: the analysis as a function of outdoor temperature uses the first quarter of 2025, the annual analysis the quarterly caps. The daily cost of heating 200 l of water from 10 °C to 55 °C (10.5 kWh) using a gas boiler at 95% efficiency (11.05 kWh of gas) would be 70.1 p at 6.34 p/kWh. The cost of heating the same amount of water using a heat pump depends on the COP, which in turn depends on the flow temperature (55 °C for DHW) and the outdoor temperature. Figure 1 shows both the COP and the daily cost of 200 l of hot water from a heat pump as a function of the outdoor temperature. The heat pump breaks even with gas at an outdoor temperature of approximately 10.8 °C, where the COP reaches the break-even value of 3.73; above that temperature, heat pump DHW is the cheaper option.

Figure 1. Cost of hot water (left axis, blue) and COP (right axis, green) as a function of outdoor temperature at the first-quarter 2025 price cap. The curves level off above about 37 °C, where the COP reaches the model’s cap of 8.

Turned around, the break-even condition gives the spark gap required, as a function of outdoor temperature, for the heat pump to break even. Even at the Cambridge design temperature of −2 °C, where the COP is only 2.96, a spark gap of 3.11 (the COP divided by the boiler efficiency) would be sufficient.

Figure 2. Required spark gap for DHW break-even as a function of outdoor temperature. The spark gap of 3.92 for the first quarter of 2025 is shown for reference, along with the design temperature of −2 °C where a spark gap of 3.11 would be needed. The curve levels off at 8/0.95 = 8.42 where the COP reaches the model’s cap of 8.

Annual Cost of DHW

To assess the annual economics of heat pump DHW, the analysis uses the recorded half-hourly Cambridge temperatures for 2025 with a daily hot water schedule of 100 l at 04:00 (morning) and 100 l at 15:00 (afternoon), totalling the baseline 200 l/day. For each batch, the COP is calculated from the outdoor temperature at that moment, which accounts for seasonal and daily temperature variations. Over the 365 days, the average outdoor temperature in Cambridge was 10.3 °C, resulting in an effective COP of 3.68 for DHW, i.e. total heat divided by total electricity. Over the year, the 10.5 kWh a day add up to about 3,830 kWh of heat, or about 4,030 kWh of gas at 95% boiler efficiency. Comparing energy costs only:

  • Heat pump: £270 per year (electricity)
  • Gas boiler: £262 per year (energy only)
  • Difference: £8 more expensive (+3%)

The heat pump is slightly more expensive because the effective COP of 3.68 lies at the lower end of the 2025 break-even COPs, which ranged from 3.67 in the second quarter to 3.98 in the fourth. The heat pump is cheaper on most days in spring and summer, when the COP is higher, and more expensive on 220 days in all, most of them in autumn and winter. Parity over the year would require a spark gap of 3.88 or lower.

Figure 3. Daily DHW costs throughout 2025 (left axis) and average outdoor temperature (right axis, green fill). Heat pump costs (blue) vary with outdoor temperature whilst gas energy costs (orange dashed) change only with the quarterly gas price, between 69.5 p/day and 77.3 p/day.

Conclusion

In the Cambridge climate, heat pump DHW would have been about 3% more expensive than gas on an energy-only basis in 2025, £270 against £262 a year, so the two are roughly on a par. What stands between heat pump DHW and a clear saving is the UK’s spark gap, between 3.87 and 4.19 in 2025, a matter of energy pricing and policy rather than a physical constraint. Even at the design temperature of −2 °C, where the COP falls to 2.96, a spark gap of only 3.11 would achieve break-even. Several European countries have spark gaps below 3, at which heat pump DHW would break even almost all year round in this climate.

The situation changes when space heating and DHW are considered together over a whole year, as the gas standing charge is then avoided altogether; that is the subject of Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Space Heating and Domestic Hot Water.

Analysis conducted on a 1930s semi-detached house. Code and methodology available at github.com/PeterWurmsdobler/heat-pump-cost [1].
