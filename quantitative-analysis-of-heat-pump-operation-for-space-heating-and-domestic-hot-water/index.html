Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Space Heating and Domestic Hot Water

A full-year simulation of space heating and hot water with the 2025 weather and price caps: what a heat pump would have cost us compared with gas.

The essays Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Design Temperature and Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Hot Water investigate heat pump operation for space heating and domestic hot water (DHW), respectively, at specific outdoor conditions. This essay combines both into a simulation driven by a year of recorded outdoor temperatures to estimate what running a heat pump on a standard tariff would have cost in total, and compares the result with a gas boiler.

Operating Conditions

The object of investigation is our 1930s semi-detached house, a type described in Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses, with the dynamic properties derived in Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Heating: a thermal capacity of C = 21.0 MJ/K, a heat transfer coefficient of h = 142.6 W/K, the associated time constant τ = C/h = 146,937 s (40.8 h), and internal gains from appliances and occupants of Qa = 500 W; as explained there, the log burner is deliberately not modelled. The radiator constant is K = 71.2 W/K1.2, the gas boiler has an efficiency of 95%, and the equations of the dynamic model are the same as in that essay. Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Design Temperature showed that a 6 kW heat pump should provide enough heat for both space heating and DHW¹.

Domestic Hot Water Requirements

As derived in Quantitative Analysis of Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Hot Water, the DHW requirement is assumed to be 200 l/day, heated from 10 °C to 55 °C at a flow temperature of 55 °C, which requires 10.5 kWh of thermal energy. A heat pump rated at 6 kW needs about 1.75 hours for that, for which 2 hours are set aside in the same four slots as in the design-temperature simulation: 40-minute pre-heats at 04:00–04:40 and 15:00–15:40, each followed by a 20-minute top-up at 07:00–07:20 and 18:00–18:20, respectively.

Space Heating Requirements

Space heating is only required if the average daily outdoor temperature is below 15 °C; above that, the internal gains are assumed to keep the house warm enough. When the heating is on, the targets are:

  • Night (22:00–06:00): Maintain ~17 °C setback,
  • Morning (06:00–09:00): Achieve 19 °C comfort,
  • Day (09:00–17:00): Maintain ~17 °C setback,
  • Evening (17:00–22:00): Achieve 19 °C comfort.

As in the design-temperature essay, the comfort temperature is an average of 19 °C, i.e. 20–21 °C in living areas, 18 °C in bedrooms, and about 15 °C in the hallway.

Heat Pump Controller Design

The controller operates on a rolling 24-hour planning window that starts at 22:00 each evening, the end of the evening comfort period when the indoor temperature is expected to be at its set-point. At this point, the outdoor temperatures for the coming day are read from the recording (as a proxy for a good weather forecast), and two scheduling decisions are made: whether the 24-hour mean temperature is below 15 °C, in which case space heating is activated, and whether the minimum temperature falls below 2 °C, in which case the defrost allowance of 10 minutes per hour (roughly 17% of the available heating time) from the design-temperature essay is reserved. In all cases, the DHW slots are reserved before the space-heating schedule is computed.

When space heating is required, a feed-forward plan is computed by solving a weighted least-squares problem: 24 hourly power levels are chosen to minimise the weighted sum of squared deviations from the target temperature profile, evaluated at every minute of the window. The optimiser assigns a uniform weight of 200 to all time steps in the two comfort periods, which distributes the heat evenly across each period rather than concentrating it at one end, and a weight of 5 to the setback periods. The power bounds for each hour are derived from the steady-state heat demand at the prevailing mean outdoor temperature: comfort hours and the two pre-comfort warm-up hours carry a higher minimum power to ensure the house reaches its set-point, whilst setback hours have a lower minimum that still maintains a 17 °C floor.

Heating Integration

Once the hourly schedule is fixed, it is executed minute by minute, with a proportional correction term, capped at 30% of the feed-forward value, compensating at each step for any discrepancy between the thermal model and the actual response of the house. The simulation of the thermal behaviour of the house is heat-source-agnostic: the algorithm works out the power requirement, which, given the radiator constant, determines the required flow temperature irrespective of the source. From that, the electricity or gas needed follows from the achievable COP at the flow and outside temperatures, or from the boiler efficiency, respectively.

Simulation Results

The simulation was run against the outdoor temperatures recorded at the Cambridge Botanic Garden station throughout 2025, 17,506 half-hourly readings. The daily planning cycle described above was applied to each of the 364 days from 2 January to 31 December, and the resulting heat pump electricity costs were accumulated and compared with those of the gas boiler over the same period.

Of the 364 days simulated, space heating was active on 270. On the remaining 94 days the mean outdoor temperature was at or above 15 °C, so the heat pump was only operated for DHW and only DHW costs were incurred. The costs are based on the quarterly Ofgem price caps listed in Table 1; as before, the electricity standing charge is excluded and the gas standing charge is added to the gas scenario.

Table 1. Ofgem price cap unit rates and standing charges for 2025, by quarter, for a typical dual-fuel household paying by Direct Debit.

Over the 364 days, the heat pump delivered 6,980 kWh of space-heating thermal energy, consuming 1,815 kWh of electricity. DHW required a fixed 3,822 kWh of thermal energy across the 364 days, consuming a further 1,037 kWh of electricity, an effective COP of 3.69. Combined, the simulated system produced 10,802 kWh of useful heat from 2,852 kWh of electricity. This is consistent with the recorded gas consumption of 11,508 kWh in 2025, which at 95% boiler efficiency delivers around 10,932 kWh of useful heat. With the same 3,822 kWh of DHW heat, this leaves about 7,110 kWh of recorded space heat against the simulated 6,980 kWh; differences of roughly 1–2% suggest that the model replicates the real system reasonably well, supporting the identified value of h.

Across the 270 heating days, the space-heating SCOP, i.e. total space heat divided by total electricity (6980/1815), was 3.85. The quarterly spark gaps for 2025 were 3.92 (Q1), 3.87 (Q2), 4.06 (Q3) and 4.19 (Q4), i.e. break-even COPs of 3.73, 3.67, 3.86 and 3.98 with a 95% efficient boiler. On individual days, the coldest winter days returned SCOPs of 3.0–3.5, below the quarterly break-even COP, whilst milder days in spring and autumn reached 5–6 and the warmest heating days reached the COP limit of 8 used in the model.² By quarter, the SCOP was 3.58 in Q1, below its break-even COP of 3.73, but 4.25 in Q2, 4.61 in Q3 and 4.04 in Q4, above theirs; Q1, with almost half of the annual space heat (3,380 kWh), is the only quarter in which gas would have been cheaper for space heating on unit energy costs.

The annual electricity cost for space heating and DHW combined was £734.70. The comparable gas cost, including the gas standing charge for every day, was £848.96, giving an annual saving of £114.26 in favour of the heat pump. This saving is essentially the gas standing charge, which accumulates to roughly £117 over the year; without it, the two options would cost much the same in energy.

Specific Days

Three days were selected for detailed examination: the coldest recorded day in January, a more typical cold winter day also in January, and a mild spring day in April that still required some space heating. For each, a profile plot shows the indoor temperature, outdoor temperature, set-point and heat delivered; a second plot shows the flow temperature and COP alongside the quarterly break-even COP. The equivalent pair of plots for every simulated day is available in the assets/annual [1] folder on GitHub.

Coldest Day

The coldest day identified in January 2025 was 11 January, with a mean outdoor temperature over the 22:00-to-22:00 simulation window of −3.1 °C and a minimum of −6.8 °C. With the minimum well below the 2 °C threshold, defrost cycles of 10 minutes per hour were included in the schedule.

Figure 2. Indoor temperature, set-point, outdoor temperature, and heat delivered on 11 January 2025.

The heat pump delivered 60.1 kWh of space heat over the day, consuming 19.68 kWh of electricity, a space-heating SCOP of 3.06. At the Q1 rate of 24.86 p/kWh, the space-heating cost was £4.89. DHW required 3.61 kWh of electricity at a cost of £0.90, bringing the daily heat pump total to £5.79. The gas equivalent for the combined space and DHW load, at 95% boiler efficiency and including the daily standing charge, was £5.03. On this most demanding day, the heat pump cost more to run than the gas boiler, consistent with the findings in Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Design Temperature.

Figure 3. Flow temperature and COP on 11 January 2025. The dashed red line marks the Q1 break-even COP of 3.73 (spark gap 3.92 × 0.95).

The COP trace reflects the varying load through the day. During the early part of the overnight setback, the heat pump runs at low power while the house cools slowly towards the 17 °C setback target; the required flow temperature is then low and the COP about 5. When the pre-morning ramp begins, the greater power needed to bring the house to 19 °C raises the flow temperature towards 60 °C and the COP falls below 3, well below the Q1 break-even COP of 3.73. The evening comfort period follows a similar pattern.

Cold Winter Day

A more typical cold winter day in Cambridge is illustrated by 5 January 2025, with a mean outdoor temperature of 4.2 °C and a minimum of −1.6 °C. The overnight minimum was below the 2 °C defrost threshold, so defrost cycles were included alongside DHW slots.

Figure 4. Indoor temperature, set-point, outdoor temperature, and heat delivered on 5 January 2025.

The heat pump delivered 35.4 kWh of space heat, consuming 10.14 kWh of electricity at a SCOP of 3.49; at the Q1 rate, the space-heating cost was £2.52. DHW required 3.15 kWh of electricity at a cost of £0.78, giving a daily heat pump total of £3.31 against a gas equivalent of £3.38, a difference of just 7 p. In conditions like these, with nights still below freezing but mild days, the heat pump and the gas boiler run at broadly similar daily costs.

Figure 5. Flow temperature and COP on 5 January 2025. The dashed red line marks the Q1 break-even COP of 3.73 (spark gap 3.92 × 0.95).

With a milder mean outdoor temperature than on the coldest day, the required flow temperatures are lower and the COP improves during the setback periods, reaching the limit of 8 during light heating runs around midday. The morning and evening ramp-ups still push the flow temperature towards 60 °C, temporarily depressing the COP. The day’s SCOP of 3.49 remains below the Q1 break-even COP of 3.73, so at this point of the heating season the unit energy costs still favour gas; it is the gas standing charge that offsets the difference on the day, and the milder days of the rest of the year that tip the annual balance in favour of the heat pump.

This day compares less favourably with gas than the constant 5 °C day in Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Heating, where smooth operation was 30% cheaper than gas. Here, the DHW and defrost slots leave less time for heating, so the heat is delivered at higher power; the planner has no flow temperature cap; and the COP follows the hourly outdoor temperature, which fell below freezing overnight.

Mild Spring Day

The mild spring day selected was 22 April 2025, with a mean outdoor temperature of 9.8 °C and a minimum of 3.6 °C. No defrost was required; only DHW slots interrupted the heating schedule.

Figure 6. Indoor temperature, set-point, outdoor temperature, and heat delivered on 22 April 2025.

The heat pump delivered 17.8 kWh of space heat, consuming 4.22 kWh of electricity at a SCOP of 4.21. At the Q2 rate of 27.03 p/kWh, the space-heating cost was £1.14, with £0.77 for DHW, giving a total heat pump cost of £1.92. The gas equivalent was £2.41, a saving of £0.49 on this day.

Figure 7. Flow temperature and COP on 22 April 2025. The dashed red line marks the Q2 break-even COP of 3.67 (spark gap 3.87 × 0.95).

At milder outdoor temperatures, the reduced heat demand requires lower flow temperatures, and the COP improves accordingly. On this day, the COP was at the model’s cap of 8 during most of the overnight setback and throughout the daytime setback, when the flow temperature is barely above the indoor temperature. The morning ramp-up, when higher power is needed to bring the house from about 18 °C back towards 19 °C, temporarily raised the flow temperature and reduced the COP, but the day’s SCOP of 4.21 was well above the Q2 break-even COP of 3.67.

Radiator Upgrade

As established in Radiator Upgrades [2], replacing all radiators with higher-output models raises the radiator constant of the house from K = 71.2 W/K1.2 to K = 93.5 W/K1.2, at an estimated cost of about £2,000 for materials, a rough estimate; I would do the work myself because of the particular requirements for running the pipes. The larger surface area allows the same heat output at a lower flow temperature, which in turn raises the COP on every heating day. The full-year simulation was repeated with K = 93.5 W/K1.2, with all other parameters unchanged.

Table 2. Full-year simulation with the current radiators (K = 71.2) and with the upgraded ones (K = 93.5). The extra £43.67 saved each year pays back a £2,000 upgrade in 46 years.

The space-heating SCOP rises from 3.85 to 4.24, and the annual heat pump cost falls from £734.70 to £691.03, while the gas cost is unaffected; the extra annual saving of £43.67 pays back the estimated upgrade cost of £2,000 in approximately 46 years.

Figure 8. Annual overview with upgraded radiators (K = 93.5 W/K1.2). The cost gap between heat pump and gas widens slightly compared with the current radiator installation, but the shape of the curves is otherwise similar.

The conclusion is consistent with the design-temperature analysis: the upgrade raises the SCOP and reduces running costs, but the savings are too small relative to the capital cost to justify the investment on economic grounds alone. Whether it is worthwhile for other reasons (longer system life, quieter operation at lower flow temperatures, or a margin of comfort on the coldest days) is a separate question.

Indoor Temperature Profile

The indoor temperature profile produced by a heat pump controller differs noticeably from that of a gas boiler operated in the conventional way. A gas boiler is typically run at maximum power to recover quickly from a deep night setback, so the house spends part of the morning warming up, part of the evening cooling down, and the night well below the daytime temperature. The heat pump model here lets the indoor temperature drift down only to about 17.5–18 °C during the night, brings it steadily back towards 19 °C before the morning routine, and keeps it close to that level across both comfort periods. In practice this is likely to feel more even and less draughty. A secondary benefit of the smaller overnight temperature swing is reduced condensation: when indoor surfaces stay above the dew point throughout the night, the moisture cycling that drives condensation on cold walls and windows is attenuated. This is a further argument for continuous low-temperature heating over intermittent high-temperature heating.

Conclusion

Running a heat pump for both space heating and DHW in Cambridge during 2025 would have cost approximately £735 against a gas equivalent of £849, a saving of around £114 with no radiator changes. Essentially all of that saving comes from eliminating the gas standing charge; that the unit energy costs alone are roughly on a par reflects the 2025 spark gap rather than a limitation of the technology. The payback of about 16 years in Convincing Numbers to Install a Heat Pump for Domestic Heating rests on a heat-pump tariff (OVO, £0.15/kWh); at the 2025 price caps, £114 a year would take about 40 years to recover the same £4.5k. The annual planner does not cap the flow temperature; with the smoother, low-flow-temperature operation shown in Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Heating, the saving would likely be larger, so the £114 is a conservative figure. A radiator upgrade to K = 93.5 W/K1.2 would improve the space-heating SCOP from 3.85 to 4.24 and reduce the heat pump bill by a further £44, but at an estimated cost of £2,000 in materials the payback period of roughly 46 years makes it difficult to justify on running-cost grounds. Time-of-use tariffs such as Octopus Cosy are not explored in this annual simulation; on the single winter day of Quantitative Analysis of Dynamic Heat Pump Operation for Domestic Heating, however, the April 2026 Cosy rates brought no advantage over smooth operation.

The results are sensitive to the ratio of electricity to gas prices. Within 2025 alone, the quarterly spark gap ranged from 3.87 to 4.19. Had the whole year been priced at the most favourable quarter (Q2), the simulated heat pump would have saved about £143 against gas; at the least favourable (Q4), about £88. Excluding the gas standing charge, the heat pump would have been £24 cheaper and £36 dearer than gas, respectively, and the payback of the radiator upgrade would have been 43 and 45 years.

So when am I going to get a heat pump? Well, on the 2025 record the economic case is borderline: a heat pump would have run at roughly the cost of gas, the saving being essentially the gas standing charge, and a small shift in the spark gap moves the result either way. With the cheap fabric measures done and the existing radiators adequate for most of the winter, the case now rests on energy prices and installation cost rather than on the house itself.

My final note on this series concerns two factors for the adoption of heat pumps in the UK:

  1. compact, self-contained internal units that are simple to install, like appliances (and cheap), and
  2. control software that keeps flow temperatures low and can exploit time-of-use tariffs where the rate differential is large enough.

Notes

  1. Capital expenditure: This analysis focuses purely on operating expenditure, i.e. the operating cost of meeting all heating needs with a heat pump instead of a gas boiler, assuming one had either system in place. If one started with no system, or needed to replace it anyway, the capital expenditure over the expected lifetime would also have to be taken into account. There, some work remains to be done, as in my view many installations are made too expensive by unnecessary complexity. See Impediments to UK Heat Pump Adoption and Possible Solutions for more details. ↩
  2. High COPs at small temperature lift: The COP estimate is a fixed fraction of the Carnot efficiency, which grows without bound as the lift between outdoor air and flow temperature shrinks. Real heat pumps do not achieve this: at very small lift and light load the compressor cannot modulate down far enough and cycles, and auxiliary loads such as fans, pumps and controls dominate the electrical input. The model therefore caps the COP at 8 as a practical upper limit. The cap matters only when there is little heat demand: about 7% of the annual space heat (471 kWh of 6,980 kWh) is delivered at the cap, mostly on days with a mean outdoor temperature above 5 °C; without the cap, the annual space-heating SCOP would be 3.87 instead of 3.85. ↩
