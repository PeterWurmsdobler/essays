Rectifying Some Misunderstanding of Atmosphere and Weather

Recalibrating my mental model on the atmosphere, the weather and the proportions involved.

‘Lovely day, isn’t it?’ — ‘Indeed, though some rain is forecast later this evening’. In many countries of the world, and particularly in the UK, the weather is a common topic; it demonstrates how humanity is subject to a complex global system, so difficult to predict, let alone be controlled. The purpose of this post is to address and rectify a misconception of weather with respect to the water cycle: moisture from the oceans is transported over large distances above land to bring rain, irrespective of the vegetation underneath (partly due to the misrepresentation of proportions).

Atmosphere & Weather

First I would like to share a few thoughts on atmosphere & weather, from a simple model as taught in school, one with distorted proportions, to a more complex representation, with rectified proportions.

The Simple Model

Speaking of myself, despite better knowledge about the numbers involved, my model or image of atmosphere and weather is influenced by diagrams that show the different atmospheric layers out of proportion with respect to the size of the planet. The diameter of the Earth is about 12,742 km (7,918 miles) and the outermost layers of the atmosphere reach about 10,000 km (6,214 miles), i.e. the magnitude of Earth’s diameter. Weather, however, occurs only in the troposphere, the lowest layer of Earth’s atmosphere, up to a height that varies by latitude, from about 7 km (4 miles) over the poles to 20 km (12 miles) at the equator; on average the weather layer is roughly 1/1000 of the Earth’s diameter, i.e. a thin film as we shall see later.

Figure 1. Carruthers Atmospheric Layers (NASA, 2025) showing the layers of the atmosphere: the Troposphere (0–12 km), where weather occurs; the Stratosphere (12–50 km), containing the ozone layer; the Mesosphere (50–85 km), where most meteors burn up; the Thermosphere (85–600 km), with increasing temperatures; and the outermost Exosphere (600–10,000 km), where gases escape into space [Image-1].

An important aspect of the weather is of course the hydrological cycle, or water vapour cycle, the cycle responsible for precipitation, quite often depicted like in Figure 2 (and this is perhaps the source of misunderstandings): a horizontal extent of say 1,000 km (621 miles), stretching from an ocean over land to some mountains; vertically, water vapour rises over the ocean up to great heights, what looks like 1,000 km (621 miles), too; this vapour is then transported all the way inland over the entire distance resulting in precipitation at the end of its journey. Eventually water will run off back to the ocean; a simple cycle.

Figure 2. Hydrological cycle (water vapour cycle) of water through ocean evaporation, transported over land, precipitating and finally run-off towards the ocean again [Image-2].

What is misleading in this presentation? For starters: scale. Rain clouds exist mostly at an altitude of about 1,000 m (3,280 feet), i.e. 1/1000 of the distance shown, 1,000 km (621 miles). What is the consequence for the understanding of weather? Due to the fact that distance and altitude are depicted out of proportion, a false idea is planted in our minds: moisture rises over the ocean and is transported by prevailing winds far into land and continents. The presentation alludes to the false model that moisture transport happens nearly in the Exosphere, but most importantly, it happens independently of what’s underneath, be it vegetation or desert, grassland or forests; according to the common presentations, moisture transport is invariant to vegetation; back to that later.

Admittedly, looking up into the sky, by night or day, gauging the distance is not easy to the human eye; the atmosphere appears nearly endless when in fact it finishes, as far as weather is concerned, at 10 km (32,800 feet) altitude which is about the distance to the horizon when I am standing on the roof of my house, 8 m (26 feet) above ground. For instance, an aircraft flying at 10 km (32,800 feet) appears somewhat farther away than if it was horizontally 10 km (6.2 miles) away on the ground. I have the impression that we somewhat equate metres in altitude to kilometres in horizontal distance: 1,000 m altitude feels like 1,000 km horizontal distance, a misjudgement of proportions by a factor of 1000 leading to a common misconception about weather.

A Model in Proportion

As mentioned earlier, the outer layer of the atmosphere goes up to 10,000 km, i.e. nearly the diameter of Planet Earth; however, Earth’s limb — the boundary between the ground and space — goes up to the so-called Kármán line at 100 km above sea level as shown by Figure 3, taken from the International Space Station at an altitude of 400 km (250 miles), not very high up in space at all. As seen in Figure 3, the troposphere which goes up to 10 km occupies only the lower 1/10th of the blueish hue that marks the boundary of the ‘visible’ atmosphere (100 km); clouds are formed at about 1 km, i.e. 1/100 of the height of the Kármán line.

Figure 3. Earth’s limb — the boundary between the ground and space — as seen from the International Space Station, the dividing line between the atmosphere and outer space is called the Kármán line (100 km above sea level) [Image-3].

Again, weather happens in a very thin sliver of the atmosphere, up to about 10 km, perhaps even within 1–2 km; in comparison to the Earth’s diameter of 12,742 km, that is very little, about 1/1000 of the Earth’s diameter. The troposphere is more like a cling film (10 μm) around a cherry (12 mm diameter). Or, consider a ball with 1 m diameter: weather happens within a layer of 1 mm thickness, possibly the extent of the surface roughness of said ball; or think of a ball one foot in diameter: weather happens in a 1/100 inch layer. Another one: if the Earth was a baseball, weather happens between the ripples of the rubber surface.

To help with understanding proportions, let’s look at the volumes of the Earth, all air in the atmosphere and all the water on the planet. The troposphere is where most of the air is contained; all this air in the atmosphere could fit, at sea-level density, in a small ball next to the planet as shown in Figure 4. The amount of water on Earth’s surface could also be captured in a single ball; the ocean it seems is merely a drop of water spread out over planet Earth like a thin layer of wet paint on a billiard ball; that’s all there is (average depth of the oceans is about 4,000 m, i.e. about 1/1600 of the Earth’s radius). And mind you, of all that water only a tiny fraction is fresh water that supports life on land.

The total volume of Earth’s water (smaller sphere) and the total volume of atmospheric mass compressed to sea-level density (larger sphere), shown to scale alongside the planet. Atmosphere & Weather Revisited

With a better understanding of the quantities and dimensions involved let’s revisit some concepts of atmosphere and weather.

Atmosphere Revisited

There are perhaps a few aspects that are relevant to understanding the atmosphere and the water vapour cycle: atmospheric pressure and temperature profile as a function of altitude. Within the first 10,000 m (32,800 feet), pressure drops from about 1,000 mbar to 300 mbar and the temperature drops from about 18 °C to −50 °C [1]. Therefore, it is quite obvious that weather does take place only in the lowest layer of the atmosphere, the troposphere, and even there, mostly in its lower parts where rain clouds are formed. Commercial aircraft usually travel above the clouds, say at 36,000 feet, flight level 360; also note that the space shuttle or the international space station are only ‘grazing’ the atmosphere at a mere 400 km above sea level; therefore, all of humanity’s ‘space travel’ is actually very close to home and not far into space at all.

Figure 5. The 1962 U.S. Standard Atmosphere Model. Vertical profiles showcasing temperature variations, atmospheric pressure gradients, and air density relative to geometric altitude [Image-5].

From Bay of Biscay to Vienna

The climate of my native Austria is mostly determined by westerly winds, bringing moisture in the form of rain and snow. I do even remember from listening to weather forecasts that most precipitation supposedly originates from moisture evaporating off the Atlantic Ocean; and quite often the Bay of Biscay was mentioned as the area of origin for rainy weather. Following the simple model about moisture being transported from oceans inland, the consequence would be that water vapour rises over the Bay of Biscay to form clouds; they are then transported all across Europe to Austria to bring rain, independently of any vegetation, and high above all land features as shown in the map and elevation profile below.

Figure 6. Map and elevation profile from the Bay of Biscay to Vienna, Austria, the profile exaggerated in height; terrain heights from SRTM [2], map from Natural Earth [3].

However, given the distance (1,450 km = 900 miles) and altitude involved (about 2,000 m = 6,500 feet), the elevation profile and hence weather layer should be rather presented in proper proportion: a very thin line as shown in Figure 7, admittedly not very practical.

Figure 7. The elevation profile from the Bay of Biscay to Vienna, Austria, in proportion: 1,450 km long and at most about 2,000 m high, a ratio of about 1/730. This means, for a 150 mm wide line, the profile is about 0.2 mm high (or 1/100 of an inch), i.e. a very thin sliver indeed.

It seems to be obvious that, on top of prevailing winds, weather has to be in constant interaction with the land and vegetation underneath. That said, it is now very clear to me that local weather pockets can exist easily, partly driven by geography, which in turn may influence and create special vegetation, which again can maintain a certain micro climate. People flying gliders do know that, too, as some small landscape features, e.g. a forest edge, a town or a ridge, can create strong uplifts. Such a feature only needs to be of the same order of magnitude of altitude up to which weather occurs, 1,000–2,000 m, i.e. a feature a few kilometres in horizontal extent.

Given the perspective on the relationship between the altitude of cloud forming (around 1,000 m) and geographic distance (around 1,000 km), it is very unlikely that moisture would be transported irrespective of what’s underneath, the common misconception; a better model for long distance moisture transport is needed: the Biotic Pump provides one; more on its details in The Biotic Pump — How Forests Drive Continental Rainfall.
