Reducing Cognitive Load in Reading Piano Sheet Music --- Continued

Simplicior, a plugin for MuseScore that aims to make reading sheet music a bit simpler in various configurable ways.

This essay is the continuation of Reducing the Cognitive Load in Reading Piano Sheet Music, which has received more attention than I had anticipated. Feedback fell mostly into two camps: some readers can relate to my difficulties in reading music, recognise issues with the established notation system, and even welcome an attempt to improve it; others suggest in essence practice, practice, practice, stating that the established European notation system is as complex as it needs to be because it tries to communicate the rich complexity of music for a wide range of genres and for a wide range of instruments, too. Gaining proficiency in reading music needs time such that the brain recognises patterns rather than sequentially processing individual notes.

My intention was and is still not to completely change a notation system that evolved over about 1000 years, beginning with Guido of Arezzo, let alone invent a new system (there are plenty of those around, mostly failed). My proposal addresses first and foremost my own shortcomings, a reading disability for sheet music (perhaps some form of dyslexia); reading music is harder for me than it is for others. I do admit that I would need much more practice to reach a level where pattern recognition emerges (comparable to the effort of learning to read English). The objective here is to make reading easier, to reflect equal temperament tuning, and to accept the piano as target instrument, all in order to simply allow:

What you see is what you play

Simplicior

As announced in Reducing the Cognitive Load in Reading Piano Sheet Music, I ventured into writing a so-called ‘plugin’ for the well-established music notation software MuseScore [1]. Working on the plugin, which I called Simplicior [2], necessitated learning new programming languages (QML and JavaScript), which was interesting in its own right. The implementation phase revealed a few subtleties and technical challenges that needed addressing; they were discussed on ‘Facilitating reading sheet music’ [3] and related topics on MuseScore’s forum posts [4]. The remainder of this essay elaborates on some features of Simplicior [2] and explains a few concepts beyond what is contained in the repository’s README file [2].

Figure 1. Graphical User Interface of the Simplicior MuseScore plugin.

As stated on the GitHub repository Simplicior [2], the goal of Simplicior is to make reading sheet music a bit simpler, less complex, and hence sight reading, too; any combination of the following options is possible:

  • use only one clef class (treble) throughout with adaptive octave offsets to minimise ledger lines and keep notes mostly within a staff while avoiding too many octave transitions;
  • use only notes that represent the 12 tones in a chromatic scale of equal temperament, no double flats or double sharps, i.e. enharmonic notes;
  • use only the open/atonal signature, equivalent to C major, to represent a score as if it was written in C major and present all notes outside C major as accidentals;
  • primary notes (white keys on the piano, equivalent to C major) that are altered in pitch through sharps or flats (and become black keys on the piano) change note head colour and/or shape, but keep their position: this applies to C♯/D♭, D♯/E♭, F♯/G♭, G♯/A♭, A♯/B♭;
  • alternatively, primary notes that are altered in pitch through sharps or flats and end up being tonally equivalent are represented by one unique note head (and colour), and set at a common position: this applies to C♯=D♭, D♯=E♭, F♯=G♭, G♯=A♭, A♯=B♭;
  • optionally, if sharp or flat symbols (as well as the natural symbols in a key) become redundant due to colour or shape encoding, do not show these symbols ♯/♭ any more.

The following goes into more detail on some features and shows some possible combinations without exhausting all permutations.

Adaptive Treble Clef

The objective here is to only use a treble clef for both the left and the right hand staff, as well as offset the clef by one or two octaves such that notes fall mostly within the staff while avoiding changing octaves too often. This can be reworded: Suppose I have a list of bars where each bar contains notes; each note is characterised by its pitch as MIDI number from 0 to 127. I would now like to assign a reference pitch to each bar where the reference pitch is one in the following list: 47, 59, 71, 83, 95 (the MIDI pitch of Bs, the middle line in treble clef for consecutive octaves). The pitch assignment should optimise the following criteria:

  • the sum of all distances of all notes to the respective reference pitch (i.e. the B in each clef) for all bars should be minimised;
  • changes in reference pitch (i.e. octave changes) from one bar to the next bar in the sequence should be penalised.

The Simplicior [2] plugin uses dynamic programming to generate an optimal sequence of clefs (treble clef and its octaves) given a configurable cost for the transition. No cost would mean that clefs would follow the notes up and down from bar to bar; a high cost would result in an average clef that satisfies all bars equally. For instance, the following bar:

Enforcing Enharmonics

Modern Western music uses a chromatic scale at equal temperament where there are 12 semi-tones in an octave with the ratio between two consecutive tones being the 12th root of 2. Given a certain key signature containing sharps or flats, raising or lowering such a note by another semi-tone does in theory create a double sharp (written as a single x-shaped sign) or double flat (♭♭), respectively; this may be important for understanding music. For playing along the ‘what you see is what you play’ mantra, however, these notes should simply be resolved to the enharmonic equivalent as follows, which only leaves: C, C♯/D♭, D, D♯/E♭, E, F, F♯/G♭, G, G♯/A♭, A, A♯/B♭, B as available notes. 

F♭♭ → E♭, F♯♯ → G, F♭ → E
C♭♭ → B♭, C♯♯ → D, C♭ → B
G♭♭ → F,  G♯♯ → A, E♯ → F
D♭♭ → C,  D♯♯ → E, B♯ → C
A♭♭ → G,  A♯♯ → B
E♭♭ → D,  E♯♯ → F♯
B♭♭ → A,  B♯♯ → C♯

Atonal Signature

A piece of music is usually written in a key which positions the musical body (its notes, chords and melodies) on an absolute tonal range rooted in its tonic. In an equal temperament the relative distance between notes will not change with transpositions; in absolute terms, however, the overall perception may well change as instruments, including human voices, change characteristics with absolute pitch after all. That said, even if the key signature is taken away and all notes are represented with accidentals in order to keep the pitch of all notes the same, the implicit key remains. Hence presenting all non-white keys as accidentals may help reading as one does not need to remember the key signature and what notes are flats or sharps, again following the ‘what you see is what you play’ principle.

NOTE: If a piece was written in a certain key, there are nevertheless many other ‘keys’ present as chords, dominants, subdominants, etc. Despite being converted into an atonal representation, those keys, or chords in their keys, still persist.

Encoding Non-Naturals

Whether a score is represented in a certain key with implicit flats/sharps (i.e. notes are not shown explicitly as such and flats/sharps are inferred), or as an atonal piece with explicit flats/sharps, it may help to indicate flats/sharps graphically in any combination of colour and shape.

Figure 6. Ludwig van Beethoven, Sonate No. 8, ‘Pathétique’, Opus 13, 1st Movement, 1st bar, treble clef only and presented in an open/atonal key with all non-naturals being explicit and shape coded.

Figure 7. Ludwig van Beethoven, Sonate No. 8, ‘Pathétique’, Opus 13, 1st Movement, 1st bar, treble clef only and presented in an open/atonal key with all non-naturals being explicit and shape & colour coded.

Going a bit further, if the sharp/flat nature is already encoded in the colour and/or shape, in a certain key signature or in an atonal representation, the accidental symbol for flat ♭ and/or sharp ♯ becomes redundant and can be dropped altogether.

Figure 8. Ludwig van Beethoven, Sonate No. 8, ‘Pathétique’, Opus 13, 1st Movement, 1st bar, treble clef only and presented in an open/atonal key with all non-naturals being explicit and shape & colour coded, but with no sharp or flat signs as they are redundant information in this combination.

If a unique shape is chosen for both flats and sharps and the position is chosen to be the same for enharmonic notes, C♯=D♭, D♯=E♭, F♯=G♭, G♯=A♭, A♯=B♭, then all enharmonics are presented with the same note head at that very position. Colour can help to see whether the note was a sharp or a flat.

Figure 9. Ludwig van Beethoven, Sonate No. 8, ‘Pathétique’, Opus 13, 1st Movement, 1st bar, treble clef only and presented in an open/atonal key with all non-naturals being explicit and colour & shape coded, using the same diamond shape for flats and sharps, but offset positions, no sharp or flat signs.

Conclusion

In summary, Simplicior allows performing certain simplifications of the musical notation system. Since many musicians read scores on an electronic tablet these days, they could even choose the most convenient way that works for them. I would not like to impose what the best way is; but I would say this: the best way to present sheet music will be the one that allows reading and performing the music most efficiently.
