Switched Transport Network for Personal Mobility Pods

The dream of travelling in our own personal space, the Personal Mobility Pod, routed through a public transport network of trains.

Recently my family and I travelled from our home in Cambridge, UK, to Besançon, France, using mostly public means of transport. The journey took approximately 12 hours door to door for a distance of 550 miles (900 km) and comprised 6 legs: taxi to Cambridge station, Thameslink service to London St. Pancras, Eurostar to Lille Europe, short walk to Lille Flandres, Train à Grande Vitesse (TGV) to Besançon Franche-Comté TGV, and a short car journey to get to our final destination.

Figure 1. Train à Grande Vitesse (TGV) carrying personal mobility pods: from the side (top), and from above at a station (bottom), where a pod on its electric carrier slides into its compartment.

Overall it was a pleasant trip. In fact, I do not mind so much train journeys themselves, in particular when being comfortably seated in a TGV, experiencing a smooth ride at about 180 mph (~300 km/h). What is most annoying, however, is changing the means of transport, all the hassle that comes with it and all the time being lost in transition: queuing, waiting, border controls, carrying luggage, putting suitcases in luggage compartments, looking for your seats, taking your luggage out again, alighting the train, and more carrying and walking. If only we could travel in our own personal space, in a Personal Mobility Pod routed through a transport network.

In 1992, some 27 years ago, on a train journey in Austria, I thought about a concept involving this kind of personal pod travelling either locally on top of motorised carriers or, for long distance journeys, on top of trains. Since I did not find a solution for moving these carriers efficiently between homes and train stations, I did not give it any further thought. Nevertheless I kept the general idea in the back of my head. Only now, with the advent of autonomous vehicles a solution may finally become possible. In the following I would like to lay out some concepts combining the Personal Mobility Pod, autonomous vehicles and a train network.

Background and Origin

In the early 90s I used to travel overnight to project meetings in the West of Austria while living in Vienna, in the East of Austria. On a global scale both places are not too far apart, but still around 600 km which warranted a night train. The same train offered a service that allowed people to take their car with them on their journey, a service used mostly by business people. There was a bit of inconvenience of waiting with your car at the train terminal in Vienna, e.g. getting all cars in a single file, then driving them onto the train and making sure all drivers have boarded the train before the train could leave. The overnight journey in the sleeping compartment was fine but was followed by more hassle at the destination train station, e.g. waiting for everybody to get the car off the train, all in a single file. The advantage was, however, that on both sides of the journey you could use your personal car for the last mile or a bit more.

But why, so I thought, would you transport a heavy vehicle piggy back on the train when all you were interested in is taking your personal space with you, some kind of personal compartment, a personal pod? Why not just transport these pods on the trains? Why not use a shared or rented undercarriage to carry these pods locally on the last mile? Why not provide a system where the pods are pushed from the undercarriage onto a train, all in parallel, rather than in a single file. Why not offer such a service at all major train stations? Why not have some form of switched network that allows routing these pods over high speed train networks? Could this work similar to routing data packets in Internet connections (TCP/IP packet switched networks)?

Of course, there are many different needs for mobility: the daily commute to work, taking your children to school, the weekly shopping or a quick trip to the corner shop to get the pot of cream, the family holiday, etc. And for all these needs there are various possible modes of transport including walking, or a combination of those. I am sure there are statistics about their distribution of frequency, miles covered, energy spent and carbon footprint. All these mobility cases will have their own requirements for transport systems and no single means of transport will be able to cover all requirements for all mobility use cases. What I try to address with a switched transport network for personal mobility pods is the door-to-door trip over a longer distance for one or several people and their personal belongings, such as but not limited to a family trip or a business trip. For this kind of applications the advantages would be:

  • personal mobility pods allow passengers to stay in the same personal space for the entirety of the trip; this makes longer trips more acceptable, in particular as a family with small children and all that goes with it.
  • shared undercarriages at both ends of the trip allow travelling from home to the nearest pod terminal for departure, and the converse at the destination. It should even be possible to continue using the undercarriage on a rental basis at the destination if desired.
  • trains offer an energy efficient means of transport to cover large distances for a large number of passengers because the largest gain in energy efficiency is first in sharing the means of transport across a large number of people and second using an energy efficient means of transport (e.g. low rolling resistance); both together yield a small energy expenditure per person and distance travelled.

So how would all that work?

The Personal Mobility Pod

The future world of personal mobility will certainly include a wide range of means of transport, old ones such as trains, trams, underground, buses and bicycles, or new ones such as autonomous vehicles and various forms of micro mobility to cover short distances; the combination of walking, cycling, public means of transport and shared autonomous vehicles will be catering for most needs of personal mobility. The main driver for the development will need to be a suitable metric for economics such as the energy and carbon footprint per person and distance travelled or Alternative Measures of Progress. Once these metrics have found their way into market mechanisms it will be prohibitively expensive to own a car for individuals let alone SUVs. Capitalising on the advantage of sharing the means of transport, and by extension sharing the energy expenditure, autonomous vehicles will certainly cover a certain portion of the short and medium distance mobility market. Hence there will be no need for individually owned vehicles. But that is not all as this assumption does not acknowledge all drivers for car ownership.

People tend to own, and are likely to desire ownership of cars for all sorts of reasons. They are not necessarily irrational but can be explained rationally, e.g. status symbols or love for technology. Another reason is a practical one. As long as you do not have family using public means of transport, an autonomous vehicle such as a robot-taxi or an electric scooter is all acceptable. When you travel as a family, however, or just like to be in your personal space, then the combination of public means of transport, micro-mobility or autonomous vehicles may not meet all your mobility needs. In contrast, it may even be more hassle to travel as a family using all those means of transport. For this reason alone people do love owning their cars because they can keep a lot of personal belongings in the car. As a matter of fact, it would be quite tedious to keep these personal belongings in your house and load them into an autonomous vehicle, e.g. a baby seat, even if it features an ISOFIX mechanism. So owning something that is part of a transport infrastructure could be an attractive proposition.

Being at the centre of this concept, the personal mobility pod can be imagined as a car with its undercarriage stripped off; all that remains is a passenger space what in French is called habitable, the space inhabited by the passengers. There would be no bonnet, no engine, no drive train, no wheels. Starting with this approach, the design can be extended to accommodate different needs: it could be slightly taller than usual car interiors, or the seats could be facing each other for a 4-seat pod. There are many possibilities for personalisation as long as its footprint and envelope remains compatible with the undercarriage and the envisaged means of transport.

In addition to the physical envelope, the family of pods would need to meet certain requirements. First in terms of extent, there could be different size classes like with ISO shipping containers. Second, there needs to be a physical interface to the carriers, the undercarriage or trains. Third, there has to be an electrical interface, e.g. for power to the pod for air conditioning or any electrical equipment running in the pod. Fourth, there may have to be an interface for a drive-by-wire control of the undercarriage such that the combination of pod and undercarriage can be driven by a passenger on the last mile if needed. And last but not least, there will need to be some safety constraints on the pod. Other than this, anything is possible, hardwood flooring or carpets, designer couch, you name it.

There is still the small issue of comfort breaks and bathrooms. There are two scenarios. The pod may be used either locally for short distances on an undercarriage, or for longer distances on a dedicated train carriage. As for the former, similar to ordinary cars, there is no need to integrate bathroom facilities in a pod. As for the latter, see the section on the specialised carriages for trains how bathroom and other facilities are addressed for long distance journeys.

By virtue of not having any moving parts, engine, drive train nor wheels, the personal mobility pod can be quite cheap. It is in essence only a box (perhaps even made of wood) with some soft furnishing. Most of these pods would be owned by families. Instead of parking a car in your front garden, it would be a pod, consuming less space than a full size car. A cynic might say it would look like a builder’s skip or the remainder of a neglected car in a deprived neighbourhood where the wheels have been stolen from the parked car. But it does not need to look like that, it could look quite swish. Alternatively, the pod could be put in a small garage.

As for people who do not have front gardens or garages, who live in flats for instance, an underground parking could provide space for pods. Alternatively there could be pod storage facilities where people can keep their pods. There could also be public pods available for rent from such places. If needed, the pod would be brought to them at the location needed using appropriate autonomous undercarriages and a routing network.

The (Autonomous) Undercarriage

This undercarriage consists in essence of an electric drive train and 4 wheels, batteries, a drive-by-wire interface and a mechanism to receive a personal mobility pod, akin to skateboard design by Israeli company Ree [1]. The electrical drive train is well understood these days, with innovations that open up more possibilities such as the Protean360 [2], an ‘advanced electric-drive corner module designed for next-generation urban mobility pods’. As far as the batteries are concerned, they only need to provide power for local trips and hence do not need to be too big and onerous. With regards to the docking mechanism, there should be ways to automatically pick up and secure the pods on the undercarriage with minimal human intervention.

With regards to the driver interface, when I thought about that some 27 years ago I assumed that there will have to be a mechanical means to connect a steering column and all mechanical controls between the personal mobility pod and the undercarriage. Technology has moved on since and there are several possibilities to control and drive the entire vehicle:

  • Human controlled: using the drive-by-wire interface, the personal mobility pod can be fitted with some kind of controls that allows controlling the steering wheel angle, acceleration and brakes, perhaps as a pull-out from the pod similar to a conventional steering wheel and pedals, or perhaps more similar to a moped handle bar or joy stick.
  • Fully autonomous: sensors and autonomous software stack needed to drive the undercarriage would be fitted. Since only a small number of such undercarriages is needed in comparison to the number of pods, the undercarriages could be quite sophisticated as their cost is shared and they would more or less operate 24/7.
  • System controlled: the undercarriage could be controlled by a traffic guidance system on dedicated lanes, e.g. at the railway station or in limited operating domains.

The undercarriages could be owned by a fleet operator, or by the transport network operator. In some instances, the undercarriage could be owned by owners of pods, too, if it makes economic sense. For example, the personal undercarriage could use higher capacity batteries, or more powerful electric motors. However, there would be not much need for personal ownership I would think, as the undercarriage would be used only locally from time to time for short trips, or a trip to the next transport network terminal.

The Pod Carrying Train Carriage

For long distance journeys the pod would be travelling on dedicated carriages. These would be a hybrid between passenger and cargo carriages, a flat bed carriage with a roof and wide sliding doors or shutters on one side, but closed with windows on the other. Pods would sit on the carriage similar to ISO containers, all mounted along their longitudinal axis and allowing enough space for a corridor on the carriage. Given the Loading Gauge, the maximum width of the carriage, being in the order of 3.15 m and assuming that pods would be as large as normal passenger cars, i.e. in the order of 1.7 m, this should leave enough space for a corridor running along the mounted pods. The corridor would allow passengers to get off their pods and access shared facilities such as toilets or a restaurant car. It may also be essential for safety reasons having such a corridor as an escape route.

The Switched Transport Network

Continental Europe still enjoys a decent rail network, perhaps due to the fact that it is mostly state owned and maintained with a long-term view which is often the case with publicly owned infrastructure. Relying on such a national network, major cities could have terminals where the undercarriage pod combination can drive up onto a parallel lane to the platform, or the automatic network will drive the vehicle to the allocated location next to the rail track where the train is expected to arrive. On arrival of the train, a robot will move the pod from the undercarriage onto the train. After the train has departed, undercarriages are driven to underground parking of the fleet operator, waiting for their next assignment.

Since it is unlikely that a direct train service will run between all cities, some routing has to take place. It is in essence a logistics problem. Knowing the trip details of all scheduled journeys, with departure cities and times as well as all destinations, it should be possible to work out a routing schedule in real time given the train service time schedules. The latter may be mostly static, but could adapt to demand. Apart from the computational problem to be solved, which I am sure is not insurmountable, there is the practical problem of shifting the pods between trains. Well, I could imagine some kind of traditional shunting, i.e. sorting items of rolling stock into complete trains where several pods on a single carriage have the same origin and destination. In addition, if there are pods of different origin or destination within a carriage, some mechanisms can be devised where the pods are shifted between carriages being on two adjacent rail tracks at some shunting stations. The whole process can be optimised and would not take longer than changing trains in a traditional sense.

As the loading, unloading and shunting process becomes more automated and sophisticated, the pod terminal service can be rolled out to smaller cities and towns in due course. Consequently, there will be even less need for individual car ownership to accommodate long distance family trips. The switched pod network may even include airports and air travel; perhaps that’s pushing it a bit. Admittedly, the network would need investment on a large scale, for a very long term, on a national level, or perhaps transnational level. The European Union would be a good candidate, or China.

Alternative Journey Cambridge — Besançon

So how would the journey mentioned at the beginning of the article look like using a conceivable alternative transport system? As a family in Cambridge, we won’t be needing a car. We may own a personal mobility pod as we do like to travel with our stuff locally or in Europe to see family or bring wine and cheese from France. With a switched pod transport network in place, I would expect the pod (with us already in it) being picked up by an autonomous undercarriage at a convenient time of the day and taken to Cambridge South, a new railway station accommodating pod travel. Arriving there just in time, the pod would be moved onto a carriage to somewhere in or around London. There, the carriage would be shunted onto a train to France, and perhaps somewhere else in France if needed. I would hope that within 12 hours the pod would arrive at Besançon Mouillère, the old train station adapted for receiving pods. From there a local undercarriage will take us to our final destination. It would have been a pleasant journey, might have even happened over night, and we would not even have had the impression of having lost time.
