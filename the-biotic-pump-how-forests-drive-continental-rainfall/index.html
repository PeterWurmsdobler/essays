The Biotic Pump --- How Forests Drive Continental Rainfall

A model to explain how moisture is transported from oceans to continents, relying on forests and forming the basis of a stable climate.

In Rectifying Some Misunderstanding of Atmosphere and Weather I pointed out that the simple model of prevailing winds transporting moisture from oceans far into continents is not satisfactory; a different model is needed. The Biotic Pump Theory [1], proposed by Russian physicists Anastassia Makarieva and Victor Gorshkov, offers an explanation for moisture transport into continental interiors far from oceanic moisture sources. This theory challenges the traditional view that rainfall arrives independently of the vegetation underneath; instead it suggests that healthy forests actively ‘pump’ atmospheric moisture from oceans deep into continental landmasses through transpiration and evaporation processes. As I am not an atmospheric physicist, I can only report this theory, which remains debated. If it holds, it has implications for understanding Earth’s climate system and for the role of intact forest ecosystems in maintaining the water cycles upon which human civilisation depends.

The Continental Moisture Problem

Terrestrial life, depending on the availability of water, faces a fundamental challenge for all species on land: under gravity, water inevitably flows from land to ocean through rivers and run-off. To maintain adequate moisture for life to flourish, this gravitational water loss must be continuously compensated by atmospheric moisture transport from ocean to land in the form of precipitation, i.e. a balance of water from land-ocean and ocean-land must be maintained.

As pointed out in Biotic pump of atmospheric moisture as driver of the hydrological cycle on land [2], atmospheric moisture transport weakens exponentially with distance from the coast, with a characteristic decay length ranging from 100 km to 800 km and an average of 600 km, as shown in Figure 1. Empirical data from non-forested regions (North Australia, Northeast China, West Africa, Argentina, and North America) consistently demonstrate this exponential decline in precipitation as distance from moisture sources increases. This 600 km limitation is enough to keep the UK mostly wet; to me it is now understandable that most moisture brought in from the West will have been lost by the time it reaches East Anglia.

Figure 1. Exponential drop of precipitation P (mm/year on logarithmic scale) on distance x (km) from the source of atmospheric moisture on non-forested territories [Image-1].

But what about land masses larger than 600 km? The observed physical constraint presents a paradox: if moisture transport is limited to several hundred kilometres, how do extensive forests thrive thousands of kilometres inland in the Amazon basin, Equatorial Africa, and Siberia? The answer lies in the forests themselves: they are not merely beneficiaries of rainfall but active drivers of the moisture cycle; forests are part of the solution, a result of co-evolution of vegetation and precipitation, an aspect which is very important in the maintenance of that cycle.

The Physics of the Biotic Pump

The following tries to present a few concepts presented in and extracted from Anastassia Makarieva’s extensive publications, [3] and [1].

Evaporative Force

As most people who have walked in forests during a hot summer day know, the air between the trees is refreshing in two ways. First, the transpiration of forests, i.e. the evaporation of water through the leaves, requires energy which is extracted from the environment; photosynthesis, powered by the sun, absorbs CO2 and exhales O2, but it is the latent heat of transpiration that cools. Second, as most people can easily experience when waving wet hands in the air, the evaporation of water cools your hands down. When water turns from liquid to vapour the so-called evaporation enthalpy (2,260 kJ/kg) is required and taken from the environment making the air cooler; this energy is stored as ‘latent heat’ in water vapour.

Moist air (dry air plus water vapour, both from transpiration and evaporation) is subject to an upwards driving force, the so-called ‘evaporative force’. This force arises from the non-equilibrium vertical distribution of atmospheric water vapour in relation to the temperature lapse rate of the atmosphere, the rate at which atmospheric temperature decreases with increasing altitude (6.5 °C/km). The evaporative force drives both vertical ascension of air masses and horizontal air movement from regions of weak evaporation toward regions of intensive evaporation as shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Principle of biotic pump: moist air from ocean flows into land, rises and in combination with vegetation transpiration rises further until condensation at higher altitude; resulting pressure drop draws in more air and sustains the cycle with dry air flowing back to the ocean [Image-2].

In other words, the pump works through a self-sustaining cycle:

1.
Enhanced Evapotranspiration: Forests, with their high leaf area index (the total leaf surface area divided by ground projection area), maintain evaporation rates that exceed oceanic evaporation.
2.
Water Condensation: As water vapour rises and cools, it condenses. According to Makarieva and colleagues, since water vapour occupies more volume than liquid water, this phase change reduces atmospheric pressure above the forest.
3.
Moisture Influx: The pressure differential draws more moist air from the ocean toward the forest interior, creating winds that transport even more moisture inland.
4.
Sustained Cycle: Precipitation replenishes soil moisture, enabling continued transpiration and maintaining the pressure gradient that drives further moisture influx.

The Tug-of-War of Evaporation

Healthy forests can perform this moisture transport very well because the evaporation and transpiration with resulting pressure drop above forests is more pronounced than the evaporation over oceans. Therefore, more moist air is being drawn in, yielding more precipitation, then transpiration, etc; a virtuous cycle is created and maintained, according to the theory. In contrast, if the vegetation is poor, less transpiration and evaporation occurs, leading to an inversion of the air flow as shown below, the ‘tug-of-war’ between ocean and forest as explained in The tug-of-war between forests and oceans [4]. Healthy forests with closed canopies generate evaporation fluxes that exceed oceanic evaporation, pulling moist air inland, whereas degraded or sparse vegetation cannot maintain this pressure differential, allowing the natural exponential decay pattern to dominate and potentially reversing the airflow as shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3. Tug of war between land and sea: vegetation decides the flow of moist air as evapotranspiration over forest wins due to stronger pressure drop. (a) Deserts: evaporation on land is close to zero, so the low-level air moves from land to the ocean year round, thus ‘locking’ desert for moisture. (b) Winter monsoon: evaporation from the warmer oceanic surface is larger than evaporation from the colder land surface; the low-level air moves from land to the ocean. (c) Summer monsoon: evaporation from the warmer land surface is larger than evaporation from the colder oceanic surface; the low-level air moves from ocean to land. (d) Hadley circulation (trade winds): evaporation is more intensive on the equator, where the solar flux is larger than in the higher latitudes; low-level air moves towards the equator year round; seasonal displacements of the convergence zone follow the displacement of the area with maximum insolation. (e) Biotic pump of atmospheric moisture: evaporation fluxes regulated by natural forests exceed oceanic evaporation fluxes to the degree when the arising ocean-to-land fluxes of moist air become large enough to compensate losses of water to runoff in the entire river basin year round [Image-3].

Continental-Scale Moisture Transport

While the previous concept demonstrates how a say 600 km wide band close to the coast would draw in moisture, the real power in the biotic pump lies in how moisture is then passed on further inland.

Cascading Pump Cells

The biotic pump doesn’t operate as a single continental mechanism but as a cascade of interconnected ‘pumping cells’, based on the principles laid out in Why does air passage over forest yield more rain? Examining the coupling between rainfall, pressure, and atmospheric moisture content [5]. Coastal forests draw moisture from the ocean. As this moisture precipitates and is re-evaporated through transpiration, it creates conditions for the next inland cell to continue the process, functioning like a daisy chain of smaller pumps as shown in Figure 4. Prevailing winds provide the common velocity that carries moisture through this cascade across thousands of kilometres.

Figure 4. When water over coastal forests condenses, it lowers air pressure, creating winds that draw in more moist air from the oceans. That way a cascade of cycles of transpiration and condensation set up winds that transport moisture over a long distance.

Evidence from Major River Basins

Makarieva and colleagues see empirical evidence for the biotic pump theory, as pointed out in Revisiting forest impact on atmospheric water vapor transport and precipitation [6] with data from major river basins as shown in Figure 5:

  • Amazon Basin: Precipitation remains constant at approximately 2,300 mm/year across 2,800 km from coast to interior, showing no exponential decay.
  • Congo Basin: Similar patterns exist in Equatorial Africa, with stable precipitation around 1,900 mm/year across 2,300 km.
  • Yenisey Basin: In Siberia, precipitation actually increases inland from 430 mm/year at the Arctic Ocean to 800 mm/year 2,800 km south. According to the authors, this pattern cannot be explained by passive atmospheric circulation alone, given the region’s extreme distance from any major ocean and the low moisture content of Arctic air masses.

Figure 5. Prevailing winds in north and south hemisphere driving the biotic pump; white arrows indicate the direction of the wind: Am = Amazon, Co = Congo, Yel = Yenisey basin, etc [Image-4].

Seasonal Variation

The seasonal behaviour of the Eurasian boreal forest demonstrates the pump’s activity as shown in Revisiting forest impact on atmospheric water vapor transport and precipitation [6]. During summer months when vegetation actively transpires, moisture is transported deep into the continent from the Atlantic Ocean (precipitation nearly constant across the entire distance). In winter, when vegetation is dormant, this inland moisture transport largely ceases, and coastal precipitation remains near the coast (rapid decrease of precipitation as distance increases), all shown in Figure 6.

Figure 6. The Biotic pump in operation across the Eurasian boreal forest during the summer months when vegetation is operational and transpires moisture; very little moisture transport during the winter months [Image-5].

Climate Regulation

The biotic pump is involved in other processes affecting the climate.

Latent Heat Transport

According to the theory, the biotic pump contributes to planetary thermal regulation through latent heat dynamics:

1.
Heat Absorption: Evaporation requires energy (evaporation enthalpy), which is extracted from the environment and stored as latent heat in water vapour. This cools the forest surface.
2.
Vertical Heat Transport: As moist air rises to higher altitudes where temperature and pressure drop, water vapour condenses to form low-level clouds and releases its latent heat.
3.
Radiative Cooling: This heat is released at altitude where it can be radiated to space as the air travels back toward the ocean in the upper troposphere, contributing to planetary cooling.

Figure 7. After latent heat is released upon condensation, this energy can be radiated to space while the air travels back to the ocean in the upper troposphere; from Re-appraisal of the global climatic role of natural forests for improved climate projections and policies, Anastassia M. Makarieva [Image-6].

Change of Albedo

Through healthy forests, the biotic pump also increases low-level cloud formation, which enhances Earth’s albedo — the proportion of sunlight reflected back to space. In addition to transpiration, forests also enhance the condensation through cloud-seeding biogenic aerosols, terpenes, released by leaves of trees. Altogether, the biotic pump, according to Makarieva, provides an additional cooling mechanism independent of the more commonly discussed greenhouse gases and may play a significant role in managing atmospheric water vapour distribution and associated climatic effects.

In other publications Makarieva and colleagues argue that a lack of the heat transport mechanism and absence of low-level clouds contribute to global warming, and that this could explain part of the discrepancy between observed warming and warming modelled only by radiative forcing of greenhouse gases such as CO2, methane and most importantly, water vapour.

Ecological Implications

According to the theory, only undisturbed natural forests can maintain the evaporation fluxes necessary to exceed oceanic evaporation and drive the pump mechanism (to win the tug-of-war); the conditions are:

  • Closed Canopy: Continuous tree coverage without gaps with trees that are native to the areas in question (co-evolution),
  • High Leaf Area Index: Dense foliage to maximise evaporative surface, both in deciduous and conifers; agricultural systems with mono-cultures cannot replicate the biotic pump function,
  • Coastal Connectivity: Forest must contiguously extend from ocean or sea coasts inland and exceed critical threshold widths.

Figure 8. Current land occupied by either natural vegetation or pasture and agriculture across the Amazon biome. Cumulative deforestation data are shown up to 2018 [Image-7].

Coastal forests function as ‘moisture gates’. Even if vast interior forests remain intact at the beginning, destroying the coastal forest band severs the connection to oceanic moisture sources, causing the entire system to collapse. For instance, Makarieva and colleagues argue that a deforested band as narrow as 600 km perpendicular to prevailing winds could disable the entire continental pump system. The remaining inland forests would no longer be able to draw moisture from the ocean while losing water through run-off, potentially leading to continent-wide desertification.

Natural forests maintain a counter-intuitive principle: the more moisture evaporated from the land surface, the less moisture is ultimately lost. Conversely, the less moisture is evaporated, the more moisture is lost. By maintaining high evapotranspiration rates, forests ensure continuous moisture influx that exceeds run-off losses. The ground surface of undisturbed closed-canopy forests remains perpetually moist, manifested in their low fire susceptibility and ability to maintain transpiration even during dry periods.

Conclusions

So, where does that leave us, the global, industrialised civilisation? The biotic pump theory reframes forests from passive recipients of climatic conditions to active participants of regional, continental and global water cycles. This active role means that forest conservation is not merely about preserving biodiversity or carbon storage; it is about maintaining the basic hydrological infrastructure that makes terrestrial life possible and our civilisation depends on.

If the biotic pump theory is right, the long-term stability of terrestrial water cycles compatible with human civilisation is unachievable without the recovery and protection of natural, self-sustaining forests on continent-wide scales. The stakes would then extend beyond any single nation or region: the atmospheric moisture pumps of the Amazon, Congo, and Eurasian forests would operate as planetary-scale systems whose failure could have severe consequences for global water availability and climate stability. At this point I would like to mention one pioneering effort by Rob de Laet [7]: Cooling the Climate [8], which aims to protect the Amazon rainforest from its tipping point.
