The Correlation Between Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature

An attempt to demonstrate the cross-correlation between CO2 and GMST in Earth’s history thus far.

A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature and The Rate of Change of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature looked at atmospheric CO2 and global mean surface temperature (GMST), first as values and then as rates of change, across the same four nested windows: 485 million years, 800 thousand years, 12 thousand years, and the years since 1850. Both articles showed that the two variables are accelerating together, but neither said anything about whether one leads the other in time, which is an old and much-debated question. Being a control systems engineer rather than a climate scientist, I wondered whether a signal-processing approach might shed some light on it, taking the same four windows and cross-correlating the two records with each other. To anticipate the result, Figure 1 gives the answer for all four windows at once; I then work through each window in its own section below.

Cross-Correlation Method

The method used to calculate the cross-correlation is the same at every scale; what changes from one window to the next is only how much can be read into the result. How the correlation is formed comes first, then what sets a genuine peak apart from noise, and finally the two conditions a peak must meet before it can be placed away from zero time shift: a sampling period fine enough to resolve the shift, and a record long enough to repeat it several times over.

Forming the Correlation

Both the CO2 and the GMST record are resampled onto a common, evenly spaced timeline: 5 Myr for the 485-million-year window and 100 yr for both the 800,000-year and the 12,000-year windows, i.e. the same sampling periods used by the derivative figures in The Rate of Change of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature; the window since 1850 is resampled monthly. A zero-phase Butterworth high-pass filter is then applied to each series: it removes the slow drift the value figures already show and lets the higher frequencies through unchanged. The cutoff period was chosen, window by window, by sweeping it until the result stopped changing; the only exception is the window since 1850, where it is simply the same 20-year cutoff used as the trend line throughout the industrial-era figures¹. The cross-correlation function R(τ) of the two residuals is then formed over their full overlap, for a time shift τ running to about half the window under investigation in either direction. The convention is simple: a peak at a positive τ means CO2 leads GMST by that amount; a peak at a negative τ means the reverse.

The Noise Floor

Each figure below also plots a noise floor at ±2/, the classical large-sample threshold above which two independent, uncorrelated white-noise sequences of length n would only rarely show a spurious correlation by chance. It is a rough visual guide, not a formal significance test: the residuals here are autocorrelated rather than white, so the true threshold is somewhat higher than this simple one. A value is read against it as a signal-to-noise ratio, SNR = r/f, where f is the floor: well above one can be trusted, close to or below one cannot be told apart from noise.

Resolving a Time Shift

A basic limitation of discrete time-series analysis applies too: a correlation computed at a sampling period Δt cannot resolve a time shift shorter than Δt. Nothing below that should be read as a precise figure, only as a time shift rounded to the nearest sampling period, and least of all when the peak falls on that very first step. A time shift smaller than one sampling period could only be recovered in the time domain by correlating against a known, matching signal shape present in both records, a matched-filter approach that is not available here. This is the first of two conditions a peak must meet before it can be placed away from zero time shift, and it is the one the 485-million-year window fails: its 5-million-year sampling period is coarser than any time shift between CO2 and GMST that the physics would plausibly suggest, so a peak could not show up even if the true time shift were a hundred thousand years.

A Sequence of Repeats

The second condition is length: the record must be long enough, compared with the time shift, to contain several repeats of it. A single transition is a weaker basis than it first looks. With a known input and its measured response, one clean step would be enough: system identification can recover a transfer function, time shift and all, from a single transient, since a step contains all frequencies at once. Here, though, there is no known input to work from, only two measured records and a plain correlation between them; a lone, smooth transition then carries too little bandwidth, and too much noise, to fix a time shift with any confidence. It takes several repeats of the same feature to bring that uncertainty down. This is the condition the window since 1850 fails: its one-month sampling period easily resolves a time shift of a decade or so, of the kind the thermal inertia of the ocean would produce, but the industrial era is one long transition rather than a train of independent repeats of it, so there is nothing to average over (or to integrate coherently to improve the SNR).

Time Domain Analysis

The two middle windows meet both conditions: the roughly 100,000-year glacial cycle repeats eight times across 800,000 years, and the Holocene’s own sub-millennial variations recur often enough across 12,000 years. That is exactly why their results are the two to trust, whichever way the answer turns out. The 485-million-year and post-1850 results, each missing one condition, are better read as consistent with a small or zero time shift than as a finding in their own right.

485 Million Years

Over the Phanerozoic the peak is real but modest (r = 0.40 against a floor of 0.23, SNR ≈ 1.7); this is the coarsest and least reliable of the four sampling periods.

800,000 Years

The ice cores give the strongest peak of the four (r = 0.92, SNR ≈ 40): CO2 and Antarctic temperature are both measured directly from the same medium and vary on the same well-resolved cycle, repeated eight times. At this window’s 100-year sampling period, fine enough for the first time in this set to resolve a time shift at all, the peak is also found away from exactly zero for the first time: τ = +300 years, CO2 leading GMST, a short time shift compared with the 100,000-year cycle it occurs within. 12,000 Years

Over the Holocene, once the trend is removed with a proper high-pass filter, barely a peak survives. The largest correlation found across the ±6,000-year search shown in Figure 4 is r = 0.26 at τ = −4,200 years, an SNR of 1.0 against a floor of 0.26, right on the floor rather than above it. Nor can it be told apart from its neighbours: similar bumps turn up several more times across the plot, among them r ≈ 0.25 at τ = −5,400 years and r ≈ 0.20 at τ = +3,700 years, so there are several peaks of about the same size rather than one clearly larger than the rest. Whatever fast relationship might exist between CO2 and GMST across the Holocene, this window cannot tell it apart from noise.

Since 1850

Two different questions can be put to the same instrumental record since 1850, depending on which part of its spectrum one isolates first.

Interannual Band

Since 1850, once the seasonal cycle is set aside, the largest correlation found across the ±50-year search in Figure 5 is r = 0.28 at a time shift of +46 years, an SNR of 5.4 against a floor of 0.05. It is clearly above its nearest neighbours (r ≈ 0.19 near τ = −1 and τ = −36 years, r ≈ 0.18 near τ = +28 years), rather than lost among near-equals the way the Holocene’s peak is. That is the highest SNR any window in this article shows apart from the ice cores. The same caution applies here as to every edge-adjacent peak in this article: being right at the boundary of the range searched, its exact size and position are less settled than the SNR on its own suggests. What is left of CO2’s year-to-year variation, once the shared industrial-era trend is set aside, is mostly vegetation taking up and releasing carbon dioxide with the seasons rather than a forcing signal; temperature’s own year-to-year variation is dominated by ENSO (the El Niño/La Niña cycle in the tropical Pacific) and by volcanic noise rather than by any response to CO2; and 176 years is too short a record, too close in length to the filter’s own cutoff, to average either kind of noise away.

Figure 5. Cross-correlation of CO2 and GMST since 1850, resampled monthly and band-pass filtered to the interannual band: high-pass above the same 20-year cutoff used throughout the value figure since 1850 in A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature and rate figure in The Rate of Change of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature, then low-pass below 2 years to remove the seasonal cycle, isolated separately in Figure 6 [5][6].

Seasonal Band

The interannual band above deliberately sets aside the Keeling curve’s own seasonal cycle. Isolating that cycle instead, with a high-pass at the same 2-year cutoff used to remove it above, asks a different question altogether: not whether industrial-era CO2 leads industrial-era warming, but whether the CO2 seasonal cycle and the temperature seasonal cycle are in phase.

Figure 6. Cross-correlation of CO2 and GMST since 1850, seasonal band: both records high-pass filtered above a 2-year cutoff, resampled monthly, isolating the Keeling curve’s own annual cycle and its relationship to the annual temperature cycle [5][6]. Drawn over a ±3-year search, appropriate to a seasonal rather than an industrial-era question.

Figure 6 shows a cleanly periodic cross-correlation, repeating every twelve months as a genuine seasonal signal must, with the phase nearest zero at τ ≈−3 months: global temperature leads the seasonal CO2 cycle by about three months, not the other way round. One repeat of that same cycle, a full twelve months later at τ ≈ +9 months, is actually a touch taller in the figure; since the two are the same phase one period apart, that is noise picking between two repeats of one answer, not two different answers, so the repeat nearest zero lag is the one to read. That matches the physical picture, quite apart from anything else in this article: the Northern Hemisphere growing season draws atmospheric CO2 down through the summer and into autumn, a few months after the Northern Hemisphere’s own summer temperature peak that drives it. It is a small result next to the rest of this article (an SNR of only 1.4, and a question about the seasons rather than the industrial era), but a real and physically sensible one, and it needed no data beyond the same monthly sampling period used throughout this window.

Frequency Domain Analysis

The time-domain method above cannot resolve a time shift shorter than its own sampling period without knowing the shape of the signal in advance. A different tool can, in principle: a pure time shift between two signals shows up in their cross-spectrum as a phase that is linear in frequency, and that phase is a continuous quantity. The cross-correlation function for the time-domain method above had already been calculated, so it seemed worth looking at its frequency spectrum too; and the 800,000-year window is the one place in this set with cycles clean enough to try that on CO2 and Antarctic temperature directly.

Both records were resampled onto the same 100-year sampling period as before, and only the mean of each was removed: no trend removal comes before this step, since a filter applied ahead of a spectral estimate risks distorting the very frequencies the estimate is meant to resolve. Their cross-correlation function R(τ) was formed directly over the full overlap, for τ out to about half the 800,000-year record, and Fourier-transformed to give the cross-spectrum, the same quantity the time-domain figure above already plots. Before that transform, R(τ) is passed through a Hann window, as is usual, to soften the distortion that cutting it off abruptly at ±τmax would otherwise cause. Only the lower frequencies, covering the orbital periods with some margin either side, are shown below.

Figure 7. Magnitude (top) and phase, expressed as a CO2-leads-GMST time shift τ in years (bottom), of the cross-spectrum of the same two 800,000-year ice-core records as Figure 3, obtained by Fourier-transforming their cross-correlation function. The heavy curve uses the full record; the two light curves repeat the same calculation on the first and second half of it independently, as a check on how reliable the result is. Red points mark the eccentricity, obliquity and precession periods on the full-record curve.

The magnitude of the cross-spectrum peaks close to three periods: about 100,000, 41,000 and 23,000 years. I did not set out to look for those particular numbers; only on looking them up did I find they are three slow cycles in the Earth’s orbit, known as eccentricity (∼100,000 years), obliquity (∼41,000 years) and precession (∼23,000 years), which gradually change how much sunlight the planet receives. Nothing in the method assumes those frequencies, and noise has no reason to peak at exactly the periods the Earth’s orbit does; a Fourier transform landing on three real, physical cycles, without the method putting them there, is a more convincing check than any statistic worked out after the fact.

The phase at each of those three frequencies corresponds, on the full 800,000-year record, to a time shift of −112, −1,403 and −135 years respectively (temperature ahead of CO2 throughout). Repeating the calculation independently on the first and second half of the record, about 400,000 years each, is the only check on reliability this method has here, and it does not treat the three bands alike. At the obliquity band all three estimates, full record and both halves, cluster tightly: temperature ahead of CO2 by 1,403, 1,694 and 1,494 years. At the precession band all three at least agree on the direction, temperature again ahead, but not on the size: 135, 650 and 281 years. At the eccentricity band even the direction is not consistent between the two halves. That pattern, tightest at obliquity and loosest at eccentricity, is itself the finding: the roughly 1,500-year time shift at the obliquity band is the one part of this analysis with any claim to being real, rather than an artefact of which half of the ice core happens to carry the signal. I have no firm explanation for why it is the obliquity band. The slowness of the eccentricity band, a help for matching cycles over the whole 800,000 years, is a hindrance when it comes to pinning down its phase from only a handful of cycles.

That is a real step past the sampling-period limit the time-domain correlogram ran into head-on, and it should not be made out to be more than it is: two independent halves of one record are a thin basis, thinner than an average over several segments would be.

Conclusion

The 800,000-year window now has two different time-shift estimates for CO2 and Antarctic temperature: this article’s own time-domain correlation (+300 years, CO2 leading) and the frequency-domain phase above (roughly 1,500 years at the obliquity band, temperature leading). That the two disagree, on both size and direction, is less a contradiction than a sign that a single “time shift” is not one well-defined quantity here: a broadband correlation over eight cycles and the phase of one narrow orbital band are two different questions that merely happen to share a unit.

None of this should be read too far, and the oldest warning in statistics applies in full: correlation is not causation, and a cross-correlation, however carefully built, only ever measures the former. A high r at zero time shift is equally consistent with CO2 driving temperature, with temperature driving CO2 through the solubility of the ocean and the respiration of soils, with both being driven by some third process this analysis never looks at (the Earth’s orbit, in the case of the glacial cycles), or with some mixture of all three that changes from one window to the next. Neither method can decide between those. What they do show is that at no scale examined does one variable lead the other by anything either method could resolve with confidence, the tentative 1,500 years at the obliquity band aside. For a coupled system that is the result one would expect, and a useful check that these reconstructions are at least consistent with one another. The case for CO2 as a driver of temperature, rather than merely its companion, rests on the physics of the greenhouse effect set out elsewhere, not on anything a correlation coefficient in this article could establish.

All in all, across the three articles, the point is that despite the large variations of CO2 and GMST over the past millions of years, it is the rate of change that matters. The changes were rather small, and where large, were spread over millennia or millions of years, slow enough for the web of life to keep up with them. Yes, the Earth has seen 3,000 ppm and 35 °C; but it has never, as far as we can resolve, seen them arrive at this speed. Whether one reads that as a system being driven towards a tipping point, in the sense discussed in Shift to Sustainability Could Follow Transitions in Complex Systems, or simply as a forcing applied faster than anything downstream of it can respond, the conclusion is the same: nothing in the record examined here shows change at this pace, and the web of life has never had to adapt this fast before.
