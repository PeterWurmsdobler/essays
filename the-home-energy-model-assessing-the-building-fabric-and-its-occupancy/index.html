The Home Energy Model --- Assessing the Building Fabric and its Occupancy

A brief analysis of the new Home Energy Model, AI-generated documentation and some critique.

Over the past few years I have been working on various projects in and around our home, addressing aspects such as thermal comfort, e.g. by Improving the Thermal Performance of UK 1930s Semi-Detached Houses, or by Literally Rendering a 1930s House Thermally More Efficient. We plan to replace the gas-powered central heating system with a heat pump through the UK government’s Boiler Upgrade Scheme [1]; this requires an Energy Performance Certificate [2] (EPC) obtained through the Standard Assessment Procedure (SAP) [3]. The purpose of this essay is to point out some issues with the SAP and to introduce its successor, the Home Energy Model [4], HEM for short, as a means to produce an EPC.

Before presenting the HEM, I would like to step back and briefly explain the EPC proper and some of its shortcomings.

Energy Performance Certificate

In the UK it is necessary to have an Energy Performance Certificate [2] (EPC) for selling a property, renting out a property or building a new property (and, more recently, to benefit from the Boiler Upgrade Scheme); it is free to check whether a property has a valid EPC [5] on the government website. If no valid EPC is listed for a property, the same website helps in finding companies that can carry out a building survey and produce and register an EPC, which is then valid for 10 years.

Figure 1. Our home’s Energy Performance Certificate [2] (EPC) [Image-1].

At the time of writing (November 2025), the Standard Assessment Procedure (SAP [3]) for the energy rating of dwellings, in its latest version SAP 10.2 [6], is used by surveyors to generate the EPC. Quite often the Reduced Data SAP (RdSAP) [3] is employed as a simpler method for assessing existing dwellings to produce the input data required for the full SAP, given a set of assumptions about the dwelling. The most prominent output of the process is a score on a scale from 1 to 100, split into bands, the energy efficiency rating from A (best) to G (worst), at times called the ‘headline’ metric. Ironically, the score (or band) has no impact on the Boiler Upgrade Scheme; only its existence counts.

Two interesting figures are shown in the EPC: the annual estimated energy needed for the property including heating and hot water (kWh/year, equivalent to an average power consumption as 8,760 kWh/year equates to 1 kW average power) and an estimate for the annual primary energy use as kilowatt-hours per square metre (kWh/(m2 year), equivalent to a floor-area-specific average power consumption). The latter includes energy for lighting, heating and hot water in a property, and takes into account the efficiency of the property’s heating system, of the power station for electricity and of the energy used to produce the fuel and deliver it to the property (how far upstream does one need to go?).

Conflating Issues

My interest in the thermal behaviour of buildings comes mostly from climate change and sustainability, where energy efficiency plays an important role. Energy efficiency relates to the thermal performance of a building and its dwellers as a whole, which is influenced by the following factors:

  • time-invariant building properties: purely intrinsic factors, the building fabric, its thermal losses independent of its use, i.e. the foundation, the walls, the roof, determined by immutable building elements and their materials;
  • installed systems and appliances: these are not part of the building fabric proper and are changed only rarely, even though they can be upgraded, some more easily than others, such as boilers or hobs;
  • time-dependent internal factors: the energy consumption due to habitation, the number of occupiers, their lifestyle and habits;
  • time-dependent external factors: purely extrinsic factors such as weather and climate including insolation;
  • cross-coupling and second-order effects: parameters change due to dynamic factors, e.g. drawn curtains change the heat loss, as do airing habits to control moisture.

The annual estimated energy fuses all of these into a single figure, which certainly gives an indication of the energy consumption of a given home with everything installed at the time of the survey, implicitly assuming a number of occupants and their habits (showers, baths, cooking, thermostat settings, etc.) as well as the location and its climate. The second figure, annual primary energy use, ‘tries’ to be nearly dwelling-agnostic by being specific with respect to the floor area, but fails to do so: first, it makes assumptions about the energy generation (the primary energy mix, which used to be more constant, but with wind & solar is not any more); second, it depends on the lifestyle choices of the dwellers. Both numbers conflate fabric specifics and occupancy dependency as well as weather.

Home Energy Model

Since the current approach (SAP 10.2) to produce the EPC and its figures is neither satisfactory nor appropriate for a changing energy infrastructure, the UK government has launched a consultation to create a new methodology as well as an updated EPC. For instance, the Technical annex for chapter 2: What EPCs measure [7] states that ‘the discussion in this consultation is focused on the prospective role and appropriateness of each proposed metric as a headline metric, as part of a suite of multiple headline metrics. The government’s preferred option for domestic dwellings is to have headline metrics related to fabric performance, heating system, smart readiness and cost.’ So one can hope for an improvement.

Possible Approaches

There could be many ways to obtain a set of metrics, some specific to the fabric, some specific to the heating/cooling system, some assessing the energy efficiency of appliances, and some pertaining to the habits of the occupants. What could be determined from first principles, and what could be measured? For instance, the fabric performance could be condensed into a heat loss figure in W/K for a given property, or even W/(m2 K) as area-specific heat loss; there are ways of obtaining these values from an empirical model, e.g. using time series analysis of inputs (heating power & external temperature) and outputs (internal temperatures), adding persistent excitation, then employing modern techniques such as the Eigensystem Realisation Algorithm (ERA) to determine a state space model of the system and derive a heat loss factor, or many other quantities.

It appears, however, that the government and its advisors went in a different direction for this very complex task. As mentioned on the government website, ‘Following recommendations by the Climate Change Committee (CCC) [8], the government is developing a new methodology to increase its accuracy, robustness, and ensure it is fit for net zero.’ The Home Energy Model (HEM), sometimes referred to as SAP-11, is the next iteration of the means to produce an EPC. Home Energy Model: technical documentation [4] contains a large amount of information on all its components and aspects, e.g. General summary of the Home Energy Model core calculation — A technical explanation of the methodology [9]. In essence, it is a model built from first principles.

AI-Inferred Documentation

There is unfortunately very little documentation on how to construct a HEM for a specific property, not even concept documents or object model diagrams. Fortunately, however, a reference implementation [10] is available under an MIT License [11]. Having elsewhere explored how to create such a model with the help of AI tools, here I would like to use the lessons learned there to present some documentation inferred from the source code implementation. All diagrams below, each together with its caption, were generated by Microsoft Copilot with Claude Sonnet 4, after instructions on how to arrange the boxes to make them more presentable.

Figure 2. Required core components of the HEM schema showing mandatory simulation setup (SimulationTime, ExternalConditions, EnergySupply, Control), building structure (Zone with BuildingElements and ThermalBridging), and water & building services. Objects marked with * contain multiple named instances.

Figure 2 shows the Required core components, including the description of the building structure with its main concept of a Zone. A Zone represents a discrete thermal space with an assumed homogeneous temperature; it is defined by multiple BuildingElement instances that form the Zone’s physical envelope, e.g. walls, windows, floors through which heat can be transferred. In addition, ThermalBridging represents areas where the building envelope has reduced thermal performance due to structural elements or construction details that create preferential heat flow paths, e.g. structural connections and junctions such as floor-wall, wall-roof or wall-window transitions.

Figure 3. Detailed breakdown of BuildingElement types in HEM, showing five main types: opaque elements (walls, roofs), transparent elements (windows, glazed doors), ground elements with variants (slabs, suspended floors, basements), and two adjacent space elements (conditioned and unconditioned). Thermal and geometric properties define element behaviour.

The building fabric of a house can be modelled in various ways, e.g. as a single Zone with all external walls, ground floor and roof defining the envelope, or as several Zone instances, e.g. one zone per storey, or even individual rooms each modelled as a Zone (even though there is an issue with that, as discussed below). A static thermal analysis can be carried out for zones, the results of which are reported in the calculations. To get dynamic aspects, a SpaceHeatSystem and its Control have to be defined per Zone.

Figure 4. SpaceHeatSystem architecture showing heat sources (Boiler, HeatPump, HeatBattery, HIU) and space heating distribution methods (WetDistribution, WarmAir, ElecStorageHeater, InstantElecHeater). Systems integrate with zones, controls, and energy supplies for the heating simulation.

Each SpaceHeatSystem has a few important fields: the Control, the HeatSource, e.g. a HeatSourceWet such as a boiler (powered by an EnergySupply), the Zone it applies to and, most importantly, the emitters, i.e. an abstract name for radiators or other devices that can emit heat (or cold). The Control itself can be a combination of, say, Thermostatic Radiator Valves (TRVs) and some central timer for the boiler. Consequently, a Zone with its building elements defines an envelope and volume which can be heated by a SpaceHeatSystem, which in turn is governed by a Control system; the whole dynamic system can already be simulated without taking into account people, i.e. only a heated building fabric.

Figure 5. Control systems in HEM showing five control types (time-based, set-point, combination, cost-minimising, charge control) and their applications across heating, hot water, cooling, energy, and ventilation systems. Schedule and weather integration enable automated building operation.

One important dynamic aspect of the home brings in occupancy: domestic hot water (DHW). It is specified by the HotWaterSource in conjunction with Events, demand patterns such as showers set out in the HotWaterDemand object; together they feed the number of dwellers and their habits into the simulation.

Figure 6. Hot water systems in HEM, HotWaterSource including CombiBoiler, StorageTank, HeatPump, PointOfUse and demand patterns. Events trigger water heating based on usage schedules (shower, bath) while HotWaterDemand specifies flow rates and temperatures for different use types.

There are a few more inputs needed for a dynamic simulation:

  • ExternalConditions: weather and outdoor environment data. It provides hourly external conditions including air temperatures, solar radiation, wind speeds/directions, and shading objects; it defines the climate the building experiences; some of that can be sourced from a file.
  • InternalGains: heat sources inside the building, i.e. scheduled internal heat generation from occupants, lights, equipment, cooking, etc. It uses time-based schedules with repeat patterns to model when and how much heat is added to each zone (in watts).
  • ApplianceGains: specific appliance heat contributions, i.e. more detailed modelling of individual appliances with usage events and standby power. It tracks both the heat gain and energy consumption of specific appliances like washing machines, TVs, computers, etc.
  • ColdWaterSource: mains water supply temperature data. It provides time-series temperatures of incoming cold water (from the mains supply) that feeds into hot water systems and cold water outlets.
  • InfiltrationVentilation: air leakage and ventilation systems. It models how outdoor air enters the building through both uncontrolled infiltration (air leaks) and controlled mechanical ventilation systems.

With all these components, a full simulation can be carried out for the specified start and end time, given an input for external temperatures and/or wind. There are a few optional components, too.

Figure 7. Optional systems in HEM including heating sources (Boiler, HeatPump, HeatBattery), space heating/cooling systems, renewable generation (PhotovoltaicSystem), and smart appliance controls. These systems extend the core model for building energy simulation.

In summary, the HEM looks promising as a means to produce various performance indicators because it models static aspects of a home separately from dynamic and external ones. It allows one to distinguish between occupant-agnostic metrics intrinsic to the dwelling and lifestyle-related ones.

Snags and Synthesis

As mentioned earlier, a house could be modelled with zones for every room; however, there are a few flaws in the current schema in the definition of adjacent rooms:

  • No adjacent zone reference in BuildingElementAdjacent ConditionedSpace,
  • No way to specify which zone is on the other side,
  • No mechanism to get the temperature of the adjacent zone.

This is a fundamental design limitation of the HEM, because the current BuildingElementAdjacentConditionedSpace effectively creates adiabatic internal walls rather than proper inter-zone heat transfer. This affects the accuracy of multi-zone building thermal modelling and may explain why many example HEM models use single large zones instead of detailed room-by-room models: the inter-zone heat transfer is not properly implemented.

The freedom of being able to model a house in one zone or various zones carries its own risks. Adding complexity should yield higher fidelity; one would hope that the derived performance indicators would converge and become more accurate and precise as more detail is added. But this may not be the case with so many degrees of freedom, which carry the risk of ever-growing model complexity. In addition, since there is no provision for model verification as far as I can tell, a highly detailed model might even be less accurate than a simple one. An unverified precise model is not trustworthy.

Perhaps, then, a hybrid approach to modelling would be more promising: use the simplest model possible, perhaps only one zone, with a limited parameter space, then use measured inputs such as heating power and outdoor temperature, together with measured indoor temperatures, to optimise those parameters. The result is something between a black-box parametric model and a white-box model from first principles (pure HEM); it would be a data-driven model that is implicitly verified.
