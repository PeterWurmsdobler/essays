The Limits of Natural Science

Reality can only be grasped through models; what the models cannot capture leaves a vacuum, and quasi-scientific explanations rush in to fill it.

It is interesting to read the various standpoints on science and esotericism. The fundamental problem seems to me to be the following.

Since the Enlightenment, the paradigm of science has dominated our world view, in particular natural science with its practice, technology (Paul Feyerabend). The essential principles for arriving at knowledge (as distinct from metaphysics) are those of reproducible description and of prediction.

Systems are described with lumped-parameter models. Reality (seen as a naive realist, Karl Popper) can only be grasped through models, be they deterministic or stochastic. A beer glass falling to the floor, for example, can be described quite well with a mass, the acceleration due to gravity and a few other parameters. Stochastic influences can then be integrated with stochastic models. The description of the citric acid cycle in the mitochondria, or the reduction of economic systems to a few numbers such as GDP or the inflation rate, are other examples of thinking in models. Characteristically, these models should be reproducible and, furthermore, are subject to a feedback process: measure with hypotheses, and with these results set up new hypotheses. Emphasising either the one (theories can be derived from observations) or the other (all experience is theory-laden) seems to me to render the process of gaining knowledge only poorly. All scientific disciplines surely agree on what alone is scientific, within their paradigm. It only gets silly when a model is elevated to reality.

What sets natural science apart from the others is its capacity for prediction with deterministic and stochastic models. Our technology is a product of it, since this reductionist thinking has produced machines that are, in turn, easy to handle with this thinking (no na net, as the Viennese say). The consequences are all the worse when this technology is then declared a necessity, the only useful thing.

The crux of the whole matter, however, is this: thinking in models can of course not render by far everything that ‘is the case’ (Wittgenstein), whatever that may be. If the generally accepted method for arriving at knowledge is the scientific one (‘American scientists have found that …’), then an explanatory vacuum remains. Simply saying ‘yes, I just believe it’, or taking a pragmatic approach, is not en vogue.

That is why, here too, people help themselves to quasi-scientific vocabulary: they talk of energy and of harmonic oscillations, and drag in some model or other by the hair. The problem, however, is that the statistical variance of the statements is greater than the variance of the phenomena themselves. Theories of every kind thus find the door wide open. I can link the phenomenon that trees should be felled only at certain times with Kepler’s laws, but also with other, abstruse models. Common to all of them is that the phenomena hide in the variance of the attempts at explanation (statistically speaking).

Be that as it may, money can apparently be made from the fact that the scientific way of thinking seemingly does not suffice to explain the world adequately. It lacks transcendence.
