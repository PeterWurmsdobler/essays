The Rate of Change of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature

An attempt to demonstrate that the recent rates in CO2 and GMST are well above anything the Earth has seen thus far.

A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature plotted atmospheric CO2 and global mean surface temperature (GMST) as values, across four nested windows sixty to six hundred times apart: 485 million years, 800 thousand years, 12 thousand years, and the industrial era since 1850. It also showed, in an overview figure at the start, the rate of change of each variable next to its own value on one shared logarithmic time axis. That overview already made an essential point: the wild-looking ancient excursions happen at a rate indistinguishable from zero, while the calm-looking modern rise produces the sharpest feature either rate panel displays. This article goes through the rates in more detail, window by window. Figure 1 repeats just those two rate panels in a logarithmic time scale, enlarged, before working through each window in turn.

Figure 1. Rates of change of atmospheric CO2 (top) and GMST (bottom) across the full 485-million-year record, on the same logarithmic, reversed time axis. Four concatenated data records were used, handed off at 800 kyr, 12 kyr and in 1850, about 200 yr ago (thin vertical lines): for millions of years the rate is very flat, for hundreds of thousands of years slightly more active, for the Holocene mostly flat again, but the industrial era increasingly spiky, culminating in the sharp modern rise.

Introduction

Generally speaking, an ecosystem is not necessarily affected by an absolute GMST or CO2 level as such: as it can be seen in A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature, during the past 500 million years CO2 has been at 3,000 ppm before and GMST was much higher than today, at 35 °C global mean; life did perfectly well and the Earth got along fine. However, life can be severely affected by a change faster than its species can migrate, adapt or evolve, and the same holds for the ocean’s ability to buffer CO2, for soil carbon, for ice sheets: each of Earth’s sub-systems has a characteristic response time or time constant, and what matters is whether the exogenous forcing is slower or faster than that intrinsic time constant.

Therefore, in order to assess the evolution of the Earth’s climate and carbon system, one must look at the rate of change rather than the absolute value, i.e. the first derivative of atmospheric CO2 and GMST with respect to time. The rate panels of the overview figure already showed that speed across the whole record on one shared axis: indistinguishable from the beginning of the records until the last centuries, then the sharpest feature either panel contains. The four figures below plot that same signed rate of change for each window, in units per century throughout so they can be read against one another; the data sources are listed in the table in A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature. The axes are linear, so the shorter windows are dominated by the present-day rate; that is not a defect of the figures but their subject.

Methodology

Two methodological choices apply to every window below, set out here once rather than repeated four times.

Low-Pass Filter and Differentiation

Wherever a low-pass filter is needed before differentiating a noisy record, it is a zero-phase Butterworth filter, and its cutoff is quoted throughout as a period rather than a frequency, consistent with the periods already used for each record’s own sampling. The cutoff period is chosen per series from how much of that series’ own rate-of-change variance survives it: the shortest cutoff period that keeps at least 80 % of that variance, not the value’s variance, which is dominated by slow excursions regardless of cutoff period (differentiation multiplies the power spectrum by (2πf)2, so the rate series carries real energy at short periods the value series does not). Filtering less than this reports a single noisy sample pair as the record’s fastest rate; filtering more discards genuine variability these archives are documented to preserve.

The 485-million-year window needs no such filter: PhanDA’s own five-million-year sampling period is already coarser than any filter cutoff period would be. For CO2 and GMST at 800,000 years, 700-year and 250-year cutoff periods were used respectively. For the 12,000-year window, a 220-year cutoff period was used for both CO2 and GMST. These cutoff periods lie within independently published physical limits for how finely these archives can resolve a signal at all¹. Every such filter is zero-phase, run both forward and backward through the data so it introduces no delay; the price is that it has to assume something about the data just beyond the edge of the record, which biases the filtered curve there only slightly, as the individual sections below note where it matters.

Representative Contemporary Rate

The terms “today’s rate” or “the present-day rate” are used throughout as the point of comparison: it is always a trailing least-squares linear trend over the most recent twenty years, read at the end of each record. Twenty years is short enough to track the current trend rather than one from decades ago, and long enough that a single noisy year cannot move the estimate much; it is also the same cutoff period used for the heaviest curve in A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature’s value figure since 1850, so the two can be checked against each other.

Table 1 shows why twenty years rather than something shorter or longer: CO2’s trailing rate climbs steadily as the window shortens, from 188.7 ppm per century at fifty years to 247.2 ppm per century at ten; temperature’s climbs too, down to twenty years, but then dips slightly at ten, a sign that the window has become short enough for a single recent year’s noise, an El Niño or La Niña year or a cluster of anomalous months, to move the estimate as much as the underlying trend does. The table’s own twenty-year row for CO2, 237.9 ppm per century, matches the twenty-year Butterworth-filtered curve of Figure 5 below, read at the point nearest the present its padding allows, to within a few per cent: two different methods agreeing, reassurance that neither is an artefact of how it was computed.

All four rows are the same quantity, the rate now, differing only in how far back the trend is taken; the chosen twenty-year window is in bold.

485 Million Years

Over the Phanerozoic, at the five-million-year sampling period the record permits, the fastest sustained change in CO2 is about 0.02 ppm per century and the fastest in temperature about 1 × 10−4 °C per century. The present-day rates stand respectively roughly eleven thousand and twenty-five thousand times above those, or some four orders of magnitude higher.

Figure 2. Rates of change of atmospheric CO2 and GMST over the past 485 million years, at a 5-million-year sampling period to match the PhanDA reconstruction’s stage resolution [3]. Positive numbers mean warming or a CO2 rise, negative ones cooling or a drawdown. Dashed lines mark the five mass extinctions listed in A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature; several of the largest excursions fall at or close to them. The present-day instrumental rate is quoted in the legend rather than drawn as it would be vastly off scale.

The vertical lines marking the five mass extinctions listed in A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature are significant. The largest CO2 drawdown in the whole record occurs in the stages running into the Ordovician-Silurian event, which is what one would expect of an extinction attributed to glaciation; the sharpest cooling of the entire 485 million years happens immediately after the Permian-Triassic. The association is suggestive rather than causal, and at this resolution it can be no more than suggestive (an average over a five-million-year sampling period does not carry any information about what happened inside that period, Nyquist theorem), but it is there, and it is consistent with the general observation that it is the transitions, not the levels, that coincide with the extinctions.

800,000 Years

The ice cores support a far smaller sampling period than the value figures needed, 100 years, which does carry some noise, so a zero-phase Butterworth low-pass is applied before numerical differentiation (see Methodology above): 700 years for CO2, which keeps 90 % of its own rate-of-change variance, and 250 years for temperature, which keeps 84 %.

Figure 3. Rates of change over the past 800,000 years, resampled at a 100-year sampling period and low-pass filtered before numerical differentiation: 700 years for CO2, 250 years for temperature, each chosen to keep the large majority of that series’ own rate-of-change variance [1][2]. The present-day instrumental rate, about fifty-one and three times the fastest natural rate here for CO2 and temperature respectively, is quoted in the legend rather than drawn, so the eight glacial-interglacial cycles remain visible at their own scale.

At those cutoff periods, the peaks correspond to identifiable events: the eight glacial terminations, where the system flipped out of a glacial state comparatively quickly. CO2’s fastest natural rate, about 4.6 ppm per century, falls during one of them. Temperature’s fastest, about 0.95 °C per century, does not: it is a single, roughly century-long departure some 95,000 years ago, rather than a value repeated across all eight cycles. The present-day rate exceeds the two by factors of about fifty-one and three respectively. Both curves are bounded by their own filter’s settling requirement rather than by the 100-year industrial threshold: CO2’s 700-year cutoff period needs enough margin that both the curve and the fastest-rate search behind it stop at 349 years before present (1601); temperature’s 250-year cutoff period settles less severely but still stops short of the threshold, at 238 years before present (1712). Three is a modest ratio next to the thousands in the Phanerozoic period. This means that something in this ice core’s past came within a factor of three of today’s warming rate, once for perhaps a century or two, in the single most turbulent stretch of glacial ice the record has. Today’s rate has held for over fifty consecutive years and is still rising; a different situation altogether.

The eight terminations do not look equally abrupt in the figure, and the reason is the archive, not the filter: EPICA Dome C’s own native sampling is far denser near the present than deep in the past, a median 47 years between measurements in the most recent 100,000 years against 621 years in the oldest 100,000 the window reaches (the CO2 composite the same, 44 against 532 years). A 250-year cutoff period resolves genuine variability at that timescale where the ice is measured closely enough to support it, and can only interpolate smoothly across it where it is not, so the most recent of the eight deglaciations, the best resolved, shows a warming of 0.64 °C per century, while the oldest, some 720,000 years back, shows only 0.08 °C per century. Nothing here says the earlier deglaciations warmed more gently; the ice that would show otherwise was never measured finely enough to say so. The same effect explains why the curve’s own oscillations grow towards the present: that part of the record is not more variable per-se, only better resolved: a shorter native sampling period there means faster oscillations can be resolved at all.

12,000 Years

Within the Holocene itself, the same reasoning (see Methodology above) gives one shared cutoff period for both series rather than two: 220 years, which keeps 82 % of CO2’s own rate-of-change variance and 87 % of Temp12k’s, close enough between the two that splitting them, as the 800,000-year window does, would not change much. Atmospheric CO2’s fastest natural rate is about 7.7 ppm per century, reached somewhere in the last thousand years of the pre-industrial Holocene rather than at any one well-known event; GMST’s fastest, about 0.10 °C per century, occurs at the very start of the window, where the last deglaciation is still finishing. For the seven thousand years in the middle both rates run well below even that. The present-day rates stand some thirty-one times higher, for both CO2 and GMST, again too far off to share the linear axis below without flattening the Holocene’s own variability, so once more quoted rather than plotted.

Since 1850

The fourth window, Figure 5, is simpler to generate: only the instrumental records. It mirrors that value figure since 1850 directly rather than picking one curve out of it: each record is resampled onto a common, uniformly discretised timeline with a one-month sampling period, passed through the same three zero-phase Butterworth low-pass filters (five-, ten- and twenty-year cutoff periods), followed by numerical differentiation, in the same colours and the same progression from thin to thick as in A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature.

Figure 5. Rates of change since 1850, from the instrumental records alone [1][5][6]. The three darker curves are the derivatives of the three low-pass filtered data in the value figure since 1850 of A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature, same colours and weights, each drawn only as far as the filter is supported by data; the palest, thinnest curve is the year-on-year change from an annually resampled record, with no Butterworth filter of its own.

A fourth, thinnest curve of all is the year-on-year change: no Butterworth filter behind it, only a resample onto a uniformly discretised timeline with a strictly annual sampling period, one sample at the same point in the calendar year every year, ahead of the numerical differentiation². Sampling always at the same point in the seasonal cycle removes it just as effectively, if less elegantly: much as a notch filter tuned to that exact frequency and phase would, without being designed as one. Numerical differentiation then removes the slow secular trend the same way it does for every curve in this figure; together, the two act like a band-pass, and what survives in between is the band this curve intends to show: real year-to-year variability, ENSO (the El Niño/La Niña cycle in the tropical Pacific) and volcanic eruptions, not an artefact of the sampling period.

Nineteenth-Century Noise

One more feature of this figure is worth articulating: the twenty-year filtered temperature curve varies by several degrees per century in the record’s first decades, comparable to or larger than most of what follows for the rest of the nineteenth century. That is not the filter failing: it is HadCRUT5’s own sparse nineteenth-century observing network, far thinner and far more uncertain than the coverage the twentieth century provides, showing up exactly where numerical differentiation is expected to amplify it. The same variability, at a scale that makes it look unremarkable rather than alarming, is what A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature’s overview figure compresses onto its own shared axis alongside the modern rise.

The Twenty-Year Trend

The level of a curve represents the rate of change; its upward movement is its acceleration (the rate of the rate). The heaviest, smoothest curve, the result of the twenty-year filter, is the one to read first: modest or uneven through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, then climbing sharply from around 1950, to roughly 245 ppm per century for CO2 and roughly 2.9 °C per century for temperature by the point where the filter still has data on both sides of it.

Shorter, Noisier Curves

The other three curves answer a different question: what is the rate doing more recently than the twenty-year filter can say? A shorter cutoff period needs less padding at the ends, so the ten-year curve is settled to about 2020–2022 and the five-year one to about 2022–2024, each several years closer to the present than the twenty-year curve’s 2015–2017³. The effect is visible at a glance: the shorter the cutoff period, the more the curve keeps the interannual variability the twenty-year filter was designed to remove. El Niño and La Niña years and volcanic eruptions push the five-year curve up and down by roughly 80 ppm per century and 5 °C per century around whatever the underlying trend is doing, which is real variability, not filter error. The palest, thinnest curve, the year-on-year change, needs no Butterworth filter and none of its padding, so it runs right up to the last measurement, but scatters by roughly 50 ppm per century and 7 °C per century from one year to the next; it is drawn thin so that scatter does not distract from the figure’s main point.

A Lag Between the Two Panels

One asymmetry between the two panels is worth naming: the acceleration is unmistakable in the CO2 panel and considerably harder to read in the temperature one, even though both are there. Two factors compound to produce that. First, the present-day trend is large compared to its own noise for CO2 and is not for temperature: the present-day CO2 trend, around 245 ppm per century, is three to five times the 50–80 ppm per century of interannual variability added to it, so it stands out clearly even in the noisier five- and ten-year curves; the present-day temperature trend, around 2.9 °C per century, is comparable to, or smaller than, the 5–7 °C per century of variability ENSO and volcanic eruptions add to it, so the same acceleration is present but lies underneath noise as large as the signal itself. Second, there is a physical explanation: the ocean’s heat capacity means global mean temperature responds to a given radiative forcing over years to decades, not immediately, a first-order lag element at least. A lag between CO2 accelerating and temperature following is therefore expected by the nature of things, not a shortcoming of the estimator: the temperature panel is not showing a weaker phenomenon, only a noisier and more delayed window onto the same one.

Caveats

Three caveats apply to the numbers above, across every window rather than to any one of them; they are gathered here rather than left scattered through the text.

A Single Sensor, Scaled by a Constant

The 800,000-year temperature reading rests on EDC, one sensor at one point in Antarctica, already flagged in A Brief History of Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature’s 800,000-year section: its local signal is turned into a global-mean equivalent by dividing by a polar-amplification constant well supported for the slow, orbitally-paced glacial cycle it was derived for. Whether that same constant also holds for the much shorter, centennial-scale feature behind the factor-of-three ratio above is a separate question this analysis cannot check: a single sensor has no second, independent point at that timescale to compare itself against, so there is no way from this data alone to tell a genuine global signal apart from noise local to that one site. The safer reading is therefore to treat that one ratio as a lower bound, not a precise figure: today’s rate is at least three times the fastest the ice core shows, and quite possibly more.

One Noisy Peak Is a Poor Basis for a Ratio

A single noisy or locally-sourced point can dominate a ratio, and the 0.95 °C per century figure above is exactly that, a single event rather than a repeated one. The more robust check is the one worth running on any noisy channel: take a mean and standard deviation over a few hundred to a thousand samples instead of trusting the single largest one, and ask how far outside that spread today’s rate lies, rather than what the record’s single worst excursion was. Restricted to the 800,000-year window’s most recent, best-resolved 100,000 years, so that genuinely well-resolved variability is not mistaken for a wide natural spread, the filtered temperature rate has an effectively zero mean and a standard deviation of 0.22 °C per century; today’s 3.0 °C per century lies about thirteen standard deviations out. That is not a formal significance test: the rate series is autocorrelated rather than independent samples, so the usual Gaussian-tail reading of “thirteen sigma” does not apply here, and the number should be taken as a scale, not a probability. Even allowing for that, the departure is too large to rest on trusting any one noisy peak. The same caution applies wherever else this article reports a single peak; it is only demonstrated here, on the 800,000-year window, where the density of samples makes the comparison possible.

Resolution Limits What Can Be Compared

A finite difference cannot resolve anything faster than the record it is taken from. That is why the ratios in Table 2 shrink sharply once the record becomes finely sampled, from twenty-five thousand at 485 million years down to single figures at 800,000 years for temperature: a genuine effect of resolution, in the sense that a coarser record simply cannot see fast events that did happen, but not in the sense that the surviving numbers are themselves artefacts, since the 800,000-year and 12,000-year rates are filtered exactly as far as each archive’s own documented resolution supports, no further. A 150-year event 400 million years ago would be invisible in a record sampled every five million years; the Phanerozoic panel cannot rule it out, only say that nothing like it survives in what was preserved. The ice cores share a milder version of the same limit, since air takes decades to centuries to seal into bubbles. The fairest comparisons are therefore the 800,000-year and 12,000-year windows: their 100-year sampling periods are far closer to the present-day rate’s own 20-year window than the Phanerozoic figure’s 5 million years, so the two rates being compared are measured on much more similar terms. Even there, today’s CO2 rate is comfortably an order of magnitude above anything the ice cores have recorded; the rate of warming is a more mixed picture, an order of magnitude above the Holocene’s own fastest natural rate but only a factor of three above the single fastest one the 800,000-year ice core preserves. That factor of three was reached once, briefly; today’s instrumental rate has held for over fifty years and is still rising.

Conclusion

The window since 1850 is left out of the table: it is entirely industrial, so has no natural rate to compare against.

The derivatives establish that both variables are accelerating; they say nothing about whether one leads the other in time. The Correlation Between Two Earth Variables: Carbon-dioxide and Global Mean Surface Temperature takes up that question next.
