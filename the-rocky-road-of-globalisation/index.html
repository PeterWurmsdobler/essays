The Rocky Road of Globalisation

From the technocrat to the economocrat: economic efficiency without stated boundary conditions, positive feedback and a narrow horizon.

The word globalisation is on everybody’s lips and is perceived in very different ways. On the one hand it is praised as the great blessing for humanity, on the pragmatic but foolish premise that there is no alternative anyway. On the other hand, many Cassandra cries can already be heard, and they are entirely justified. Without any claim to completeness, this essay adds one more by postulating the path from technocrat to economocrat as the determining factor.

Building on the great achievements of the natural sciences in the nineteenth century, their executive organ, technology, first flourished with industrialisation at the end of that century. Later, until the second half of the twentieth century, this growth continued, driven alternately by the armaments machine and by the necessary reconstruction after the world wars. The necessity of material growth, however, was never questioned. Thus a technical and scientific paradigm took shape and lodged itself in people’s minds.

This technocratic world view is defined by a strong belief in technical feasibility and by the feeling that the Earth can be mastered with the methods of natural science. Any negative effects of a still immature technology can be compensated for, or so the technocrats believe, by improvements and more technical innovation.

The positive effects for the Western world are obvious: prosperity was achieved for very many people, a prosperity previously reserved for a few, namely members of the Church, the nobility and industry. Back then, the human resources of a large part of the population were used up for a smaller part of society; now it is natural resources, made accessible by the methods of technology. Furthermore, a great deal of energy and mechanisation has genuinely and substantially raised the quality of life of part of the world’s population, e.g. when one thinks of the development of working hours or of medical care. The problem has thus shifted somewhat, in that the circle of those favoured by the consumer society has been widened.

Nevertheless, a way of thinking oriented towards material, and therefore quantitative, progress forms the basis of the prosperity attained in a dominant part of the world, and it is stubbornly clung to. But continuous development, i.e. a process of information, requires a great deal of resources for purely energetic reasons. That this comes at the expense of some people, or of the whole Earth, created a corresponding awareness only with a certain delay. Somewhat later, therefore, a counter-movement broke away, the ecological wave. Even the most hard-boiled technocrats have realised that the squandering of natural resources must come to an end. Later still, the ecological counter-movement was corrupted by being fitted back into the same way of thinking. The result is growth thinking with a green tint.

As successful as the technocrats’ concept has been so far, such a development cannot be continued. The economy takes various paths, but none leads to success. Finally, the market economy, or at least a reductionist variety of it, feels legitimised by the collapse of the Eastern Bloc to make a universal claim, so far with success.

In recent years it has become apparent that economic growth has not risen to the extent necessary for a quantitatively growing system. Analysis shows quite clearly that new technical means such as automation and electronic data processing have increased an economy’s capacity to produce so much that, on the one hand, the capacity to consume has already run into saturation and, on the other, closely coupled with it, the available workforce is no longer needed, above all when work can be moved to low-wage countries. The relocation of jobs to developing countries happens, or so it is claimed, in most cases purely out of social commitment, since the population in those countries can thereby earn foreign currency in order then to acquire the coveted consumer goods or armaments, or simply to pay back the national debt.

This means that economic growth is surely limited not only by natural resources or by nature’s capacity to absorb, but also by the over-saturation of Western society. In short, the economy stagnates, the most dreadful phenomenon in a world fixated on growth. The question immediately arises, of course, how stagnation is to be countered. There are basically two approaches which, treating the symptoms, suggest growth. First, society’s capacity to consume must be increased, and second, its capacity to produce can be throttled.

The first step is easy to take. Any company can, by raising its advertising budget and moving capacity into marketing, stir up enough consumer spirit to keep the economy going. This already happens, in that through targeted indoctrination a large proportion of graduates of business schools strive for careers in ‘marketing’ or ‘management consultancy’. These, then, are the cheerleaders of the consumption–production machine.

To throttle an economy’s capacity to produce with a fixed number of employees, mechanisms must be installed that reduce productivity through internal friction. The first that come to mind are quality assurance according to ISO 9000, constant reorganisation, or the renaming of the same structure without content. Internal communication then ties up many workers without any increase in productivity as seen from outside. One phenomenon of this activity is that an employee’s small importance is compensated for by a great deal of outward show, such as fine English job titles. Thus an ordinary pharmaceutical sales representative may well advance to ‘Manager Medical Services’.

The procedures just mentioned, however, cannot have a lasting effect on a growth-oriented economy. They are therefore merely attempts to increase growth. A way out of the strained economic situation was and is only possible through a change of consciousness, i.e. a paradigm shift. The seemingly successful change is already apparent, away from the purely scientific thinking of the technocrats towards an approach from economics, the economocratic paradigm, which manifests itself in a profound statement: ‘Everything must become more economical’ (whatever that means). The success of this solution, so it is argued, can already be seen in the economic upswing of the USA, and will, as unfortunately always, take hold in Europe somewhat later.

So where does the secret of success lie? For one thing, the new thinking of the economocrats is not reductionist like that of the old generation fixated on technology, or so at least it is claimed. To think economically means to think universally, globally, i.e. not only of money but also of other forms of investment such as shares, warrants and investment funds. Of human dimensions, however, there is unfortunately never any talk in a hypertrophic financial market in which everything is judged only by its so-called economic efficiency. But what is economical?

Like the definition of optimality, economic efficiency depends on an objective function within a certain time horizon and a certain radius, i.e. on a criterion that is optimal in space and time. The term ‘economical’ can only mean that, under certain boundary conditions of space (not only in the geographical sense) and time, the efficiency of an economic process is optimised, i.e. maximised. Without stating the boundary conditions under which it was established, the notion of economic efficiency therefore makes no sense. Merely to speak of ‘economical’ is entirely legitimate, but unfortunately meaningless.

What is currently called economical is a crude reduction of reality, since the radius of the objective function is kept very small, above all as far as the social dimension is concerned. The reduced economic efficiency concerns not only the narrow horizons in space and time, but also a human dimension. Just as Galileo’s principle of measurability found its way into natural science, the principle of monetary valuation now reigns: put a price on what can be priced, and make priceable what cannot yet be. But why can this reduced picture of economic efficiency be sold so well? And why does the indoctrination with an unshakeable faith in economic efficiency work so well across national borders, without any thought for the social and ecological implications?

Closely linked with the notion of economic efficiency is growth. In itself, growth is nothing bad, only at present growth is reduced to what can be valued in material or monetary terms, in that an increase in consumption and production furthers growth. Moving activities into the social or artistic and musical domain does not serve growth by today’s economic valuation, whereas a car accident does. Nor is family work or bringing up children seen as work that furthers growth. Economical is what furthers growth in this reduced sense, and vice versa. That is the determinant of the thinking behind the economisation of the world.

Probably the most important secret of the success of global ‘economisation’ is the principle of positive feedback of subjective pressure through opaque international economic entanglement. An example: a worker or employee is employed by a multinational corporation in which he himself holds shares through investment funds. As a shareholder, he exerts pressure on the management through the investor on the one hand, and as a consumer on the other, in that he always wants to buy the cheapest, thus spurring the company to become ‘more economical’. The pressure is thus led round in a circle and raises the demands on all involved. The consumer, the investor and the company each operate according to their individual optimisation with a small horizon, since each wants to be ‘economical’ for himself.

Through coupling and entanglement, as just indicated, an extremely complex economic structure is created, with the behaviour of a spiral, or rather of a vicious circle. For economic pressure creates counter-pressure, or propagates while narrowing the frame for valuing economic efficiency. But what drives this circulus vitiosus, and why is there no regulating mechanism? The mainspring of the process is surely people’s insatiability and lack of contentment. Nor are the limits of their actions made plain to them, on the one hand regarding resources, on the other regarding social consequences. In a small, comprehensible economic unit it would be hard to put pressure on one’s neighbour, on whom one knows one depends. If society is anonymous, however, there is no regulator. This anonymous economic pressure produces a dynamic process of globalisation, driven by egocentric optimisation with narrow horizons and promoted by the catalyst of anonymity. That companies then merge into ever larger, anonymous units is the consequence. The result is transnational corporations that undermine democratic structures by, in turn, dictating what is to be understood by economic efficiency. Quod erat demonstrandum.

The turn towards a higher degree of economic efficiency, however, also has disadvantages. Not everybody can take part in a flourishing economy. Unemployment benefits and a functioning state with a strong executive, law and order, can however hold off social destabilisation. An even distribution of work and of the wealth generated is therefore not necessary, since such a procedure would be neither economical nor efficient under the terms set by those who profit from the system. And what is economical is likewise set by those who hold the levers, and those are large economic units; and so another circle closes.

If in the past the economy was managed primarily by regulatory and directive measures, then of course there is an immediate cry for deregulation, sold as liberalisation. ‘Liberal’ is then taken to mean that large transnational companies can act freely on the international financial market by making their own laws. In a democratic setting, liberal ought rather to mean that suitable measures preserve the freedom of the small units by reining in dominant forces.

But when even social democratic politicians demand that politics should serve the economy (and they surely mean only its exponents), then democracy is already lost: the fox has been put in charge of the henhouse. Once again a headless flight forward is taken, this time by the economists, who seek to make the economy more dynamic through the reductionist notion of ‘economical’. Currency policy rather than sustainable social and ecological policy is then demanded as the short-term maxim.

If the time dimension is included in the calculation of economic efficiency, some things may suddenly turn out to be uneconomical, or vice versa. The social dimension, too, can reverse many a calculation. To assume, however, that the small frame of an individual economic agent suffices to achieve optimality on the large scale seems very naive. Nor can a system ever set itself the boundary conditions under which it optimises.

The principle of individual optimisation, i.e. a market economy, is not to be overthrown; only the objective function must not be formulated by those who benefit from it, otherwise economic efficiency is merely distorted. The boundary conditions, these economic objective functions, should always correspond to society’s state of knowledge and understanding, and must be developed democratically together with specialists from the social sciences and ecology. Ecology is not an absolute quantity, since ecological action could, in the light of the future, turn out to be reductionist again. What matters is always the intention, namely not knowingly to neglect external costs. And in case of doubt one should act on the principle in dubio pro natura. Ecology can thus be understood as long-term economy, as economy with wide horizons in space and time, as sustainable economic activity.

The notion of sustainability is beginning to take root in people’s minds, but few are willing to bear the personal consequences. What would they look like? Live consciously! That does not mean, of course, that everybody must reflect on the entire world economic situation when buying a bread roll. But one can at least ask that consumer behaviour be subjected a little to thinking beyond one’s own self, for instance: what do I bring about when I buy a roll from the baker, or from a large corporation? Who will probably pay my unemployment benefit: the one who keeps a family business going here, or a company that shirks its responsibility when the ground gets hot and moves abroad, even though it first blackmailed the state into subsidies in exchange for jobs?

A way out of this seemingly overwhelming situation is surely conceivable, on the one hand, through militant action at the top of the economy. But demonising the economy is misplaced, for we are all the economy, consumers as well as producers. And the board member of a transnational corporation is also only an executor who let himself be instrumentalised by a system for his own advantage. Therefore a counter-spiral must begin at the grass roots, through the thinking of all involved and through courageous, sustainable action, and not through resignation, talking oneself into one’s own powerlessness. In the democratic sense one vote can achieve nothing, but in sum a great deal. There is also a life beyond consumption.
