Useful Energy and Mining Limits on the Sower's Way

A published model updated for useful energy and limited by mining: even starting now, mines can hardly grow fast enough for the CO₂ budget.

Building a renewable energy system requires energy itself; Tom Murphy called this the Energy Trap [1], and Sgouridis, Csala and Bardi formalised it as The Sower’s Way [2]: during the energy transition, part of every year’s energy production has to be invested in the renewable energy capacity of the future, like a fraction of this year’s harvest that has to be put aside as seed for future crop production. According to The Sower’s Way, renewable installations have to accelerate from 0.12 TW of nameplate capacity per annum in 2013 to a peak of 7.3–11.6 TW per annum in the late 2030s, for an early or a late phase-out of fossil fuels. Their model answers a pragmatic question: if fossil fuels are to be phased out within a target carbon budget, what do we have to build, and what are the consequences of delaying the build-out?

There are two interesting aspects that haven’t been addressed in The Sower’s Way. First, their model looks at the required capacity of renewable energy in isolation; it does not ask whether mines can supply the metals needed for such a build-out, nor how fast mining can grow to reach that supply capacity. Second, it assumes that the energy provided as primary energy has to be replaced by renewable energy of the same magnitude; in reality, the useful¹ energy needed is much less, about 40% of current primary energy [3] and [4]; therefore, after the electrification of the economy only a fraction of the primary energy needs to be supplied by renewables. Nevertheless, the nameplate capacity of renewable installations has to be much higher than the mean power they deliver, due to their small capacity factors. Since the Sower’s model is public [5], I was able to rebuild it from its equations and tables, confirm its results, and then update its energy model for useful energy and add constraints for mining limits.

The Sower’s Model

The model balances energy year by year, with every flow of energy expressed as a mean power in TW, i.e. TW-years of energy per year: demand is set by the population and the power each person uses; fossil fuels supply what a carbon budget allows following a phase-out profile; renewables fill the gap, and the energy to build them is taken from all sources. Phasing Out Fossil Fuels

Without a phase-out, fossil emissions would follow a path called by the authors without intervention, given as a table and shown in Figure 2: the emissions peak at about 40 Gt per annum in the late 2030s as fossil fuels deplete. The phase-out is chosen by two numbers: the year t0 in which it starts, and a carbon budget, i.e. the CO2 that may be emitted from 2013 onwards. The authors take the budget from the IPCC’s budgets that may keep warming below 2 °C: 510, 990 or 1,505 Gt of CO2, depending on the climate model; here 990 Gt is being used. Nothing enforces such a budget, of course; it is at best an aspiration. From t0 onwards, the emission rate of each fossil fuel is modelled to follow a bell-shaped curve:

Here r0 is the fuel’s emission rate at time t0, and R its share of the budget that is left at t0; the budget is shared between oil, gas and coal in proportion to their emission rates at time t0. The curve starts at r0, so it joins the path without intervention without a jump, and its integral from t0 onwards is exactly R, so the emissions use up the budget. Since the bell peaks at t0, the phase-out uses only its falling half: from t0 on, the emissions never rise again. How fast they fall follows from R and r0: at a constant rate r0, the budget left would last R/r0 years and then stop at once; along the curve, the emissions instead halve after about 0.9 times as many years, by when 71% of R is used, and the rest trails off. The less budget is left when the phase-out starts, the steeper it has to be. The curve is in fact the derivative of a logistic curve, i.e. the cumulative emissions grow along a logistic curve and level off at the budget.

Population, Affluence and Demand

The population is given as a table, i.e. it does not respond to the energy system, as shown in Figure 2. The population grows from 7.2 billion in 2013 to 8.2 billion today, 9.6 billion in 2050 and 10.9 billion in 2100, and is still growing then. In comparison, the UN now expects it to peak at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s and to ease to 10.2 billion by 2100 [6].

The power per person w(t), i.e. the net primary energy each person has available per year, as a mean power, can be taken as a proxy for affluence. Without intervention, it is given as a table, too, see Figure 2: it would grow from 2.4 kW in 2013 to 2.9 kW in 2100. The model bends it from 2015 onwards towards a chosen value by 2100, here 2,000 W, which the authors cite as a lower limit for a high-income society. The demand is the population P times the power per person, i.e. E(t) = P(t) ⋅ w(t). With 2,000 W per person in 2100, the demand peaks at 24 TW around 2050 and falls to 22 TW by 2100, against 31 TW without intervention, see Figure 2.

Energy to Build Renewables

Fossil fuels supply primary energy to the extent their emissions within the budget allow, given the emission intensity of each fuel. Renewables fill the gap between demand and fossil supply, shared between wind, solar photovoltaics, solar thermal and geothermal power in proportions given as tables. Building renewables takes energy, which follows from their net output N, i.e. the power they deliver on average, their lifetime of L years and their ERoEI (energy returned on energy invested): a plant that delivers a power of N on average returns N ⋅ L TW-years of energy over its lifetime, so it takes N ⋅ L/ERoEI TW-years to build. Every year, plants with an output of ΔN have to be added, and the part N/L that reaches the end of its life replaced; building both takes the energy, in TW-years per year, i.e. as a mean power,

The two parts differ. Replacing the plants that retire takes N/ERoEI, i.e. 5% of the output for an ERoEI of 20, as if each plant paid back its construction over its life. Growth, in contrast, takes L/ERoEI TW-years per TW added, i.e. 1.25 for a lifetime of 25 years, since a new plant has to be built in full before it delivers anything.

The ERoEI counts the energy a plant actually delivers over its life, so it already includes the capacity factor, i.e. the ratio of what a plant delivers on average to its nameplate capacity, the power it delivers at full output. The energy invested therefore follows from the output, not from the nameplate capacity; the latter follows from the output and the capacity factors, again given as tables, and only matters for the metals.

The model steps through time one year at a time: each year, N follows from the gap between demand and fossil supply, ΔN is the addition from the year before, and the energy invested is taken from the net energy of all sources, fossil and renewable alike. The fossil part of the energy invested emits CO2 too, which the phase-out has not allowed for; the model therefore runs a few times, each time lowering the budget left for the phase-out by these emissions, until all emissions together stay within the budget.

The ERoEI is chosen, here 20 for wind and fixed fractions of that for the others, and it rises with learning as more is built. For photovoltaics, which supply about half of the renewable energy by 2100, the model file sets a quarter of wind’s value, but the authors’ published runs start at half, i.e. at 10; with learning, its ERoEI is about 22 today and 25 by 2050. A review of the published estimates, harmonised to the same sunshine and lifetime, found 9–34 [7], so the model sits in the middle of that range.

Rebuilding the Model

As a whole, in The Sower’s Way, population, affluence without intervention, emission intensities, the shares and capacity factors of the renewables are given; the budget, the start of the phase-out, the power per person in 2100 and the ERoEI are chosen; the fossil phase-out, the build-out of renewables and the energy invested follow. The model itself is written in AnyLogic, a commercial modelling software platform which uses an open, text-based XML format to represent its models. Since I do not have an AnyLogic licence I chose to rebuild a version in Python from the equations and data tables in the authors’ model file, then checked it against the output of their runs, which they published with the code. Figure 3 shows a run from my rebuild which should be equivalent to Figure 1, with the same settings and drawn in the same way.

Figure 3. Primary energy by source for a phase-out from 2020, an ERoEI of 20 and 2,000 W per person in 2100, from my Python rebuild with a budget of 990 Gt, in the colours and order of the original.

My rebuild confirms their results. The two charts agree in the total, in the net energy of each source to within a few per cent, and in the timing of the phase-out. Two settings had to be taken from the authors’ published runs rather than from the model file: the start of the ERoEI of photovoltaics, as above, and the split of the renewables between wind, photovoltaics and solar thermal power, which in their runs gives photovoltaics twice the output of wind by 2100. The model file also prescribes a time step of one year. With these, the rebuild invests about a tenth more energy than their runs, and the peak rates quoted in the paper, 7.3 and 11.6 TW per annum, i.e. the highest mean over ten consecutive years, come out at 7.1 and 11.2 TW per annum, about 3% less, for reasons I could not find in the model file. The rebuild also confirms the central finding of the paper: the later the phase-out starts, the steeper the build-out has to be.

Replacing Useful Energy

The Sower’s Way replaces fossil primary energy one for one with renewable energy. This is what has become known as the primary energy fallacy: most of the primary energy of fossil fuels is lost as heat in engines, boilers and power stations, whereas electric motors and heat pumps deliver the same services with less than half of it. The authors anticipated this. Since their methodology focuses on primary energy supply, they note that ‘an objection can be raised that lower levels of primary energy are realistically needed’, and address it by running the model for a wide range of demand per person, down to values lower than even the most optimistic estimate [2]. A lower demand per person, however, still replaces fossil fuels one for one; it does not show what the substitution itself changes, e.g. in the build-out and the metals it needs.

Therefore, I refine the model to make the substitution explicit: the demand is set in useful energy, and the renewables replace fossil fuels for the services they deliver. The demand, in TW, is the population times the useful power each person uses, i.e. the useful energy per year as a mean power. For the population, I use the record and the UN’s projection quoted above, as given by Our World in Data [8], Figure 4.

Per Capita Useful Energy

The world’s average primary power per person has grown from 1.4 kW in 1965 to 2.0 kW in 2000 and 2.3 kW in 2025, without traditional biomass [9]. This is an average over a very uneven world: in 2025, a person in the United States used 8.6 kW, in the United Kingdom 2.9, in China 3.6, in India 0.85 and in Africa 0.45 kW. How much of it ends up as a service? For the world, Ember finds about a third for 2023 [3], Brockway and colleagues 47% for 2020 [4], depending on how the heat from boilers and the energy used as feedstock are counted; the rest is lost, mostly as heat in power stations and engines. I use a round 40% in between, for the present and the past alike; for the UK in 2025, I found about 41% in Mineral Resource Usage for the Renewable Energy Transition. With 40%, the useful power per person is 0.93 kW for the world in 2025.

What could the world settle for? The UK has shown that a developed society can do with less: its primary power per person stayed at about 5 kW from 1965 to 2005, and then fell to 2.9 kW by 2025, partly through efficiency, partly because heavy industry moved abroad. I take today’s UK level, 1.15 kW of useful power per person, as the world’s average in 2100: 24% more than today. The idea is that perhaps in developed countries like the UK the energy consumption per capita remains about the same, with an increase of affluence compensated by efficiency gains. The developing countries should be allowed to ramp their per capita energy usage up to the levels of the developed countries. Conversely, countries used to squandering energy such as the US have to come down to those levels through efficiency gains and perhaps a change of lifestyle. The useful power per person moves from today’s world average to that level along the same smooth transition that the Sower’s model uses for its 2,000 W.

Population and power per person put together, the world’s useful power grows from 7.6 TW in 2025 to 9.5 TW in 2050 and 11.7 TW in 2100. From fossil fuels, these services would need 30.2 TW of primary power in 2100, two fifths more than the Sower’s demand of 2,000 W per person, i.e. 22 TW.²

Supplying Useful Energy

How much renewable electricity is needed to replace a unit of fossil primary energy? It takes three factors. First, fossil fuels and biomass deliver a share u of their primary energy as useful energy, here u = 0.4 as the 40% above. Second, a unit of electricity, from wind and solar as well as from hydro and nuclear power, delivers η units of useful energy. Third, to deliver the same useful energy, a unit of fossil primary energy has to be replaced by r = u/η units of renewable electricity. The model takes η = 1, i.e. electricity counts one for one as useful energy, so that r = u = 0.4, a little more than the third I assumed in Mineral Resource Usage for the Renewable Energy Transition. The model thus needs only the one figure, 40%, and one assumption. The renewables’ output N is now the electricity that replaces useful energy; the energy invested and the nameplate capacity follow from it as before. This applies from 2027 onwards; the renewables already in place by then stay as they are.

Is η = 1 the correct number? It depends on the use: an electric car turns about 83% of its electricity into motion, i.e. η = 0.83, whereas a heat pump delivers about three units of heat per unit of electricity, i.e. η = 3. For a whole economy, the uses have to be weighted by the energy each takes, which is not straightforward: some deliver more than one unit of useful energy per unit of electricity, others less. Ember estimates that for the world, an electrified energy system would need about as much electricity as the useful energy it delivers, i.e. η ≈ 1, since the gains of heat pumps make up for the losses elsewhere [3]; a rough estimate of my own for the UK in 2025 gives about the same with direct electric heating, and more with heat pumps.³ One for one is thus a fair middle, and on the safe side as heat pumps spread.

At the time of writing (end of 2026) recorded data go up to 2025. Up to the start of the phase-out, the Sower’s model follows the projection its authors made in 2013, in which wind, solar, solar thermal and geothermal power deliver 2.2 TW on average in 2025 from 13 TW of nameplate capacity; in reality, they delivered only 0.63 TW from about 3.7 TW, mostly solar [12] and [13]. The extended model therefore starts from the record: from 2013 to 2025, these sources deliver what they actually did, and the demand is lowered by what the projection delivered beyond that. Since less has been built than in the projection, photovoltaics have also learned less: in my extension, their ERoEI is about 19 today and 24 by 2050, against 22 and 25 in the Sower’s model.

Hydro and nuclear power follow the record, too: 0.83 TW in 2025 against 0.91 TW in the projection [12]. After 2025, hydro power grows as in the Sower’s model, within the half of its economic potential that the IEA finds still untapped [14], whereas nuclear power, whose output has hardly changed since 2006, stays at today’s level. Fossil fuels and biomass stay as in the Sower’s model. All runs from here on let the phase-out start in 2027, 2030 or 2035, with an ERoEI of 20 and a budget of 990 Gt. Since the original budget counts from 2013, and about 520 Gt of it are emitted by the end of 2026, about 470 Gt are left from 2027. So more than half of the budget has already been used up for lack of decisive action.

Results for Useful Energy

With useful energy as the demand, the world will need less energy by 2100 than in the Sower’s model, as shown in Figure 5. The power supplied falls from 20.0 TW in 2027 to 12.8 TW in 2050 and 14.5 TW in 2100, of which 11.3 TW are renewables and 3.2 TW other sources.

The transition looks much easier to achieve. The energy invested is at most 4.7% of the energy supplied, against 7%. The CO2 emissions do not change, since they are set by the budget: about 470 Gt from 2027 for each start.

From Useful Energy to Nameplate Capacity

Useful energy is what people need; what has to be built, and mined for, is nameplate capacity. The capacity factors are about 0.15 for photovoltaics, as the sunshine hours are a fraction of the 24-hour day and there is less in winter, and 0.25 for wind, as in the Sower’s model [5]. In 2100, the renewables have to deliver 11.3 TW on average, or 11.8 TW including the energy to replace themselves; at the capacity factors of the model, that takes 63 TW of nameplate capacity, i.e. 5.6 times the net output, Table 1. Photovoltaics alone account for nearly two thirds of it. Without the change to useful energy, the nameplate capacity would be about 100 TW.

That is not yet all. A capacity factor is an average; to get through calm and dark weeks (Dunkelflaute), a renewable system needs more: an overbuild beyond the capacity factor, whose surplus is curtailed in good weeks as in Scotland; storage to balance day and night; and larger grids, perhaps even a global one, to move electricity between regions [15]. The model includes storage, which grows with the square of the renewable share to 12 hours of the average renewable output at 100%, but neither overbuild nor grids; I look at both in the Caveat.

Finite Mining Capacity

The energy transition may encounter limits on the supply of the minerals needed and, by extension, of how fast mining can grow. With mining included, the model can be run in two ways. If the growth of mining is given, the build-out and the CO2 follow: when the metals fall short, fewer renewables are built than the plan requires, fossil fuels cover the difference, and the carbon budget is overrun. If this budget is given, the growth of mining it requires follows. After the mining model, one subsection looks at each case.

Metals and Mining

The metals that could hold the transition back are copper, lithium, nickel and cobalt, for the renewables themselves and for the batteries of storage and electric vehicles, assuming that the current technologies will be used throughout the energy transition; substitution is not considered here. The metals follow the nameplate capacity, not the useful energy: about 3 t of copper per MW of nameplate capacity, and the lithium, nickel and cobalt in the batteries for storage and for electric vehicles [16]. The batteries of electric vehicles grow with the renewable share to 90 TWh, e.g. 1.5 billion cars at 60 kWh each; like those for storage, they close half of the gap to what is wanted each year.

How fast mining can grow is best judged from how fast it has grown in the past. Our World in Data’s record of mine production [17] shows that from 1980 to 2000, before the transition, copper and nickel both grew by 2.9% per annum on average, Figure 6; since then, lithium and cobalt have grown much faster, mostly for batteries, i.e. the transition has already begun: over the last 20 years, copper has grown by 2.4% per annum on average, nickel by 4.5%, cobalt by 6.8% and lithium by 13.3%. The fastest growth sustained over 20 years since 1950 ranges from 5.1% per annum for copper to 13.9% for lithium.⁴ In the model, the mining capacity of each metal is carried on from its latest record, that of 2024, at its mean growth of the last 20 years to 2027. From 2027, its growth ramps up over ten years, as new mines open, from that mean to that fastest rate, and no further; the transition already under way thus carries on without a leap. How much faster mining could grow is left to the Sensitivity.

Figure 6. Mine production of copper, nickel, lithium and cobalt since 1950 (lithium since 2000), on a logarithmic scale, with the mean growth of the last 20 years and the 20 years of the fastest growth; drawn from data by Our World in Data [17].

The rest of the economy keeps wanting more metals: an economy of green growth is still built on growth, and with affluence come energy and resources. Today, electric vehicles, batteries, solar and wind take about 11% of the copper, 17% of the nickel, 62% of the lithium and 32% of the cobalt [18]; the rest of the economy, including the electricity networks, uses the remainder. From 2024, its use of each metal grows by 1% per annum, close to the IEA’s projection for the other uses of copper and nickel to 2050 (about 0.9%), and between the growth of the world’s useful energy (0.6%) and the pace of mining from 1980 to 2000 (2.9%); the Sensitivity looks at other rates. This sets the base that the mines have to deliver anyway; the build-out gets only what they deliver beyond it, i.e. nothing is taken from the rest of the economy.

In order to avoid a sudden leap in mining, the model carries on from today’s mining capacity: the round figures per MW and per kWh above are scaled for each metal until today’s renewables and batteries take exactly the transition’s share of today’s output; the scaling covers what the round figures leave out, e.g. other battery chemistries, nickel for other clean technologies, and losses and stocks along the supply chain. Likewise, the batteries made each year do not fall below today’s, carried on at the pace of lithium mining. At the end of their life, 85% of the metals in plants and batteries are recycled, once recycling has been built up over ten years from next to nothing; this is optimistic, since today about half or more of the copper, nickel and cobalt is recycled at the end of its life, but less than 1% of the lithium [19].

Mining Required by the Budget

If the budget is to be kept, the mines have to deliver what the plan requires, Figure 9. Since the rest of the economy keeps growing, the build-out comes on top of its growth. I express what the budget requires as a multiple of each metal’s mean growth of the last 20 years, the pace from which the mines start in the mining-limited case, Table 3.

For a start in 2027, copper has to grow by 6.0% per annum to 2034, 2.5 times as fast as over the last 20 years and faster than in its fastest 20 years; cobalt has to grow 3.3-fold by 2041, by 8.9% per annum, within its record, and lithium 4.7-fold, by 11.6% per annum, less than over the last 20 years; nickel, too, stays below its record. These are averages, however: in its first years, the plan asks for much more, since electric vehicles follow the renewable share, and the batteries made each year double from 2029 to 2030. For a start in 2030, copper, lithium and cobalt have to grow faster than in their fastest 20 years, copper 2.7 times as fast as over the last 20 years. For a start in 2035, copper has to grow 6.0 times as fast, 2.8 times its record, and the other metals 1.3 to 2.3 times; this comes mostly from a single year, since with so little budget left, fossil output has to fall by a third from 2035 to 2036.

A later start makes it worse, since the build-out has to be steeper. If instead the phase-out were kept while the mines limit the build-out, up to 16, 27 and 52% of the energy demand would go unmet around 2040, for starts in 2027, 2030 and 2035.

Sensitivity

How fast mining can grow decides the outcome, and it is uncertain. In the mining-limited case, the growth of each metal ramps up over ten years from its mean of the last 20 years to its record; Table 4 shows what happens if it ramps up to half, or up to twice, the record instead. Even with mines ramping up to twice their record, the budget is overrun for every start: the build-out needs its metals early, e.g. the copper it requires grows by 8% in each of its first two years, whereas the mines start from their recent pace. How long the ramp takes matters about as much: for a start in 2027, the CO2 from 2027 is 511 Gt without any ramp, and 561 Gt with one of 15 years, against 541.

How fast the rest of the economy’s use of metals grows matters less than how fast the mines grow, Table 6: from flat to 2% per annum, a rate that governments still aim at, the CO2 from 2027 rises by 22 to 67 Gt, the more the earlier the start; at 2.9% per annum, i.e. if the economy used metals as fast as in 1980 to 2000, it rises by another 21 to 97 Gt. If, on the other hand, the build-out could take its share of the metals from the rest of the economy, the CO2 for a start in 2027 would be 500 Gt, with the rest of the economy getting at worst 43% of what it needs. Counting electricity one for one is on the safe side: if it delivered η = 1.25 or 1.5 units of useful energy on average, e.g. with heat pumps for all heating, a unit of fossil primary energy would be replaced by r = 0.32 or 0.27 units of renewable electricity; the nameplate capacity in 2100 would be 52 or 44 TW instead of 63, and the CO2 from 2027 would be 522 or 511 Gt instead of 541.

Conclusion

My rebuild confirms the Sower’s Way: with the authors’ settings, it reproduces their chart, the energy for the build-out is there, and the later the phase-out starts, the steeper the build-out has to be.

With the demand set in useful energy and the model starting from the world as it is, the world gets more useful energy per person by 2100 than today, yet the renewables need only about two thirds of the nameplate capacity of the Sower’s Way, since they replace useful energy rather than primary energy. Still, the mines narrow the way: if they set the pace, the CO2 emitted from 2027 overruns the plan by about a seventh for a start in 2027, and by almost two thirds for a start in 2035. Even mines ramping up to twice their record cannot keep the budget, since the build-out needs its metals in the first years.

If the budget is to be kept along the Sower’s Way, it depends on when we start. Starting in 2027, copper has to grow 2.5 times as fast as over the last 20 years, faster than in its fastest 20 years, while the other metals stay within theirs; starting in 2030, copper, lithium and cobalt have to grow faster than ever; and starting in 2035, copper would have to grow six times as fast as over the last 20 years, 2.8 times its record.

In short, an early start asks the least of the mines, and waiting for the mines to catch up does not help. Whoever insists on the budget, but waits, asks the mines for more than they have ever delivered.

Caveat

I coupled the mining limit to The Sower’s Way in my own way: the energy invested scales with what is actually built, the fuel mix stays that of the plan, and the metals per TW are rough. From 2013 to 2025, only the total output of the renewables is anchored to the record; their mix stays that of the Sower’s model, with more wind and solar thermal power than in reality. The share of 40% lies between two global estimates and is applied to the past alike, and the UK’s energy per person leaves out the energy embodied in what it imports. Like the Sower’s model, the budget counts the CO2 from fossil fuels only, whereas the IPCC’s budgets cover all CO2, including that from land use. The warming figure ignores slow feedbacks of the climate, e.g. thawing permafrost releasing methane.

Overbuild and grids make it worse, since copper sets the pace. With 1.25 times the nameplate capacity and 1 t of copper per MW for grids, or with 1.5 times and 2 t, the CO2 from 2027 for a start in 2027 rises from 541 to 612 or 685 Gt. The ERoEI of photovoltaics, on the other hand, hardly matters: starting it at a quarter, half or one and a half times wind’s value, the share of the energy invested in the build-out is at most 6.7%, 4.7% or 3.4%, but the CO2 from 2027 changes by 3 Gt at most, since the mines, not the energy, set the pace.
