Viewing a Heat Pump Installation through the Levelised Cost of Heating

Like transport or food, a warm home has a daily price; taken over the life of the installation, it serves as a metric for comparing heat pumps with gas boilers.

The median reported cost of installing an air source heat pump under the government’s Boiler Upgrade Scheme (BUS) [1], before the grant is deducted, was about £13,000 in 2025/26 [2], and quotes of around £15,000, which is what I was quoted, are common; taking the £7,500 BUS grant into account, the net cost to the homeowner is still £5,500 to £7,500. In my social circle, a heat pump remains a purchase largely confined to those with spare capital; perhaps that is beginning to change. Efforts are under way to reduce the cost further: some installers, combining the BUS grant with their own cashback schemes, are bringing the net cost down to around £3,500, putting it on a par with a gas boiler installation (£2,000–£4,000; the government estimates the average at about £3,500 including VAT [3]).

The question whether to install a heat pump is, however, only relevant when a heating system actually needs replacing; there is little justification for scrapping a well-functioning gas boiler on principle, given the energy embodied in the existing system and in the new one that would replace it, even though the old boiler continues to emit CO2. Nevertheless, around 1.6 million gas boilers are installed in the UK each year, so the need to choose arises constantly. Most people still choose a gas boiler, largely because of the perceived cost and complexity of a heat pump installation. But perhaps I have been approaching the issue from the wrong angle, seeing the installation only as a cost.

The Daily Cost of Heating

Domestic heating, like transport or food, has a daily price. In 2025 our home used 11,508 kWh of gas, i.e. about 10,900 kWh of useful heat for space heating and hot water from a modern condensing boiler running at 95% efficiency. For prices I use the central case set out in the postscript to Convincing Numbers to Install a Heat Pump for Domestic Heating, the mean of the Ofgem price caps from Q1 2024 to Q1 2026: 25.74 p/kWh for electricity, 6.34 p/kWh for gas and a gas standing charge of 31.93 p/day. At these prices our gas heating costs roughly £2.32 per day. This feels like the cost of heating, because the boiler itself was paid for long ago and barely registers. The more useful question at replacement time is therefore not ‘How much does a heat pump cost?’ but ‘What is the total daily cost of keeping the house warm and producing hot water?’, with capital and financing included. I would like to call this the levelised cost of heating, either as a total cost or in pounds per day, over the expected life of the installation.

Capital Cost over 25 Years

A gas boiler has a life expectancy of 10 to 15 years; a modern heat pump can run comfortably for 20 to 25 years. Over a 25-year horizon, a gas boiler must therefore be bought twice. At £2,000–£4,000 per unit, financed at 5% APR over 12.5 years and with the second purchase subject to 2% annual inflation, the combined capital cost over 25 years works out to roughly £6,100–£12,300. A single heat pump at £3,500–£7,500 (after the BUS grant), financed over 25 years at 5% APR, amounts to roughly £6,100–£13,200; at the lower end, the capital cost of the heat pump is already on a par with that of two gas boilers. With 0% finance, available through government-backed schemes, e.g. Heat Scheme [4], the capital cost of the heat pump is simply the purchase price, £3,500–£7,500.

Energy and Running Costs

Delivering 10,900 kWh of useful heat requires about 2,700 kWh of electricity from a heat pump with a COP of 4, at roughly £700 per year, or 11,500 kWh of gas at £730 per year. The difference in unit cost is small because the spark gap of 4.06 sits close to the ratio at which the two break even on energy alone, 4/0.95 ≈ 4.2. What tilts the balance is the gas standing charge of £117 per year, which is saved once the gas connection is removed.

Adding £100 per year of maintenance for either system, the total cost over 25 years works out to roughly £29,800–£35,900 for two gas boilers and £26,200–£33,200 for one heat pump at 5% APR, equivalent to £3.27–£3.94 per day for gas against £2.87–£3.64 per day for the heat pump, as shown in Figure 1. With 0% finance, the range for the heat pump drops to £2.58–£3.02 per day, up to £1.35 per day less than the gas equivalent.

Flexible Tariffs

The heat pump is cheaper than gas in most scenarios at standard finance rates, and cheaper across the full range at 0% finance. It does better still on a time-of-use tariff such as Octopus Agile or Cosy: with optimising software such as Havenwise [5], which shifts heating to periods of lower wholesale prices and away from the evening peak, heat pump owners have achieved average rates of around 15 p/kWh over 2024 and 2025. At that rate, the annual electricity cost for heating falls from about £700 to roughly £410, and the levelised cost of heating drops to £2.07–£2.84 per day with standard finance and £1.78–£2.22 per day with 0% finance, around 30% to 45% less than heating with gas.
