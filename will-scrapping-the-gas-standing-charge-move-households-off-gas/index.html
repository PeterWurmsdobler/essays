Will Scrapping the Gas Standing Charge Move Households Off Gas?

Scrapping the gas standing charge lowers the gas bill of most households, which weakens their case for leaving gas; for most, the change is also small against the cost of an alternative.

On 28 October 2026 the Chancellor announced that the gas standing charge is to be scrapped: Ofgem is asked to remove it in 2027 or as soon as possible after, and the fixed costs behind the standing charge are to be paid through the unit rate instead for the total revenue to remain invariant to that change [1]. The government expects 60% of households to save around £45 a year, and says that the household at the median gas consumption will benefit; some households with high consumption will need targeted mitigation [2].

To me the question is: will scrapping the standing charge make gas more or less attractive, and will it make households move to something else, a heat pump say? Well, my hypothesis is that a household is less likely to invest in an alternative if it has a smaller gas bill after the standing charge is scrapped because the alternative saves comparatively less. The data cannot show how households behave, only who gets a smaller bill and by how much, so that is what I tried to work out, from the distribution of gas consumption of English and Welsh households.

The Gas Bill

The reform moves money between households; how much depends on the distribution of gas consumption over households: those who use less than the mean benefit, those who use more lose as detailed in the following sections.

The Arithmetic

The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero (DESNZ) publishes a sample of four million properties in England and Wales, with their annual gas consumption and a few features of the property [3]. The latest year is 2024, published in June 2026. It gives 19.9 million households with gas, using 223.7 TWh in total, i.e. 11,233 kWh per household on average¹. With the Ofgem price cap of October to December 2026 [4] (direct debit, excluding VAT), the gas standing charge brings in £2.05 billion a year and the unit rate £17.00 billion, with a total revenue of £19.05 billion. Scrapping the standing charge at constant revenue requires dividing £2.05 billion by 223.7 TWh: the unit rate must rise by 0.96 p/kWh including VAT, from 7.98 p/kWh to 8.94 p/kWh, i.e. by 12%. The standing charge is 29.63 p/day, £108 a year, the figure in the announcement.

The Distribution

Each household in the DESNZ data has its gas consumption over the year, in kilowatt hours, rounded to 100 kWh. Figure 1 counts the households in bins 100 kWh wide, scaled up to all 19.9 million. The mean is the total consumption divided by the number of households, 11,233 kWh. The median is the consumption that half of the households stay below, which works out to be 10,000 kWh.

Figure 1. Annual gas consumption per household in England and Wales, 2024, in bins of 100 kWh, with the median and the mean marked.

The distribution is far from Gaussian: it rises steeply, peaks between 7,000 kWh and 10,000 kWh and has a long tail to the right. The median therefore lies below the mean, and 58.5% of households use less than the mean.

Winners and Losers

The increase is the same for every household: 0.96 p for each kilowatt hour, i.e. £9.60 for every 1,000 kWh, but each household no longer pays the standing charge of £108. The two cancel at the mean consumption, 11,233 kWh, since 11.2 × £9.60 is about £108. A household that uses less than the mean pays less than before, one that uses more pays more: 58.5% of households pay less and 41.5% pay more. Figure 2 multiplies two factors: the number of households at each level of consumption (top) and the change in the bill of one household (middle). The product, at the bottom, is the change in all bills together.

Figure 2. Top: households by annual gas consumption, as in Figure 1. Middle: change in the annual gas bill of one household with that consumption when the standing charge is scrapped; the dots mark the lower quartile, the median, the upper quartile and the 95th percentile of households. The grey band, in all three panels, is the consumption from about 6,000 kWh to 16,400 kWh at which the bill changes by less than £50; it holds 61% of households. Bottom: the two multiplied, the change in all bills together for each 100 kWh bin; the green and the orange areas are equal.

The median household, with 10,000 kWh and a gas bill of £906 (all prices include VAT), pays £12 less, 1.3%. At the lower quartile (6,600 kWh) the bill falls by £45, 7%; at the upper quartile (14,300 kWh) it rises by £30, 2.4%. For 61% of households the change is smaller than £50, and for 93% smaller than £100. Only a few pay much more: the annual bill of the 7% at the top, 1.4 million households, rises by more than £100, which means a consumption above about 21,600 kWh, and that of 1.5% (0.3 million) by more than £200.

In total, £482 million a year moves from those who pay more to those who pay less, which is 2.4% of the £20 billion that households pay for gas, VAT included. The green and the orange areas at the bottom of the figure are equal for that reason: many households save a little, fewer households pay a lot more. Those who pay less save £41 a year on average; the government quotes £45 for 60% of households, from a household survey, so the two sources broadly agree.

Which Homes Gain

Consumption follows the size and type of the home, so the change does too. Figure 3 shows the average change for two groupings.

Figure 3. Mean change in the annual gas bill when the standing charge is scrapped, by property type and by floor area; negative means a saving.

Flats gain £37 a year on average, and 84% of them pay less; detached houses pay £40 more, and only 34% pay less. Homes of 50 m2 or less gain £44, those above 200 m2 pay £124 more. The energy rating tells the same story. Homes rated A or B save £30 on average, those rated E, F or G pay £23 to £33 more. The council tax band, which follows the value of the home, shows it too: bands A and B save £20 on average, bands F, G and H pay £58, £111 and £164 more.

Moving Off Gas

In the effort against climate change, heating has to move from gas to electricity, and the reform is read as a push in that direction: Octopus Energy’s chief executive welcomed it as further improving the ratio of electricity to gas prices [1]. However, I expect the opposite for most households: the reform makes their gas bill smaller and so encourages them to stay with gas.

A household can respond in two ways. It can use less gas, by insulating, turning the thermostat down or fitting a more efficient boiler. Or it can leave gas altogether: a heat pump instead of the boiler, an induction hob instead of the gas hob, and the gas connection removed. Only moving off gas ends the CO2 emissions.

Replacing the Boiler

The choice arises mostly when a heating system has to be replaced, as around 1.6 million gas boilers are installed every year in the UK. Viewing a Heat Pump Installation through the Levelised Cost of Heating finds that heating costs £3.27 to £3.94 a day over 25 years with gas and £2.87 to £3.64 with a heat pump, capital included and finance at 5%, for a house with about the mean consumption at 2025 prices; the ranges overlap. The reform changes only the gas range, for most households by pence a day. That creates no stronger incentive for a heat pump; for most households the gas range moves down.

Where the change points towards the heat pump, it reaches the households that need it least. Bills rise most for large homes in the higher council tax bands, and many of those households will hardly notice. Among them are, I suspect, people who care about the environment and, like many I know in Cambridge, can afford a heat pump anyway without counting the capital cost. The households that would have to be persuaded, I suspect with smaller bills and little spare capital, get a nudge the other way.

Cooking with Gas

The simplest step off gas is the hob: an induction hob needs no heat pump and no plumbing. For a household that cooks with gas alone, the reform removes much of the reason to take that step. Take one with 1,000 kWh: it pays £108 of standing charge for £80 of gas, £188 in total, and with the standing charge gone £89, so leaving saves half as much. The data leave out properties using less than 1,000 kWh a year, but about 0.5 million have a gas meter and used less than that in 2024, some of them probably for the hob alone. In my opinion it would be desirable to get those off gas as well, for the sake of emissions reduction and allowing the decommissioning of the gas network to proceed more smoothly.

Conclusion

The numbers are consistent with the hypothesis: the reform lowers the gas bill of most households and so weakens their case for leaving gas. That being said, they come from a static calculation, not from observed behaviour, and the hypothesis stays a hypothesis. What the reform does reliably is to move money from large to small homes. If the aim is to move households off gas, the cost of the alternative, the effort of installing it and the price of the electricity it runs on matter much more, as I tried to show in Impediments to UK Heat Pump Adoption and Possible Solutions.
